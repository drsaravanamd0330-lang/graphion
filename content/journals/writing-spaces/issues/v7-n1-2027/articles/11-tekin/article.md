# Introduction

If you are currently dragging your feet on a writing assignment, you are absolutely not alone in your lack of motivation to write. In fact, here comes a confession: that I am a published scholar who currently teaches a writing course to undergraduate students shouldn’t fool anyone. This very chapter is a result of many days of procrastination fueled by my own lack of motivation. That being said, I must also acknowledge that the ubiquitous use of generative artificial intelligence (AI) systems such as ChatGPT has disproportionately impacted students, and particularly, their motivation to genuinely engage with writing tasks. In his recently published sensational article, James D. Walsh paints a dismal picture of academia where most students report little motivation to write and largely rely on AI to complete what they perceive to be meaningless writing tasks without much critical thinking (i.e., they lack the motivation to write). Students are not happy with their over-reliance on AI, either (Shirky). So, instead of thinking students are lazy, have little or no interest in learning (and writing), and are more inclined to commit academic dishonesty than before, I believe that we simply need to find strategies that will turn writing from a chore into a purposeful core activity to increase students’ motivation to write. One such strategy is to assume the role of an academic citizen who has a responsibility to share your hard work and knowledge with the community or communities you belong to or have a particular interest in. I argue that doing so will help you reframe seemingly mundane writing tasks into authentic and meaningful ones and harness the motivation you need to write in academia and beyond.

Being an English-as-a-second-language-speaking, first-generation, international university student from an academic culture drastically different from the North American one, I had the hardest time maintaining my motivation to write my doctoral dissertation that explored attitudes, perceived threat, and contact between international students and host communities. The dissertation was virtually the only thing that stood between me and the Ph.D. degree I was pursuing at the time. It was “just another assignment” that I needed to complete like all the previous assignments---just an extremely large-scale one! No matter how many times I repeated, “This is just another assignment”, my mind preferred doing anything but writing the dissertation. I was second-guessing every word I used (should I say international students “face” challenges or “encounter” challenges?), let alone producing a sentence; questioning my place in the North American academic context (no one in my family had done it before, so what was so special about me?); doubting my line of reasoning (is this a valid argument or will the first reviewer shut it down?); and so forth. It was only after I stopped seeing the dissertation less as “an assignment to complete” and more as a means to potentially bring about a positive social change for international students that I could make headway. Once my perception of the task at hand shifted and the dense fog in my head lifted, the true “why” of my dissertation became crystal clear. I was able to dodge the flurry of doubts coming my way and see how my findings could raise important stakeholders’ awareness of the pressing issues facing international students and host communities while contributing to the efforts to improve the relations between these two groups. In other words, it wasn’t about me anymore; it was about pursuing a meaningful cause. This is not to say that my dissertation solved international students’ problems across the world and to proclaim myself a hero. My dissertation research is only a droplet in the ocean, and there is still so much work to be done. However, it has since become clear to me that finding a cause that is dear and near to one’s heart can help spark the motivation needed to complete writing tasks, however daunting they may seem. I believe that one way to foster this type of mindset toward writing assignments is to assume the role of an academic citizen, which allows us to see the bigger picture and focus on writing for positive social change for our communities instead of simply writing for a grade.

# Thinking Like an Academic Citizen and Writing for Social Change

I get it. College *is* a big milestone in your life marked with a drastic shift into adulthood, leaving you juggling new responsibilities while tackling a constant stream of academic tasks. Therefore, it is easy to lose sight of the fact that the work you do in your classes has meaning beyond a grade. However, you don’t need to wait until graduation to make a difference in the communities of which you are a part or about which you care, and there is no better time than today to put your academic skills (e.g., critical thinking, writing, and research) into use to bring about positive change for those communities.

One easy way to do so, as suggested earlier, is to assume the role of an “academic citizen.” According to Ashley Holmes, who wrote a *Writing* *Spaces* essay about writing for social change, an academic citizen is “someone who takes the writing and research [they] do in college and puts it to practical and civic use in [their] communities in the hopes of contributing toward positive social change” (199). As this definition suggests, academic citizenship concerns civic engagement and is open to all who are willing to actively work for the benefit of their communities regardless of one’s place of birth or first language. In her chapter, Holmes urges us to think beyond our immediate school environment to positively contribute to our communities with our research and writing. As a starting point, she recommends choosing a public space (e.g., libraries, parks) to observe life as it unfolds and freewrite in order to find a timely topic or an issue that has relevance either to you as a person or to the community you’re observing. As academic citizens, students can tap into their academic skills to bring about a positive social change, which is a noble cause in and of itself; however, the benefits of being an academic citizen don’t end there. By taking on the role of an academic citizen, you can re-envision your current writing assignments to make them more interesting, meaningful, and valuable, all of which play a critical role in cultivating writing motivation (Wigfield and Eccles).

# Writing Motivation and Being an Academic Citizen

Let’s ponder for a second: Have you ever needed to motivate yourself to scroll through your social media feed (e.g., Instagram, TikTok)? The answer is probably “no” because engaging with social media is not inherently challenging and is rewarding, lighting up areas in your brain related to instant joy and gratification (Meshi et al.). Just by swiping your finger on the screen, you are presented with an endless stream of media that stimulates your brain in the moment. Moreover, such engagement with social media is also rewarding since it provides you with up-to-date content to discuss with your peers, acting like a social glue. Writing, however, *is* challenging and *is not* instantly rewarding. That is, gratification associated with the act of writing tends to be delayed. In other words, most, if not all, of us like “having written something” but not “currently writing” something. Therefore, you might need to proactively work on your writing motivation.

To achieve this, you need to understand what fuels your motivation. One longstanding theory on motivation, expectancy-value theory, suggests three main factors: feelings of self-efficacy (i.e., how competent you perceive yourself to perform a given task), interest in the task, and the value attached to the task (Wigfield and Eccles). Regarding self-efficacy, you can consider editor and book coach Ariel Curry’s example, where she invites us to compare how confident we may feel about doing laundry versus performing at the Olympics. She suggests that we are likely to have higher self-efficacy (i.e., feel more competent) about doing the laundry, which is an oft-repeated and (mostly!) successfully completed house chore. If doing laundry is still unfamiliar territory for you, think, instead, of ordering your favourite drink at your local coffee shop. You have ordered it competently so many times that you don’t even think about it for a second and question your ability to do so. From the writing standpoint, how confident you are with your writing skills largely depends on your past (successful) experiences with writing. If you have had many positive writing experiences, you will probably have a high sense of competence and will be more motivated to write (Bruning and Kauffman). The other two factors, your interest in and the value you attach to a writing task, on the other hand, will vary depending on the writing assignment. Now, take a second to consider two writing assignments (past, present, or future) and write down how motivated you feel toward them on a scale of 0--10, where 0 = cannot be bothered, and 10 = cannot wait to start. You may realize that even though your self-efficacy beliefs toward writing remain constant, your level of motivation is different since one assignment may align better with your values or interests over the other. What this suggests is that even though you have little control over your past experiences with writing, which tend to impact your current level of confidence and motivation, you have relatively more control over your interest in and how much value you attach to a writing task by thinking of various (authentic) ways to make the writing task more meaningful. If you can make the task more meaningful, then you can stimulate more interest and increase its personal value, thereby promoting your motivation to write (Boscolo, Bruning and Horn, Troia et al.).

The more you perceive the task at hand as important and as part of your identity (i.e., who you are) and believe that it has utility value (i.e., there are benefits to reap for you and your community), the more motivated you will be to complete it (Boscolo). Furthermore, similar to what I described earlier based on my personal experiences, if you come from a different linguistic and cultural background and are relatively new to the North American academic context, you may question your place in it and lack a sense of belonging. Thinking of multiple ways to increase an assignment’s utility value for your new communities as an academic citizen can, in fact, foster a sense of belonging in the new community as your academic work may drive positive social change in it. The more you do this, the likelier you are to feel “accepted, respected, and valued by” the new community, all of which drive a sense of belonging (Strayhorn 28). And remember! The outcome of your work doesn’t need to be earth-shattering. That is, I don’t mean to say that the term paper for your environmental biology course will help us find *the* solution to the impact of climate change on biodiversity.

Yet, you can indeed aim to communicate some of the relevant findings of your research from that paper for your environmental biology course with your social network in the form of a snappy meme, or with your town’s municipality by creating a clear infographic or presenting a cogent argument in an email, or writing an editorial for the newsletter of your birdwatching club outside of school. By doing this, you are not only fostering positive change regarding an issue that impacts communities beyond academia (more in line with Holmes’ point), but you are also addressing the “Why” in the question “Why am I writing this?” that I posed earlier to enhance your writing motivation. By stepping into the role of an academic citizen, you make a complex issue more digestible for those around you who may have no background in biology, to continue the previous example, potentially convincing relevant stakeholders to take urgent action while also tapping into a well of motivation to write that you may not have previously recognized. With that said, always remember to get solid feedback on your writing before going public to ensure the accuracy of information and study the needs and expectations of your target audience to better cater to their needs. Since I have shown you the potential benefits of taking the role of an academic citizen for you and your communities, let’s now consider how you can apply this to your writing assignments.

# Re-Envisioning Writing Assignments Like an Academic Citizen

Now, it is time for you to consider a course assignment that involves research and extensive writing. Put on your academic citizen glasses to think about how the academic skills you apply and the knowledge you will acquire by the end of the assignment can benefit the communities to which you belong and those in which you have a particular interest. Social and language-related issues typically affect all of us, regardless of our academic discipline. For instance, suppose there is ongoing prejudice against a group of people who speak a specific variety of a language. In that case, it will affect the community you are part of and are interested in, whether you are studying sociology, statistics, or any other discipline. Therefore, in what follows, I will describe a writing assignment that students in my first-year writing course complete at the beginning of the term that aims to raise their critical language awareness by reflecting on the link between language and society. I will then discuss how the assignment can be interpreted from an academic citizen standpoint to enhance writing motivation.

The Writing Story assignment comes from a first-year university-level writing course that welcomes students from a wide range of linguistic and cultural backgrounds interested in diverse fields of study, from anthropology to computer sciences. Therefore, the assignment, the first among three others, asks students to write about their past literacy experiences and re-interpret these experiences through the lens of a threshold concept[^2] that suggests “writing is impacted by identities and prior experiences” (Wardle and Downs, Chapter 5) and sets out to provide a space for self-reflection for students so that they understand “where they come from” and decide “where to go” in their writing journey. Put differently, by reflecting on their past writing experiences, students have a chance to make sense of their current attitudes toward writing. For instance, when discussing this assignment, some international students report how their education system exposed them only to “standard” American or British English at school, ignoring other varieties of English (e.g., African American Vernacular English). With this assignment, they are invited to think critically about how this approach may have shaped their attitudes toward other varieties of English and put a constraint on their relationship with English.

To encourage students to approach this assignment as academic citizens, I ask what they would like to achieve with their essay, and I usually get a couple of rounds of responses such as “I want to get a good grade, obviously!” or “I just want to finish it by the deadline.” Consequently, I urge them to think beyond their current context to remind them that their target audience is not me, their professor, and that they should consider who might benefit from their reflective essay. At this point, for instance, students realize that they can write to inform their future peers that writing in college is drastically different than writing in high school, to empower people from the same or similar linguistic or cultural backgrounds to tap into all of their linguistic repertoires to reclaim their rhetorical agency instead of feeling confined by “standard” language rules, to inspire their current peers to feel more confident in their writing skills by sharing their stories of overcoming challenges. The immediate spark in students’ eyes following such a discussion is a true testament to how imagining their work reaching beyond the classroom context can fuel their motivation to write. I hope that, as of today, you will start seeing writing assignments not as items to check off your to-do list by a deadline or a simple means to get a grade for a course but more as a tool to share your knowledge and vision with others, whether or not your professor prompts you to do so.

The critical reader in you may now think that this model assignment easily lends itself to such re-envisioning, and you are correct. However, with a touch of creativity, you can also reframe STEM assignments (i.e., writing tasks for your Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics courses). For example, consider a university student tasked with writing a technical report proposing a sustainable engineering solution for their environmental sciences and engineering course. Among other potential options, such as renewable energy solutions for urban settings or an improved transportation system aimed at reducing carbon emissions, this student focuses on low-cost water filtration systems due to the significant issue of access to clean water facing Indigenous communities in rural Ontario, Canada. In doing so, this student contextualizes their assignment, linking it to a community that is integral to their identity while also creating utility value; their work may benefit their community by addressing a pressing real-world issue. Consequently, feeling more motivated, they conduct extensive research and report on several viable options that have proven effective in other contexts. Mindful of their community’s affordances and constraints, they carefully evaluate the pros and cons of each option and highlight one as the most feasible in their argument. By strengthening their paper with feedback from peers and their professor, this student decides to share their findings in a succinct report with non-governmental organizations or community leaders back home, thereby contributing to this vital social cause.

All in all, this student viewed the writing task at hand through the lens of an academic citizen and turned an otherwise mundane research paper assignment into a meaningful one. As a result, they developed skills that will serve them beyond their school years. To elaborate, they critically evaluated and synthesized information from multiple sources to make informed statements, revised their work based on feedback, studied the needs of their target audience (i.e., their current level of knowledge on the issue, the best way to convey the information) and how to best persuade them (i.e., in this case by providing facts and statistics as opposed to personal anecdotes) as well as the restrictions and allowances of their new genre (e.g., reports tend to be factual and concise and include charts and graphs).

# Conclusion

Writing is a complex activity because it requires creativity, clear judgment, and reasoning, among other cognitive skills, and it can also be emotionally taxing, particularly if one feels unmotivated to write. You may happily write exquisite poems page after page in your free time while writing a short essay for school may seem like a tall order. Such contextuality of writing motivation suggests that we all struggle with motivation to write at some point. This is precisely why it is crucial to develop strategies to motivate yourself as a student writer because you will likely be faced with a writing assignment that won’t immediately resonate with you or align with your previous writing experiences. For those moments, one strategy that you can adopt is to assume the role of a responsible academic citizen with the civic duty of creating positive social change for communities beyond academia with your writing. This shift in focus from seeing writing assignments as tasks to complete to a means to contribute to the advancement and well-being of communities will make writing more interesting and valuable as well as increase your motivation to write. Finally, based on personal and anecdotal experiences, I also believe that some student writers (e.g., first-generation university students, second-language English speakers, and international students from academic cultures different from the North American one) may find it harder to stay motivated compared to peers who speak English as a first language or are more familiar with the North American academic context. Thus, embracing the identity of an academic citizen could be of particular use for this group of students since this new identity can strengthen their positioning in academia and help foster a sense of belonging and make the act of writing more meaningful overall.

# Teacher Resources

This essay promotes the concept of being an “academic citizen” among student writers with the idea that when students embrace the role of an academic citizen, they can envision multiple ways in which their academic work may bring about positive social change for the communities beyond academia which they are a part of or interested in.[^tr-1] Thus, they are likely not only to increase their motivation to write but also foster a sense of belonging, which is particularly important for the success of student writers from multilingual and multicultural backgrounds who are relatively new to the North American academic context. Due to its focus on generating motivation to write, the chapter is suited to be assigned at the beginning of an academic term to set the tone of the course so that students perceive each future writing assignment through the lens of academic citizen and consider the larger impact of their writing instead of seeing writing assignments as tasks to complete for a grade. It is also possible to assign this chapter toward the end of the term as a “Now what?” piece to encourage students to continue writing beyond their writing course.

Since the chapter highlights the social aspect of writing as a tool to “get things done” in line with the goals of a community, it is advisable to assign Wardle and Downs’ chapter on *Threshold Concepts of Writing* prior to this essay, with particular attention to the section where authors focus on the role of writing in communities (pp. 16--18) for some background information.

Below, you will find some discussion questions in line with the theme of the essay as well as some activities to encourage students to think like academic citizens. I would recommend responding to the questions below in pairs or in small groups to make them more interactive.

# Discussion Questions

1. The author opens the chapter by suggesting that lacking the motivation to write is not reserved for students only and that no one is immune to feeling unmotivated. He also confesses that, more often than not, he finds it challenging to generate the motivation to write despite teaching a writing course and writing being a significant part of his job. How does this make you feel as a student writer? What can you say about your own motivation to write?

2. How do you usually perceive writing assignments in the course you take---do you see them as tasks to complete for a grade or do you try to see their value beyond the course? How does it impact your motivation to attend to your writing assignments?

3. In the essay, the author brings up the potential influence of his cultural, linguistic, and family background on his writing motivation. Does this resonate with you? Do you think that one’s background or past experiences with writing may impact their motivation to write? How so? Can you think of some personal examples that would exemplify this? Does this align with any text that you may have read before?

4. Go back to the section where the author briefly introduces the expectancy-value theory of motivation and revisit the dimensions of self-efficacy, interest, and value. In the essay, the author applies the theory to writing motivation. To make it more relevant to you, can you apply it to one of your hobbies such as swimming, knitting, or singing? Do you see a positive association between the three dimensions of the theory (i.e., self-efficacy, interest, and value) and your motivation to swim, knit, or sing?

# Activities

## Unsilence Yourself: A Freewrite

The first activity aims to help students get creative in terms of re-imagining their writing assignments from the perspective of an academic citizen, and it is designed as individual work. Louise Dunlap, the author of *Undoing the Silence: Six Tools for Social Change Writing* suggests that people, especially ones who do not readily align with the mainstream (i.e., ideologies of the authority figures), are systematically being silenced, and therefore, it is usually challenging to let go of self-judgement to freely express one’s truth and unveil what issues are near to one’s heart. To tackle this, she recommends freewriting as a tool for empowerment.

For this activity, ask students to get ready to write *non-stop* in the next five or ten minutes freely without any control or judgment regarding grammar, vocabulary, or the content of their text. If they feel stuck and believe there is nothing more to write, they can simply write these thoughts down, repeating them as needed, until they are back on track. They can handwrite or type and use any language they feel most comfortable with and perhaps use more than one language or language variety simultaneously. To make sure that they will write as freely as possible, assure them that the text they will have produced by the end of the activity is for their eyes only. This will likely help students tap into their intuition and creativity when linking course assignments with real world issues. Once students are ready, ask them to reflect on the issues that currently challenge them or the communities they are a part of or interested in and start the timer. Advise them to save and return to this document as needed throughout the term to update it and/or feel inspired by it when they need to put on their metaphorical academic citizen hat.

## It Takes a Village: Group Work

The second activity is designed for group work. After working on the essay, choose a week where you can dedicate some time in class for students to bring in a writing assignment from any course that they are currently taking. If applicable and possible, it would be helpful to group together students pursuing similar fields of study or similar interests or belonging to similar communities. In groups, students can take turns and share which communities they have in mind and their current writing assignment brainstorm with other group members way in which they can link their assignment with a social cause. If students did the first activity, they can refer to their freewrite document as a guide. This group activity not only helps students connect and highlight the social aspect of writing but also emphasizes the transfer of writing skills across disciplines. At the end of the activity, there can be a whole class discussion where groups may briefly share their writing assignments and how they now perceive their writing assignment as an academic citizen.
