# Introduction

My morning commute is typically about fifty minutes, which means I spend nearly two hours in my car on any given day (or longer, depending on the traffic on I-85). That’s without adding time spent dropping my kids off at school and running errands. To fill those hours and keep my mind engaged, or if I just need some reliable entertainment, I’ll usually connect my phone to the car stereo and fire up one of my favorite podcasts. If I’m in the mood for sports talk, I’ll opt for *Shutdown Fullcast* or *Split Zone Duo*. If I need to satisfy my movie nerd cravings, I’ll see which movie the hosts of *Unspooled* are dissecting this week or catch up on the latest Hollywood gossip with *The Big Picture*. Or maybe there’s an episode of *Philosophize This!* that can help me learn more about a key thinker who keeps surfacing in my research. If whichever podcast I choose really captures my attention, I’ll use my earbuds to keep listening while grocery shopping or doing my cardio at the gym.

This is the beauty of podcasts: they can be enjoyed anywhere and at any time. This fact may be why audiences in the US, which ranks among the world’s leading podcast producers and consumers (Maher), listen to an average of seven episodes per week (Hendry). Depending on your individual interests---art, politics, sports, movies, the outdoors, literature, comedy---there is a podcast series waiting for you. This media format has become increasingly popular since its debut in the early 2000s. In 2021, nearly fifty million podcast episodes were available to listeners (Winn), and that figure is sure to increase.

However, while podcasts have become commonplace, we rarely think about how they are made. It may seem like *Hardcore History* is successful because Dan Carlin is a naturally gifted storyteller, and there is some truth to this, but I assure you that he works hard at his craft. If you are reading this, it means you, too, are about to enter the world of podcasting, likely by way of a course assignment. This is exciting! It means you are being introduced to one of the most popular and dynamic media forms out there, one that has proven its staying power and has even helped launch careers. This is a great opportunity, though you may also be nervous and understandably so. Debate persists about the value of podcasts in composition courses (Woods and Wood; Lemerond and Rourks), which means there is little consensus as to how students should be taught to create podcasts of their own. This essay aims to help fill that void in at least one way.

In this essay, I offer a list of considerations and strategies to help guide you through the scriptwriting portion of your podcast assignment. Before doing that, I should make clear upfront what I am *not* offering. This essay is not a step-by-step blueprint for how to write a perfect script. That would be an impossible task. What you produce will ultimately be determined by several factors, including your subject matter, the assignment parameters as designed by your instructor, and your own creative choices. Peter Gutiérrez, a former professional scriptwriter and English instructor, puts it best when he states that audio scripts “probably have the greatest flexibility in terms of formatting and the degree to which writers can experiment and come up with an approach that works for them” (32). My goal is to supply you with practical knowledge that can be used for *any* podcasting you do, be it class-related or a personal project. Put simply, the six tips in this essay are meant to help you think through the choices that will shape your script. This is the same advice I give when teaching podcasting in my own composition courses. Hopefully, it will prove as useful for you as it has for my students.

# 1. Think Multimodally

As writing instructor Melanie Gagich explains in “An Introduction to and Strategies for Multimodal Composing” (*Writing Spaces*, Volume 3), the different modes of communication are visual, linguistic, spatial, gestural, and aural (67). Any communicative act which uses more than one of these modes at a time is considered “multimodal.” Think about the last in-person conversation you had with a friend or family member. You spoke (aural), but you likely also communicated nonverbally through facial expressions or body language (gestural). Multimodality is something you do constantly, whether you know it or not. Even the countless essays you write are multimodal (Ball and Charlton 42), combining elements of the linguistic (words), spatial (margins, line/paragraph spacing), and visual (font style/size, use of bold type or italics for emphasis).

Thinking in terms of multimodality helps inform the choices you make in writing your script. You will produce quality work if you consider your writing not as an isolated act but as one communicative element working *in combination* with others. Ask yourself questions about the choices you make while composing your script:

- How will these words sound when spoken out loud?
- What music would complement my work?
- Would sound effects be appropriate at certain points?

While this essay may be focused on the writing portion of your podcast assignment, it is also important to remember that writing does not happen in a vacuum. Because you are composing something that will eventually be translated to another media form, your podcast script is an ideal exercise in multimodality, challenging you to think of your writing beyond text only. As a bonus, when you actively think about your writerly choices and why you made them, you are engaging in *metacognition* (often defined as “thinking about thinking”) by viewing your writing not merely as a product but as a mental *process* (Taczak and Robertson 211). As most writing instructors will tell you, such self-awareness is key to being a more versatile and effective writer.

# 2. Consider the Rhetorical Situation

The “rhetorical situation” is a set of circumstances that shape communication. These include audience, topic, author, purpose, and context (“The Rhetorical Situation”). When writing your script---or *any* writing, for that matter---you should always be mindful of your rhetorical situation. Consider this essay. My intended audience is undergraduate writing students. The topic is podcast scriptwriting. I establish myself as an authority by discussing my knowledge of podcasts and citing my teaching experience. My purpose is to prepare students for a kind of writing that is likely foreign to them. Finally, the context---i.e., where this essay is published---is *Writing* *Spaces*, an open-source textbook. These considerations collectively inform how I share my ideas, from organization to tone.

When writing a podcast script, context and audience are especially important. Because podcasts are typically distributed online---and the online world is, you know, *massive*---you may be especially tempted to think that the audience for your work is simply “anyone” or “the general public.” However, listen to any number of successful podcasts, and you’ll notice an important quality that they share: all tailor their content for specific audiences. Folks who listen to *Big Screen Sports* do so because they enjoy watching and talking about a distinct cinematic genre: sports movies. *The* *Daily* is a popular option for those who want to stay up to date on current political events. I keep returning to *The Fantasy Footballers* because I want to figure out how I can win my league for the third time. Ask yourself who your intended audience is and write your script accordingly. Your work will be more focused, compelling, and effective overall if you carefully consider who it is meant for. Listeners, who have seemingly endless options to choose from, want podcasts that speak to their unique interests. They aren’t nearly as interested in podcasts created for “anyone.”

# 3. Find and Analyze Examples

It never hurts to seek inspiration from others. Fortunately, many podcasters include transcripts so that their work is accessible to diverse listeners, including non-native speakers and the disabled. (This is also justification for writing a script even if you are *not* required to do so. Including a transcript with your podcast demonstrates that you are trying to be welcoming and inclusive.) Specifically, I recommend searching for podcasts that have been produced by academic institutions, which are required to make learning materials easily accessible in accordance with the Americans with Disabilities Act. Whereas, if you search through commercially produced podcasts, you’ll find that some include transcripts while others do not.

Additionally, because podcasting requires multimodal thinking, it is helpful to listen to completed podcasts. Doing so will help you think about the relationship between text and aurality. A great source to look specifically for student work is the Student Podcast Challenge, a yearly contest run by *NPR*. Winning entries are published on *NPR*’s website and include works by middle schoolers, high schoolers, and college students (“Here They Are: The Best Student Podcasts in America”). Most entries are less than ten minutes long, akin to typical length requirements for most podcast assignments. These student works will give you some idea of what a successful podcast sounds like and how that success is achieved in large part through writing.

To that end, remember that when you are reading and/or listening to sample works, your analysis should include not just the actual content but the means through which it is delivered (Bunn 72). Pay close attention to the creative choices on display, to the *how* as much as the *what*, in the same way that you would use a metacognitive lens to examine your own work. Examples of questions you might ask include:

- What is the purpose driving this work?
- If I were the author/creator, are there any choices I would have made differently?
- Is the tone suitable given both the subject and audience?
- Does the content work well with other elements like music?
- Which techniques do I find engaging or alienating?
- How effectively does this work handle transitions between ideas?

For further guidance on how to approach others’ works with a critical eye, consider writing scholar Mike Bunn’s “How to Read Like a Writer” (*Writing Spaces*, Volume 2). While that essay does not explicitly discuss podcasting, its insights can easily be applied to different media forms.

# 4. Rethink How You Cite Your Research

Another reason why I’m a fan of podcasting is that it demands thorough engagement with one’s research. You must consider the sources collected, why you chose them, and how to introduce them to your audience. Granted, these considerations are *always* important in composition. Once again, though, writing for audio means doing things a little differently. For example, let’s say that I’m writing an essay about great American cinema and want to incorporate a line from Roger Ebert’s review of Martin Scorsese’s *GoodFellas*. I might write something like the following: *It has been said* *that Martin Scorsese’s best films are “poems about guilt” (Ebert)*. This would work fine for an essay. My chosen quote is clearly indicated with quotation marks, and the parenthetical citation credits the original author. (No pagination is included since this source was published online.) These are important textual and visual markers that ensure I have used my research ethically. However, we can’t assume that a *listening* audience will have this same information handy. Perhaps someone is not in position to access your transcript---like if they are listening while driving---or maybe they are visually impaired. How can they know which words are yours and which are someone else’s? Who is the source? For that matter, without a works cited page, how can the listener be sure that this source is trustworthy?

Therefore, when writing your script, you should provide enough information to properly contextualize your sources (Gagich 80; Woods and Ralston). Going back to the previous example, I might revise it for a podcast script thusly: *Chicago-Sun Times film critic Roger Ebert, in his 1990* *review of Martin Scorsese’s GoodFellas, writes that the legendary director’s* *films are “poems about guilt.”* That works much better for audio, doesn’t it? I have identified my source, which should always be done by full name when they are being introduced for the first time. My source’s relevance is established by mentioning his professional title. The context is made clear with mentions of the *type* of source (a review), its date, and the publication in which it appears. Additionally, any time a direct quote is used in your podcast, you should verbally bookend it with “quote” and “end-quote” to distinguish your words from your source’s. For that same reason, I recommend using direct quotes sparingly in your script, as too many can undermine your authorial voice and make your work seem less authentic as a result. (This is sound advice not just for scripts but any academic writing you do.) Remember, it is *your* distinct persona and storytelling style that differentiate your podcast from others and build genuine audience investment. Direct quotes are a great way to demonstrate that you’ve done your homework, but they are best used in moderation.

Lastly, it bears noting that there’s more to ethical citation than just making sure you have included the relevant information. Properly crediting sources is how you establish your credibility, earn the trust of the academic community, and acknowledge your debt to those who have helped shape your knowledge. When I say “properly,” that incudes demonstrating *cultural sensitivity*, which becomes even more critical when citing research verbally. For example, if you are citing an author from a foreign country, take time to learn the proper pronunciation of their name. Additionally, if you borrow a direct quote, do not “correct” linguistic styles to align with your own language, as doing so erases cultural identity and reinforces harmful notions about “right” and “wrong” means of expression (Young 112). These few extra considerations ensure that you cite your sources both correctly and respectfully.

# 5. It’s All in the Wording.

Thoughtfully crafted wording is the difference between mere description and creating an evocative world for the listener (Gutiérrez 41). Because podcasting is tied to performance more so than formal essay-writing (Jones 77), your script is an opportunity to highlight your individual voice. With podcasts, students are able to draw from “their home cultures, languages, and lived experiences as a resource” (Curlee and Early 90), and this applies as much to the writing as the actual recording. Rather than using overly generic description, prioritize wording that is more vibrant and inventive. In addition to making the writing process more enjoyable, writing more creatively allows you to capture and hold your audience’s attention more effectively. Remember the podcasts I listed at the beginning of this essay? I don’t listen to them because they all sound the same. Quite the contrary, each has its own nuanced style and delivery. People listen to podcasts because they want an *experience*, not just information. Your words, both in writing and your verbal delivery, can give them just that.

In addition to blending writing and performance (Jones 77), evocative wording serves a functional purpose by creating mental imagery that can make your ideas more accessible for your audience (Gutiérrez 41). One example of a work that is enhanced by imaginative writing is “Beaching and Moaning,” a podcast episode in the long-running series *This American Life*. The narrator, James Spring, discusses a heated public controversy in San Diego, California over the presence and safety of harbor seals at La Jolla Beach. (If you listen to this episode, be advised that it contains strong language and threats of violence.) Spring opens with the following: “I feel like it all starts with their little noses. So cute. Soft and rubbery like a pony’s. And their stubby flippers. Their plump little bodies like speckled bean bags” (“Beaching and Moaning”). His words paint a vivid picture of what the seals look like, which helps the audience understand why humans would be simultaneously drawn to and protective of them. It’s a fantastic way to kickstart the episode. More than just adding artistry, your words have the potential to concretize images and abstract concepts for your audience.

The other quality one notices in Spring’s narration is that it is straightforward and free of any specialized jargon. His metaphors are effective because they are rendered in wording that any audience can understand. You should proceed similarly while writing your own script. This is just one more way of making your work accessible. You needn’t populate your writing with the most lavish terms you can find. As the above example shows, there is poetry to be found in everyday language. Therefore, a worthwhile approach to considering word choices would be to develop a personal thesaurus “geared to the spoken word” (Gutiérrez 29). “Perspicacious” and “perceptive” may mean the same thing, but the latter is probably easier for you to say in narration and will therefore make a better listening experience for your audience. Compiling a thesaurus can also help you identify challenging words---if, for example, you are writing about a subject pertaining to a culture other than your own---so that you can check their phonetic spellings.

Given that your word choices are important, you should make them responsibly. Remember that there is a difference between enhancing and embellishing. Being overly reliant on performative language can push your work closer to the latter and compromise your position as the author. Keeping your audience engaged is important, but you still bear the responsibility for treating your subject with the sensitivity it deserves. This is especially true if your podcast is on a somber topic like sexual violence or our flawed criminal justice system, in which case there is a fine ethical line between audience engagement and reducing serious subjects to mere entertainment (Peterson). Think about what words serve your podcast and translate well to audio, and in doing so, remember that the words you choose can ultimately say just as much about you as they do about your subject.

# 6. Write It How You Plan to Speak It

As you can likely tell from this section and the previous one, I am a firm believer in the notion that *delivery* plays a crucial role in determining whether a podcast is successful or not. I also love nothing more as a writing instructor than moving my students away from the five-paragraph structure. You are likely familiar with this form of writing. In fact, you have probably written so many essays this way that by now, doing so feels like muscle memory. But imagine trying to read through a five-paragraph essay for a podcast. It likely wouldn’t go very well because that structure does not match the rhythms of actual human speech. So, as I tell my students very frankly: write your script however you plan to speak it into existence. This is precisely what I do with my own work. Just glance at the excerpt from a script I wrote a few years ago (Figure 1).

> There indeed was a time when Clemson was a “small” college town. But things change, and Clemson is no exception.
>
> Over the last decade, the city has experienced exponential growth, both economically and in population.
>
> This is undeniably tied to the growth of the university itself, which remains the top source of employment in Pickens County. When we talk about the university, for all intents and purposes, we *are* talking about the city in the same breath.
>
> Student enrollment has steadily increased over the years, and new facilities have been added, as the school has gained a greater national prominence.
>
> A lot of this is thanks to the tremendous success of the football program. Two national championships in a decade will do that. Another saying we’re fond of is “There’s power in the paw.” And it’s stronger than ever, becoming one of the most recognizable brands in college athletics.
>
> At an initial glance, Clemson’s growth might be thought of as a good thing. But it’s a bit more complicated than that.

Figure 1. A sample portion from a podcast script. Original image provided by the author.

Notice the lack of traditional structure. This is by design, because while writing this script, I was mindful of how it would sound verbalized. Put otherwise, my scriptwriting requires self-awareness of my individual speech patterns and how I present myself for an audience who (assuming they don’t have the transcript in front of them) will be relying almost entirely on my voice to guide them. You should exercise the same mindset in your work. This is one more way of practicing metacognition to produce writing that is uniquely yours.

# Conclusion

Gardner Campbell, a retired English professor, writes, “There is magic in the human voice, the magic of shared awareness. Consciousness is most persuasively and intimately communicated via voice” (40). I agree with this sentiment, but I will also add that our voices are not just manifested in speech. Scriptwriting for a podcast may be dramatically different from writing you have done for previous classes, but it also has the potential to foreground your voice, improve your writing skills, provide a welcomed outlet for creativity, and allow you to contribute to an ever-growing media form. I hope the firsthand knowledge I have shared here will help get you started and move you that much closer to being a great podcaster. Whatever original works you create now or in the future, count me among those who would love to listen.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

This essay is intended to help guide students through the process of scriptwriting for a podcast assignment. The tips offered are based on my own experiences teaching podcasting to composition students.[^tr-1] These insights are geared first and foremost toward *pragmatism*, so this essay does not offer a precise step-by-step for how to create a podcast script. Some of the advice students receive include citation practices for audio work, the benefit of multimodality for writing, and the importance of considering the rhetorical situation when writing for digital publication. Therefore, this essay has broad application for podcast assignments of any length or subject, for both solo projects and group work. Most importantly, students can use what they learn from this reading if they want to continue podcasting in the future.

Because this essay focuses on one element of a podcast assignment, bear in mind that the more technical aspects of podcasting (e.g., audio-editing, using licensed music) are not covered. This essay is meant primarily to be a resource for students being introduced to scriptwriting for the first time and who may be anxious about departing from more formal academic writing. It is presumed that scriptwriting has been built into a podcast assignment by the instructor. However, this essay can be provided to students even if they are not required to write scripts.

Additionally, this essay can be used to generate meaningful discussions about academic writing practices. For example, one benefit of scriptwriting is that it has the potential to foreground the unique voices and lived experiences of students. Instructors can use this as an opportunity to discuss the need for greater diversity in education, especially in writing courses. Or, the fluid nature of scripting, as opposed to the rigid writing structures many students are accustomed to, can lead to discussion about the importance of process- rather than product-focused writing.

Ultimately, this essay should serve three purposes: 1) helping students maximize the process of writing their podcast scripts; 2) highlighting the ways that scriptwriting can help students improve as writers; and 3) encouraging reflection about writing itself and how it is taught. In short, this essay can be used by instructors for both critical and functional purposes.

Below are discussion questions and low-stakes assignments that can be used in combination with this chapter:

# Questions

1. There is a podcast for everyone. Do you have one that you listen to on a regular basis? If so, what keeps you coming back? Think carefully about what qualities you find most engaging. Is there something about this podcast that you would change to improve it?

2. Based on what you read in this chapter, how does scriptwriting compare with academic writing you have done in the past?

3. We know now that multimodality is a part of daily life. What is one example of multimodal communication you have encountered recently? Which modes of communication were used and to what effect(s)? Was one mode emphasized more than the other(s)?

4. Why is the rhetorical situation pivotal to communication? Are there elements of the rhetorical situation that merit special attention when it comes to writing for a podcast, and if so, why?

5. What makes accessibility such a key consideration in scriptwriting? What do we mean when we say “accessibility”? What responsibilities does this term imply for us as authors?

# Activities

## Storytime With Orson Welles

On Halloween night in 1938, legendary filmmaker and notorious “trickster” Orson Welles narrated a script written by Howard Koch, which was adapted from H.G. Wells’ sci-fi novel, *The War of the Worlds*. This fictionalized broadcast, produced by The Mercury Theatre, uses a breaking-news approach to inform the public about a supposed alien invasion that is underway. The episode is infamous for causing widespread panic among listeners who believed that the events being described were real, which in turn led to public outrage and calls for greater regulations in media. Students can listen to the original broadcast on YouTube (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=crPGFZiFjfs) and read the transcript (https://www.wellesnet.com/the-war-of-the-worlds-radio-script/). Have them perform a rhetorical analysis of the *War of the Worlds* broadcast. What narrative elements do they find most effective? Are there moments of metaphorical writing that are especially noteworthy? (One that stands out to me is the description of the aliens’ faces: “The eyes are black and gleam like a serpent” [“‘The War of the Worlds’ radio script”].) How does this broadcast employ multimodality? How does Orson Welles’s journalistic delivery enhance Koch’s script? How does the broadcast use sound effects? Viewed in a broader context, what does this well-known and controversial moment in radio history tell us about ethics in public communication?

## Tell, Don’t Show

Have students locate trailers for their favorite movies or TV shows on You-Tube and write scripts for them. This is an assignment that students tend to appreciate because, among other things, it gives them an opportunity to write about media that they enjoy. (Who doesn’t like doing that?) Students should imagine that they are writing for an audience that does not have audiovisual elements like camera angles, music, special effects, and so forth to guide them. In other words, students are writing for a listening rather than a viewing audience. Each student’s goal is to match the action, imagery, tone, and pacing of their chosen trailer using the power of the written word! In translating the aural and visual into text, students are challenged to think about the connections among these different modes of communication.
