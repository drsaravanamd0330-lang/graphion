# Introduction

Imagine trying to pass freshman college English twenty-four times, as one student with undiagnosed disabilities had to do until she received assistance. We have written this chapter to help students like you understand more about disabilities that can affect writing. We are Krystia, a writing instructor, and David, her former student and a veteran with disabilities, and we include some recommended practices and resources that assist writers with and without diagnosed issues. In other words, we talk about things that help student writers with disabilities that can also help students without disabilities. We also introduce the work of some disability studies scholars and discuss existing laws and resources.

About twenty percent of the population have reading and writing disabilities (Kim 447; “Dyslexia FAQ”), in addition to other conditions that may affect writing classroom success. The lines are blurry between one disability and another, as well as with what is considered normal (Brueggemann, “An Enabling Pedagogy” 318). Nevertheless, disabilities are placed into categories to assist with therapies and mitigation (Babcock). The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) of 1975 (updated in 2004) guides K-12 accommodations (“About IDEA”) and identifies fourteen categories that can affect a student in a classroom:

> (1) autism, (2) deaf-blindness, (3) deafness, (4) emotional disturbance, (5) hearing impairment, (6) intellectual disability, (7) multiple disabilities, (8) orthopedic impairment, (9) other health impairment, (10) specific learning disability, (11) speech or language impairment, (12) traumatic brain injury, (13) visual impairment, and (14) developmental delay. (Dragoo 1)

Writers may have one or any combination of these. David shares how he navigates several disabilities as a student writer after returning to school following his military career.

# David’s Story

Prior to my service in the US Army, I had issues with attention deficit disorder (ADD). I learned better with my hands than reading a book. During my second deployment, the truck I was in got hit with a vehicle-borne explosive device. The aftereffects of this blast have taken away some memories and made it harder to keep focused---leading to a diagnosis of a traumatic brain injury (TBI). Now, when I’m trying to write an essay, I will start it just fine and then hit a wall that sometimes takes a day or two before it goes away and I can get back to what I was doing.

Just before the COVID-19 outbreak, I started getting pain in my lower spine. At first, I didn’t think much of it, but now I have severe degenerative disc disease around my L5 vertebra, bone spurs, and mild to moderate lumbar scoliosis. Due to these complications and conditions, I cannot do as much physical labor as I once did, so I decided to make a complete career change to something less physically demanding.

Going back to school was necessary for my physical and mental health. I had a few meetings with a vocational rehab specialist before I decided to go for Health Information Technology as a major.

I remembered how difficult it was for me in high school to get through any of the classes that I was taking, except for woodworking and automotive classes. I knew if I didn’t get some kind of help in the classroom, I would never be able to make it. I set up an appointment with the Student Accommodation Services (SAS) and had a long discussion with them about what I needed to do to get the help that I needed. First, my mental health provider had to write a letter telling them what my diagnosis was and what things were recommended and what I would need specific help with. Second, I sent that information back to the SAS and had another meeting to have a service plan written up. In the meantime, I was able to see someone in speech therapy, and they gave me a lot of information on different techniques for notetaking, studying, time management, and preparedness.

> While the classroom requires a lot of writing, I found I do my best

work during class if the teacher gives us time to write or I make sure to write immediately after class. The easiest way to be successful in the classroom setting is to do as much as I can while in the classroom. This way I can ask questions about the material I’m working on. If you are someone like me who hates to ask for help, know that most teachers, staff members, tutors, etc. want to see you succeed, not just in their classes, but in life as well.

To be successful in a classroom setting, you will have to find out what works best for you: it might be one thing or a multitude of things together. There are tools out there for you to use, and most are free. I use a few different things to help me:

1. The LiveScribe SmartPen lets me write my notes in a special notebook and simultaneously converts my handwriting to text in an app. A microphone on the pen will also record everything going on in the classroom as I write. When I go to study, I just tap on anything I wrote down in the app, and the corresponding audio recording will start to play from that moment in class.

2. Microsoft Word can transcribe everything for you. This means that I can dictate what I want to write, and the program will write what I say. Another Microsoft program, OneNote, can also store the audio recording.

3. Kurzweil 3000 is a text-to-speech program that can read your textbooks out loud to you. I find a combination of hearing, seeing, and saying the words helps me remember more. I was able to get the LiveScribe SmartPen and Kurzweil 3000 software

through the Student Accommodation Center. Without those programs in place, it would have taken me a lot longer to get through school. Another word of advice to any student dealing with any kind of disability: do the best you can with what you have, and don’t give up on yourself.

# Interlude

David’s story highlights how disabilities can develop throughout life. For example, David’s service as a soldier added TBI to his ADD, each of which took time to diagnose and manage appropriately. Because of diagnosed disabilities, David has a legal right to accommodations---modifications or adjustments that allow students equal access to educational activities---thanks to the 1990 Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), its updates, and other laws (“Disclosing a Disability”). While the accommodations David shares are particularly helpful for his disabilities, scholars in disability studies also challenge us to think about how everyone can benefit from such resources (Dolmage, “Mapping Composition” 25).

Jay Dolmage recommends widespread implementation of Universal Design (UD), where spaces and products are designed to be usable by all people, including those with disabilities. Examples of UD are an entrance that doesn’t have steps and a building’s inclusion of elevators. In education, Universal Design for Learning (UDL) involves practices that make education more accessible to all (Dolmage, “Mapping Composition” 25; Hamilton and Petty 6). Therefore, it’s worth thinking about how things that help David might also help writers like you generally: making use of tutors, writing time in class, and technology that helps take notes, hear, and/or dictate text.

It is worth noting that sometimes students may be misdiagnosed or not yet diagnosed, too, as a retired nurse named Ann had been. Ann was a participant in Krystia’s dissertation study about older adult writers, and when she was younger, Ann would have benefitted from similar accommodations to those David describes, but she had disabilities that were harder to identify. Unfortunately, Ann also went to school before accommodations existed in US American education, so her life shows how education for people with disabilities has been changed by late 20th century American laws protecting people with disabilities.

# Ann’s Story, by Krystia

In graduate school, I met Ann--a retired nurse who shared how she didn’t pass a college English course twenty-four times. In fact, she struggled with reading and writing her whole life. In third grade, she was given an IQ test and labeled “mentally retarded”: “my parents were told to pull me out of school to work as soon as was legally possible, which was the age of fourteen” (Nora 147). One problem was that her teachers and parents didn’t understand Ann could not read the IQ test well because of a visual impairment and learning difference, issues that were not diagnosed until decades later. No one--including Ann--understood when she was younger why she couldn’t read or write, although her father had observed her tutoring her siblings and didn’t agree with the label she’d been given (Nora 179). Regardless of her disability, she should have had been helped, not dismissed, but laws to protect a disabled student’s right to an education didn’t exist in 1948.

When Ann was fourteen, her family removed her from school and sent her to work, as the school advised. The Korean War had just ended, and she decided to join a nearby night school with veterans to continue her education, hiding her books in a random open building so her family wouldn’t find out (Nora 172). She didn’t know it was a church, but the priest noticed her and got her to explain why she hid books there. She admitted, “I didn’t want a life without an education” (Nora 173). The priest set up tutoring from retired teaching nuns (Nora 175). One-on-one instruction helped Ann, as did the night school’s use of verbal tests. Ann and her tutors theorized she may have a vision issue no one understood. When she was 21, she earned her high school diploma through oral exams (Nora 179).

Eventually, Ann moved from Pittsburgh to Atlanta to go to nursing school, and she went to double the classes required--attending both the classes for White and for Black students in the still-segregated school--to make sure she learned all the content. Using skills from prior tutors and a calligraphy teacher who re-taught Ann letter shapes by tracing letters on her back, Ann would stay up each night to rewrite nursing reports to be legible for others, as the first drafts of her notes had backwards letters (Nora 184).

After graduating and working years as a nurse, she went back to school for a bachelor’s degree. The course she could not pass was College English, which Ann repeated every quarter for six years until one teacher asked her how many times she had taken the course. After quizzing Ann verbally, the instructor noticed that Ann knew all the content and advised her to visit the college’s newly established disability services (Nora 190). This was 1980, after Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 had passed, which established a legal standard to provide accommodations and establish disability services in schools. However, it was not until 1990 that the ADA formally required disability policies in higher education.

After meeting with disability services, Ann was referred to a new vision doctor who discovered that she had multiple disabilities (Nora 191-3). The doctor first corrected her crossed eyes, which had affected her ability to focus, and eventually realized Ann also had a rare form of severe colorblindness. Lastly, she was diagnosed with dyslexia, which affects writing as well as reading (Kim 456). Once her disabilities were properly diagnosed and “reasonable and appropriate accommodations” were provided (“The Americans with Disabilities Act”), Ann completed her bachelor’s degree the next year and then earned a master’s degree in counseling a couple years later (Nora 195).

Nevertheless, Ann told me, “I wouldn’t say I felt comfortable with writing, ever” (Nora 188). Her post-master’s degree work accommodations letter stated she should never be required to write independently. When her employer tried to fire her in the 1990s for her written mistakes, she took them to court and won thanks to the ADA and its protections for people with disabilities who receive accommodations. Ann kept her job and excelled with other forms of communication, such as on the phone and in person (Nora 196).

After Ann retired, she worked to respond to her nieces’ request for her life stories, especially about how she came to march with Martin Luther King, Jr. Motivated by a supportive writing group, Ann challenged herself to draft and revise several hundred pages about her life and explained how her disability struggles led to her Civil Rights activism (Nora 199).

# What We Can Learn from Ann and David

It was both luck and determination that helped Ann get an education despite having been a child when people with disabilities were not protected. She found tutors and resources, even though these were not systematically available at the time. Even when resources were introduced to educational institutions because of IDEA and ADA and their later amendments, Ann’s needs were difficult to determine because some of her disabilities were rare, as well as because of persisting ableist educational structures. Ableism refers to systems and attitudes that privilege non-disabled people and discriminate against disabled people (“Ableism”; Dolmage *Academic* *Ableism* 7). An example of ableist thinking was when Ann’s parents were told to remove Ann from school at the earliest legal age.

Like with David, hearing and dictating texts were vital for Ann’s success. The night school she attended offered auditory tests and presentations that allowed her to show off what she learned, unlike Ann’s high school. Composition and disability scholars agree that we should expand literacy definitions to include more than silent reading and writing. Speaking and hearing text are equally valuable to silent reading and writing, and incorporating audible text can help students who might otherwise be left behind (Green 459). Patricia A. Dunn says more students can show off their meaning-making talents when writing and reading activities involve not just visual textual (silent) labor, but multisensory activities involving sound, images, movement, and more (*Learning Re-Abled* 201). Another strategy is creating computer texts using words, images, and audio recordings (Price and Bahl).

While Krystia works to incorporate UDL principles into her writing classes, including making deadlines less strict to help students who need more time (Olivas 260-1; Womack 496), not all writing classes will have accommodations built in. David’s and Ann’s stories exemplify how visiting the student accommodations office can guide you to the correct medical resources, testing, and needed accommodations. With these, Ann no longer had to work at least twice as hard as her peers and David could successfully complete his courses.

# Disabled or Not, Some Recommended Writing Practices

Practices that help disabled writers also help many other writers, just as UD products help people of varying abilities. The difference is that if you have disabilities, certain accommodations are essential, but even if you don’t, they can be helpful. Thanks to accessible design features in computers, writers can try various technologies that used to be expensive. The LiveScribe SmartPen and Microsoft apps that David recommended are reasonably priced and easy to try. Additional technology that may help:

- Listen to audio-versions of readings.
- Try dictation and text-reader technologies included in most computers.
- Have your computer read your papers to you.
- Record yourself reading your papers aloud.
- Incorporate images in drafting, even if you need to edit them out later.
- Create multisensory presentations of your thoughts.

Furthermore, you can experiment with Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI). While generative AI shouldn’t be used to write final drafts and should be cited when used (MLA Style Center; McAdoo), it can help writers by assisting with generating ideas, organizing, learning genre conventions (e.g., how to write a resume, essay), and editing (Laist 171). Sid Dobrin’s *AI and Writing* is a student guide about benefits and pitfalls of that tech, including instructions on how to avoid plagiarizing.

Sometimes, engaging the body and space around you can help, too. Experiment with movement when reading and writing. Organize ideas in the spaces around you using printouts, notecards, etc. Pay attention to your individual writing processes and time. Tara Wood’s study of students with disabilities shows that reflecting on your writing processes can help you better understand what works best for you as you create time for your drafting, writing, and revising. Be aware of how time-consuming writing is for all writers and experiment with different ways to budget writing time. See whether class time for writing can help you, as it does for David. Also, try getting individualized help at a writing center.

Another thing you can do is be considerate of others with disabilities when writing. Some ways to do this include:

- creating heading levels in documents to assist in text-to-speech tech, including alt text---what is read by text-to-speak software that describes the images and graphs (Dunn, “Teaching Disability”; Donegan 111--14);
- using easy to read fonts (Molloy 275);
- making sure presentation slides have at least 24-point font and high contrast text; and
- adding audio recordings to your digital texts (Price and Bahl).

Considering different kinds of readers is an intrinsic part of writing, and this should include accommodating readers with varied disabilities.

# Concluding Thoughts

If you are a student with disabilities or suspect you may have one or more, we recommend you meet with your college’s student accommodations office to see what resources are available. The ADA, along with other relevant laws, dictates that disability status in US higher education can only be disclosed by a student. As a result, it is up to *you* as a student to ask for assistance; this might look like disclosure of diagnose(s), choosing to visit the student accommodations office, and/or requesting assistance. You choose what accommodations are communicated, and with whom. This independence is very different from the PreK-12 environment where teachers regularly refer students for testing, and the PreK-12 schools keep records of disability status. At college, your meeting will be confidential, as the ADA is one of several laws that protect the information you share (“The Americans with Disabilities Act”; “Disclosing a Disability”).

If you don’t feel comfortable disclosing your disabilities because of the course design and/or instructor, you aren’t alone. Some disability and writing studies authors relate to this and argue you shouldn’t have to disclose (Hubrig 197; Rinaldi). We encourage you to discuss options and concerns with your academic advisor, student accommodations office, and other trusted sources at your college to determine your best options. While most colleges allow students to drop and add classes without penalty during the first week of class, check your college’s policies before making any schedule altering decisions.

If you want to learn more about the history of classes not accommodating students, we recommend Brueggeman’s short YouTube video “Why I Mind” and Dolmage’s *Academic Ableism* (available as a free audiobook). Unfortunately, US American higher education was originally designed to exclude people with disabilities (Dolmage, *Academic Ableism* 36), a complex history that Ann lived through. While Ann continued her studies despite there being no disability protections or accommodations for half her life, her attaining bachelor’s and master’s degrees was thanks to disability rights advocates and laws passed in the second half of the 20th century.

There is no formula that works for every writer with disabilities. Some may need more time (Wood 280--81) or multimodal and multisensory meaning making opportunities (Dunn; Smilges 117). Others may benefit from hearing and saying things aloud (Green 459) or class recordings with carefully revised closed captioning and assistance for the hearing impaired (Brueggemann “Why I Mind”; Zdenek 16). This is not a comprehensive list, and you may need multiple forms of accommodation. Seek out experts to assist you.

Whether or not a class is designed in a way that best accommodates your disabilities and/or writing needs, you can find additional help. Librarians, disability professionals, your professors, and even your peers have a wealth of knowledge about different resources available, many of which can be used by people regardless of ability status. Contact operating system and software tech supports to learn about and set up preinstalled accommodations. Seek out assistance. Believe in your potential for success.

# Teacher Resources

My former student David and I explored in our chapter how disabilities can affect a student in a writing classroom through two literacy narratives: David’s and Ann’s.[^tr-1] We did this to guide students who may need additional assistance to their college’s or university’s student accommodations office, as well as to introduce students to disability studies. Thus, this section for teachers will focus on strategies to build on the chapter’s content.

# Activities and Discussion Questions

Parallel activities to this chapter can include having students:

- write a reader response to the chapter.
- discuss resources available at your college/university for students with disabilities.
- share an experience or literacy narrative focusing on how a health issue impeded one’s ability to write for school and how this was managed.
- explore a library database for more articles pertaining to disability studies and accommodations for disabilities in higher education. Help students to narrow their focus to one disability, issue in disability studies, or historical moment in disability studies history.
- write a research paper or create a researched multimodal presentation that examines one issue in disability studies and higher education.

Another project could be to have a class discussion about the chapter, encouraging students to ask questions and engage with the content. Questions instructors can ask include:

- How much did you know about disabilities in higher education before reading this chapter?
- What were the most important things you learned from reading this chapter?
- What does Ann’s story show us about the importance of education law for people with disabilities?
- In what ways do both David’s and Ann’s stories reveal the permeability of disability categories and how disabilities can affect students in a higher education writing classroom?
- What can we learn from David’s story about disabilities and writing in higher education in the 2020s?

In conversations, it will be important to remind students that they are not expected to self-disclose disabilities. In fact, regardless of the follow up assignments chosen, college professors cannot directly refer a student for disability testing or student accommodations, nor ask students to self-disclose. Although Ann’s professor did this in the 1980s, according to the 1990 ADA, adult student disability status is legally protected and should only be self-disclosed by the student (“Disclosing a Disability...”). This is a stark difference from the K-12 environment where teachers are a primary source of disability testing referrals and schools hold records of disabilities, as guided by IDEA (“Disclosing a Disability...”).

College writing professors can help students---regardless of whether they disclose disabilities---by deliberately building awareness about disabilities and neurodiversity. Instructors can talk about disability services available, work to be advocates, and incorporate elements of UDL into the writing classroom and syllabus as recommended by Jay Dolmage and other writing scholars. In 2023, Lorna Hamilton and Stephany Petty sum up the literature on UDL in this way: “Existing research suggests that UDL is an effective methodology for improving the learning process for all; students undertaking UDL courses report higher satisfaction, greater engagement, and reduced barriers to learning” (6). Therefore, incorporating UDL practices will not only help students with disabilities, but all the students in a class.

# Some Ways to Make Writing Classrooms More Accessible

One easy way to accommodate students is to provide as many flexible deadlines as possible. Anne-Marie Womack’s “Teaching Is Accommodation: Universally Designing Composition Classes and Syllabi” emphasizes that “studies show that additional time tends to benefit all students, and especially disabled students” (496, referring to the literature review by Sireci, et al.). What I do in my syllabus is to provide a calendar with recommended deadlines aligning with classroom activities. Students in my class are also invited to resubmit most assignments as many times as necessary throughout the term after recommended deadlines, not only accommodating students who need more time but also emphasizing the writing process. Some professors may worry that extending the time for grading papers indefinitely throughout the term can create chaos.

However, my experience in several kinds of higher education institutions has revealed that most of my students do not take advantage of this opportunity. While my students prefer to turn work in on time and to not get behind, the opportunities for extended deadlines and revision are usually very beneficial for the percentage of students who take advantage of them.

That said, some assignments cannot be redone nor be submitted late for practical reasons, and I explain why to students. One example is peer review. In my classes, peer review is inspired by Claude Mark Hurlbert’s workshop model where students share two pages of their writing with the class (185-187). Then, they provide comments to peers about what they liked and why, along with comments structured as “how would it change your meaning if you…?” or “HWICYMIY…?” to provide options for revisions. Each peer response concludes with an endnote with general comments about what the commenters engaged with in the writing. This is an assignment that cannot be completed late or redone as students move on to new drafts, but I provide make up peer review opportunities for students who miss for excused reasons. Another example of an assignment that cannot be submitted late is a project that will be published, although in my classes this kind of project has multiple prior deadlines (including peer review dates) and many opportunities for revision before the final, unchangeable deadline.

We also help students both with and without disabilities by providing time for students to write in class--a practice David finds especially helpful. This allows the professor to provide mini conferences when walking from one student writer to another, engaging with students during the writing process. As Womack states, we should be “creating access for students with disabilities and empowering all students” (500). Making time for all students to write during class time gives students immediate access to assistance and can help all the students in the class.

A third idea is to engage student choice and authority. I allow students to choose reading and writing topics that engage and/or showcase their interests and abilities. An example of this is a reader response assignment in response to a student’s choice of a *Writing Spaces* chapter. Emphasizing choice can extend from providing topic options, as I do, through having students assist in assignment or even syllabus designs. For example, Ira Shor’s *When Students Have Power: Negotiating Authority in the Critical Pedagogy Classroom* describes how his students design their syllabi, as well as assignments. Such student-centered, critical pedagogy practices can allow students who struggle with disabilities to voice their needs and priorities in writing assignments and classrooms. I particularly mention this here because the older adult writing group that nurtured Ann’s writing during her retirement was guided by a participant design model. The members decided everything from what writing they would share to how they would share their writing and what kinds of comments they wanted (Nora 234).

Additionally, as we discussed in the chapter, multimodal assignments can be very helpful for students with and without disabilities. I have students not only write reader response essays, for example, but also craft oral presentations about these responses, and other presentation formats are welcomed. Students can be invited to participate in assignment design and brainstorm varied ways to share their developed ideas about what they read. As Dolmage states, active student input throughout a semester is an ideal for universal design, where “users should help shape the space” (*Academic Ableism* 119).

> Another thing I do is utilize Blackboard’s Ally, an AI that checks

courses and their content for accessibility, to double check that my materials are well designed for all my students. I also make sure all class videos and recordings are closed captioned. For more about incorporating elements of UDL into a writing classroom, many sources from the chapter’s Works Cited will be helpful.

# Reader Response Assignment

Here, I provide my reader response assignment for instructors who would like a more detailed example of a specific assignment that can be used with this chapter. When my students read from the *Writing Spaces* series, I have them write a reader response that engages students with the writing and research quality of the text, which inevitably also gets students discussing the content as well. I have repeatedly seen how this assignment helps students analyze a chapter’s text in rich and meaningful ways. This is the template I share with students before discussing the assignment and incorporating student input that potentially reshapes this project.

## Reader Response Assignment Directions Template:

Answer the following questions in multiple paragraphs, backing up your points by quoting and/or paraphrasing pertinent sections of the chapter you read, focusing first on your experience as a reader:

- What did you enjoy about the text? Why?
- What was intriguing? Why?
- What bored you? Why?
- What about the text made it easier or harder to read?
- What about the text made it more or less interesting?

Think also about the research done to create this text:

- What research do you imagine could have gone into the writing of your text?
- Are there any citations, and, if not, how is the research evident?
- What is the quality of the research in your text?

Your answers to these questions will provide the core content for your reader response, which may be structured as an academic essay. One purpose of this assignment is to get you interacting with well-written texts from the world outside of our classroom and to get you thinking about what makes this chapter well-written (or not). It will also get you thinking about the field of study that shapes the course you are in. Answering the questions above will help you in your own writing and in commenting on fellow student papers. This reader response will showcase your ability to accurately represent and evaluate the formal assertions of others. Work to make your reader response about 500 words long and follow the directions from the class guide on how to do both in-text and end-of-text citations for chapters from *Writing Spaces* volumes. In a week, you will also present to the class about what you wrote, share a key quote from what you read, and talk about what you learned as a writer.
