abstract: Drawing from recent research conducted with transnational, multilingual students, this essay introduces a dynamic and complex set of translanguaging practices that current students of writing can learn from.[^1] The essay starts with stories about how students in the study use their multiple linguistic and communicative resources as they navigate different contexts of their lives. These resources include translating words and ideas, mixing or switching languages, using multimodal, paralinguistic, or technological means of communication, and developing the ability to ‘read the world’ and understand affordances and constraints on communication. However, as the next section discusses, the students often felt that their translanguaging practices were unvalued, invisible, or irrelevant in classrooms that prioritized standardized language practices and Euro-American styles of writing as markers of success. The last part of the essay offers suggestions for reimagining the classroom as a transnational space. In this space, students are encouraged to draw from and reflect on their translanguaging practices, while fostering critical awareness about language ideologies which, in turn, helps them make decisions about when/where/how to resist or conform to others’ norms and expectations.

Though I am a teacher now, I used to be a multilingual, transnational student. When I lived in my home country and, later, when I moved to the US, I did not appreciate my language difference: I lamented making errors, not sounding “right,” and not having fully mastered English. Throughout my own education, I learned that to be a successful writer or speaker, I would have to scrub my accent, fix my errors, and learn how to speak and write like I had grown up in the U.K. (I learned British English in school). Also, while I generally considered my bilingualism (and knowledge of several other languages) and cross-cultural knowledge which I had gained from living in Denmark, the UK, New Zealand, and the US valuable, I didn’t consider my transnational resources relevant or welcomed in the classroom. When I became a teacher, I taught my students the same way I had been taught: I emphasized “correct” grammar and expected my students to write essays in a Euro-American rhetorical style. I asked my students to only speak English in the classroom and use English-medium sources when doing research.

Slowly, I became uneasy with this approach to teaching. I began to realize that all the corrective feedback I provided did not make much of a difference and, more importantly, I started noticing that most of my students had a negative self-image and saw themselves as “bad writers,” “bad at English,” or having a “bad accent.” Language is an integral part of who we are and our experiences and journeys in life, so I realized that this negative self-image was harmful. I started reading about “translanguaging,” which really shifted my view on language and writing fundamentally. Translanguaging scholars study how people mix and integrate all their linguistic and communicative resources in different ways depending on their communication situation; we pull from different languages, dialects, and styles, as well as other means of communication such as body language and technologies (García and Wei). The word “translanguaging” has two parts: “trans” means across or beyond, and “languaging” refers to the act of using language and other symbolic means of communication. Adding -ing to language makes it active, a verb. The word, then, illustrates the dynamic nature of language because when we cross or go beyond linguistic borders in our communication, new meaning emerges in response to the communicative situation. When looking at language through this lens, it is evident that multilinguals *and* monolinguals all use translanguaging because it is a natural way of “languaging.”

I started to understand that if translanguaging is natural, it should have a place in the classroom. Efforts to prescribe how students should speak and write according to the rules and conventions of standardized English are not only ineffective and harmful, but they create limitations for students’ learning, creativity, and expression. With this understanding, I came to see “difference in language not as a barrier to overcome or as a problem to manage, but as a resource for producing meaning in writing, speaking, reading, and listening” (Horner et al. 303). Looking at language difference as a resource also emphasizes the *choices* we make based on our assessment of our communicative situation. This emphasis on choices means we should help students---you---become critically aware of how and why you make the choices you do in shifting contexts rather than making you speak and write in a specific way. Rebecca Lorimer Leonard calls this assessment “rhetorical attunement,” which is a “sensitivity to language” (230) in different contexts, or an awareness of what works and what doesn’t work when we communicate.

To expand my understanding of translingual practice, I conducted a study with undergraduate students who self-identified as multilingual and transnational. This included students who had grown up in the US, who had come here as children or youth, and who were here on a student visa to pursue a degree. These students have transnational affiliations and regularly move across physical and/or digital borders to connect with family, friends, or communities outside the US Like me, they use multiple languages on a regular basis, such as English in the classroom and other languages while talking with friends and family either in-person or virtually. They often mix languages when they communicate with others who know the same languages they do, either when they speak, text, play videogames, or do other activities where mixing comes naturally. Sometimes they may even mix languages when they don’t intend to because “that is how [their] brains work,” as Paloma, a participant in my study, says. They often consume news, literature, music, TV, movies, social media content, recipes, menus, advertisements, and more in multiple languages. They have multiple references to and understandings of cultural concepts, events, and productions as well as rhetorical traditions that help them understand expectations for communication with different audiences or in different situations.

The main purpose of this essay is to share stories about my participants’ experiences and key implications for writing classrooms. Whether or not you identify as transnational and multilingual, I hope this can help you reframe your and others’ language difference as a resource for meaning-making.

# Communicating Across Borders: The Complex and Dynamic Use of Language

In this section, I describe patterns in my participants’ use of language outside the classroom because this is where their natural way of using language is most evident. All my participants have experiences moving across borders. The term “border” includes national and regional borders, but also cultural and linguistic borders where different expectations for communication are prevalent. The term “movement” includes relocating permanently or temporarily to a new location alone or with family or traveling back and forth between different countries or regions. But movement, especially in plurilingual settings where many languages and dialects are used, also involves moving across local settings (e.g. stores, markets, communities, or schoolyards) where different languages are used, and it involves crossing borders digitally when communicating online.

## Miras’ Story

> Miras, who grew up in a plurilingual environment in Kazakhstan, described his use of and exposure to language: he communicated in Kazakh with his family and friends, attended a Russian-medium school, learned British English as a foreign language, overheard several other languages and variations while shopping and in public places, listened to Turkish music, watched Hollywood movies, and mixed all his linguistic and semiotic (i.e. symbols, emojis, GIFs, embedded video clips etc.) resources while playing videogames.

Miras, like other participants, engages with myriad communicative resources on a regular basis, thereby developing an attunement to what kind of languaging works for him in different contexts as he learns, maintains relationships, seeks entertainment, and gets things done. By participating in activities involving so many different languages, modalities, genres, and technologies, Miras has a wide range of resources to pull from as he moves across communicative contexts.

Even participants who initially downplayed their resources by indicating they “just” know two languages are exposed to and engage with people in complex communicative contexts: they not only use or understand multiple dialects of their language, but they also engage with individuals in the US who use various dialects, and they have classmates or friends with different language backgrounds who expose them to new languages.

## Mingzhu’s Story

> Mingzhu noted she is “bilingual in English and Chinese,” but she also shared that she knows Mandarin, her “hometown language,” and Cantonese. One specific use of her Mandarin, which she learned at boarding school, is to support her mom’s literate activities. Mingzhu’s mom is a healthcare professional, whose job duties include writing professional documents in Pinyin (the phonetic symbols for Chinese characters used to transcribe Mandarin Chinese sounds into a Latin alphabet). The mom knows Mandarin but struggles with Pinyin, so Mingzhu is frequently asked to write or edit work documents, even now that Mingzhu lives in the US.

## Catalina’s Story

> Catalina is fluent in English and Spanish; she speaks Spanglish with many friends; and she is used to switching in and out of these three languages. As a child, she helped translate for her mom, for example, at doctor’s appointments. She laughed when she told me about her experiences translating between her mom and healthcare professionals and said she had no idea what half of the words meant. Yet, both her mom and the healthcare professionals depended on her---a child---to translate and broker meaning about highly sensitive and important information. Today, her mom knows more English and is completing a Master’s degree, and Catalina helps her mom with homework, while also helping her younger sister learn Spanish and complete homework.

Mingzhu and Catalina’s stories illustrate the complex work they do as they cross borders in myriad daily activities. While translanguaging is natural and therefore an ordinary phenomenon in multilingual, transnational individuals’ lives, it also involves a great deal of complexity. The complexity stems from having to work out and work across linguistic, cultural, and rhetorical differences. In their everyday lives, transnationals communicate in situations in which they perform an array of complex tasks: they translate; they quickly have to decide which language or word to use based on assessing the situation and whom their audience is; they determine if and when it works to mix languages; and they problem-solve creatively when communication breaks down. This creative problem-solving includes rephrasing, mimicking, drawing, writing, using symbols, body language, technology, and more. This complex work cultivates an ability to listen, speak, read, and/or write in multiple languages, but also the ability to attune to or ‘read situations’ and understand which resources to use. These communication acts are not only highly complex, but they require much mental work and, what’s more, they are often of critical importance: transactions need to be completed; paperwork needs to be filled out; and legal, financial, or health information needs to be understood.

# Linguistic Resources as a Strategy for Learning

The resources individuals develop from all this movement across borders can and should be utilized in US writing classrooms as they are valuable for learning but also for creativity and criticality. As I discuss below, most of my participants were dubious about the value and relevance of their translingual resources; nevertheless, it is evident they draw upon the resources to enhance their learning.

*Individual Strategies*: Individually, they take notes using a variety of strategies ranging from notes in English, another language, a mix of languages, or a mix of language and other symbols; when they review notes, they translate, look up words in dictionaries, search engines, or books; when they write, sometimes they write a whole draft or parts of a draft in a language other than English and translate later; when they read, they not only look things up, but sometimes seek out translations or support material. Rather than looking at these activities as extra work (which they partially are), they are also unique learning opportunities because students expand their rhetorical awareness of context and of concepts and phenomena that are and aren’t translatable.

*Collaborative Strategies*: In collaborating with others, translanguaging was also described a useful resource. When a student doesn’t understand a teacher or text, they will lean on others to make sense of it. Students may use a language other than English to explain something, or they may mix English and another language to help a classmate understand. This sometimes happens in conversations before or after class or via apps or social media, or it may happen in brief conversations during a lecture or during group work. This collaboration, which mirrors what students do outside the classroom, is an immensely valuable resource for learning. However, this type of collaboration is often surreptitious because students worry the teacher will call them out or penalize them. Sujin said: “Uh, so teachers are, make ‘you have to use English in the class,’ but sometimes we talk Korean with Korean friends [whispers - enacts it], like just some very little voice.”

# When Language Difference Is Not Valued

As noted, a concerning, yet unsurprising, finding is that linguistic difference is often seen as a barrier to overcome rather than an asset in writing classrooms. While many participants discussed the benefits of multilingualism as increased access to information, relationships, job opportunities, and more, they also believed that their first language or languages “get in the way,” as Hyun said, of mastering an accent-free and error-free English. Furthermore, when we discussed their linguistic resources as more than just knowing (an) additional language(s) but also the ability to cross borders and engage in complex communication activities, my participants generally didn’t think of this as assets.

On the one hand, it makes sense that participants who grew up in multilingual environments simply take their border-crossing activities for granted because these activities are ordinary and ubiquitous. On the other hand, I think there is something more pernicious at work in my participants’ negative or bland outlook on their resources. In US writing classrooms (and in most other classrooms), a monolingual approach to language is common. In a monolingual approach, there is room for one language (i.e. English). If we look at monolingualism as ideology, that is, a set of beliefs about language, this ideology tends to promote mastery of languages, with the goal of developing native-like fluency of the standardized variation of, e.g. American English (Lippi-Green). When looking at language learning (including writing) through this lens, it is inevitable to think of proficiency as a scale. On the one end is mastery and native-like fluency and, on the other end, is low-level, beginner, not proficient---labels that are all too common when students are placed in courses or evaluated. When this ideology meets translanguaging and other language diversity, these practices are often cast, not a resource, but as incorrect language in need of remediation.

I think we need to challenge this approach because it can cause you to feel that you need to monitor your language and curb your instinct (or need) to translanguage. The participants in my study overwhelmingly had negative perceptions of their English. Regardless of their backgrounds, most of them described their accent, grammar, or writing as “bad,” and I suspect they feel this way because they have heard others tell them this in the classroom and elsewhere. In “What Color Is My Voice? Academic Writing and the Myth of Standard English,” An Ha uses the concept of “writing scars” to describe past traumas stemming from messages that “[you] were no good at writing and that [your] language was not deemed academic” (72). The metaphor evokes the lingering pain or discomfort as well as the internalization of the message. Feelings of inadequacy, then, can cause shyness, embarrassment, or fear of speaking out in the classroom.

Another assumption we need to challenge is that academic writing needs to be composed in the Euro-American rhetorical tradition. Those of you who grew up outside a Euro-American context bring linguistic diversity, but also other lived experiences and knowledges about engaging an audience, making sense, persuading others, constructing a memorable idea etc. These resources should be cultivated, not dismissed or ignored.

## Halimah’s Story

> Discussing her writing, Halimah not only highlighted making a lot of grammatical errors in her writing, but she also pointed to the “wrongness” of her entire approach to writing: “I have a problem in organizing my writing, so I was writing everything, like if I am writing in Arabic, I organize everything like in Arabic, but it doesn’t work here, yeah. So I start to understand there is a problem. And then in [ESL]101 after I realize the problem, I start to change, and it becomes better.” Through teacher feedback, Halimah realized that the way she usually made meaning was no longer accepted, and to improve, her writing needed to mirror a Euro-American style.

Rather than requiring Halimah to write her paper according to a very specific model, Halimah might have been encouraged to analyze her writing, organization, and style and to consider how different audiences would experience her writing. This could enhance her rhetorical attunement and dispel the feelings of wrongness.

# Re-Imagining the Classroom as a Transnational Space

It is important to stress that the intention behind prescriptive approaches in the writing classroom is good. When I used to teach this way, my intention was good. The reasoning is that mastery of native-like English leads to good grades and, later, good jobs. And it makes sense because powerful institutions and the people who lead them promote and privilege this type of language. This is also why it makes sense that many of you desire mastery. As discussed, however, the approach raises concerns about learning, harm to students, and fairness. In the following section, I propose some ideas for what translingual classrooms should do:

# Inviting and Supporting Translanguaging Learning Strategies

Classrooms should offer opportunities for you to draw from all your resources to learn. You may prefer taking lecture notes entirely in one language and translating them into another language when you review; you may prefer taking notes in a mixture of languages and signs; you may like to discuss difficult concepts in a language other than English to better understand and contextualize concepts, ideas, and phenomena; you may have a translation app or search engine open during class to look up words or see visual representations; you may like to read a book or article in English first and then in your dominant language to fully understand; you may prefer the opposite; you may read about events, ideas, or phenomena in a language other than English to develop a basic understanding; when writing, you may write your paper in one language first and then translate it into English, either independently or using translation technologies; you may write in English and use words or phrases from other languages if you need to express something that can be said by using non-English words or phrases; you may pre-write by drawing. These are just samples of the myriad learning strategies multilingual students use, and by experimenting with them and discussing pros and cons, you can decide what works for you in different learning contexts.

# Creating a Space for Students’ Linguistic Resources

Classrooms should invite you to think about how you use language and other means of communication in different contexts of your life. They should ask you to reflect on why you use certain languages and not others in certain contexts; why you speak in one way to some audiences and in another way to other audiences; or how the different physical and digital spaces you are in may signal to you subtly or directly what is expected and accepted in terms of language use. You don’t have to be transnational to benefit from this exercise because we all use language in different ways. But I think if you record all your movements across borders, you will become more aware of all the things you already do with language and how you can hone your skills to achieve your goals.

## Paloma’s Story

> For a film project, Paloma decided to do an ethnographic project interviewing residents on the city’s South-side, a majority Latinx community. She wanted to learn about the impact of the city’s segregation and disparate opportunities in Latinx communities. In her film, she interviewed residents in English, Spanish, and Spanglish as needed, naturally deciding how to use language depending on whom she was interviewing and what the topic was. Though Paloma otherwise had a negative perception of her “unsophisticated” English, she realized that her linguistic resources allowed her to do meaningful, ethical research that contributed valuable academic knowledge because of her “unique language background,” as she put it.

By drawing from her linguistic resources, Paloma shifted her perception of “my English is bad” to “I have some really cool skills that allow me to do things other students can’t do.” I think we need to scale these experiences and adopt them into all our classrooms. Again, language is a huge part of our identity, and it connects us to our family and communities, so our language and other knowledges should be valued. It also invites more creativity in how we approach writing, research, and academic work, and it expands the possibilities for new information, perspectives, and knowledge.

# Supporting Critical Language Awareness

Classrooms should help you develop critical awareness of how language ideologies---and therefore norms and expectations for writing and communication---are tied to power and privilege. As linguist Rosina Lippi-Green notes, linguists have long established that standardized forms of language are not superior to other forms: speakers of non-standardized English (e.g. African American English) or those who translanguage have equal communicative competence and know how to get things done and make meaning. Standardized English is not inherently better suited for academic or professional language, so why does it have predominance in academic and professional writing? This is where the concepts of power and privilege are helpful. Power can be defined as the ability to influence or make decisions that impact other people. People close to power are leaders in education, science, research, publishing, media, entertainment, politics, law, business, and industry. People close to power tend to be White and middle- or upper-class, and and their proximity to power enables them to position their own language practices as the norm. As Lippi-Green explains, standardized English is a social construction based on people’s sense of etiquette, not empirical linguistic facts. The concept of privilege can be defined as unearned advantages in life that only some people have. When students from monolingual, White, middle-class backgrounds come to college, their use of English often closely resembles traditional academic English, and the cultural knowledge they possess is centered in the curriculum. This, then, creates unfairness because some students have better access to the language and literacy considered necessary for “successful” performance while others need to catch up and fill in the “gaps.”

## Ella’s Story

> Ella told me that when she was 11, she had to translate for her dad who was in the hospital because he did not speak English. She described how hard this was both emotionally but also in terms of finding the right words. When she returned to school the next day, she got a writing assignment back from her teacher, which was covered in red ink because of all the grammatical ‘errors’ she had made. Even at age 11, Ella understood the mismatch between expectations for her in the hospital and in the classroom, and she sensed the unfairness: on the one hand, the hospital should have provided professional translators, and, on the other hand, her teacher should have recognized her linguistic resources.

Unpacking the connections between language, identity, privilege, and power can help students like Ella understand their experiences with language. It can help you ask critical questions about why your language is valued or devalued in educational and public contexts, and how this could be different. It can also help you decide if you want to conform to others’ norms and expectations based on your own priorities and goals, or whether you want to resist these (Shapiro). It gives you a vocabulary and a set of ideas to help you resist. Even if your choice is to conform, this critical awareness can help you feel less like you are doing something wrong, or your language is “bad” or “broken” because you understand it is not. It can help heal your “writing scars.”

# Grading for Engagement with Language and Writing Choices

I argue teachers should stop grading for grammar and correctness in the traditional way. Research has demonstrated the unfairness of this approach (Inoue), while other research has demonstrated that you don’t become a better writer by getting a bunch of corrective feedback (Dunn). Instead, you might be rewarded for reflecting on your writing choices (e.g. grammar, word choice, and organization) by considering how various choices work on the intended audience based on things like time, place, or genre. While I am not dismissing that some of you may desire corrective feedback and learn from it as you work toward English proficiency, this feedback doesn’t need to be part of the grading rubric. Another aspect of this approach is that your choice to use Standardized American English is not dismissed, but it is no longer the assumed norm.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

Several “turns” in the field of writing studies have moved the field toward social justice and inclusion.[^tr-1] This includes shifts toward antiracism, linguistic justice, and translingualism. These arguments have facilitated a reckoning with traditional approaches to language and writing instruction, which center prescriptive pedagogies and standard language ideology. Sometimes, though, the conversations have overlooked the experiences of and implications for transnational, multilingual students whose experiences with language might differ from domestic students.

This essay seeks to address the experiences of transnational, multilingual students to better understand the experiences of this population and to foster awareness about language difference in and outside the classroom. The essay can be integrated into any undergraduate language or writing classroom that focuses on language and/or literacy. It will work well in a unit, in which students have already been introduced to basic sociolinguistic concepts and discussions about the connections between language, identity, and community as well as connections between language, privilege, and power. The first part of the essay, which reports on findings from a qualitative study with transnational, multilingual students, does not need any particular context; the latter part of the essay, which challenges classrooms that prioritize and privilege standardized language and Euro-American styles of writing, will resonate with readers who have some contextual knowledge.

In terms of the student body: it may be interesting for the class discussion if students have diverse backgrounds, but I don’t think it is necessary that the classroom enrolls a high number of transnational students. As sug-

gested, the study and my proposal for making classrooms a transnational space are also relevant for other students. Not only do we all have different experiences with language and navigating shifting rhetorical situations, but even “monolingual” students need to be prepared for communication in transnational spaces and grappling with their language attitudes and responses to language difference.

# Questions

1. On a piece of paper, visualize all the spaces in which you communi-

cate in your daily life. This could include home, school, work, and different communities. You can include physical and digital spaces.

Then, consider: how do you communicate and use language in each of these spaces? Do you notice differences in style or register? Do you use different dialects or languages? Do you use different slang, lingo, or any particular “insider language”? What does this “communication visual” tell you about yourself? Can you identify your own translin-

gual practices?

2. We all communicate in and across different contexts all the time; as

such, we might say that we cross different borders. These borders can be between home and school, between one community and another, or cultural borders, linguistic borders, national borders etc. Consider:

what helps you determine what changes you make to your language and communication style when you cross a border and enter a new space for communication? What helps you determine if you even need to make any changes?

3. The study found that participants describe most language and writing

classrooms they have experienced to be prescriptive and monolingual spaces, in which their language and writing are expected to target a standardized English, such as Standardized American English. Do the participants’ experiences align with or differ from your own experienc-

es? What are your experiences with language and writing instruction?

4. Do you feel that your linguistic and communicative resources have

been valued in different language and writing classrooms in your life?

The essay brings up the concept of “writing scars” as being traumatic experiences accumulating over time when teachers have identified a writer’s language as deficient and in need to improvement. Have you experienced “writing scars” and if so, how do they feel, and how do they impact your feelings about writing? 5. The essay argues that prescriptive and monolingual approaches to language are common, but also well-intended. Consider why the approaches continue to have dominance in the classroom. What are some advantages and disadvantages? Why? To what extent do you think teachers’ good intentions with teaching standardized English are appropriate? To what extent are they misguided? How can class-

rooms that emphasize standardized English still be inclusive and open to translanguaging?
