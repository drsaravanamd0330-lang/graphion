# Introduction: What is Literacy and What is a Literacy Crisis?

Reading and writing are essential tools for sharing information and experiences both in and out of school. The progress of a nation’s economy and culture depends on people who can share and store knowledge through writing and make sense of those writings through the process of reading (Brandt 166-167). For example, an engineer will not only have to write emails and memos but will also need to read and occasionally compose technical reports and other documentation. A doctor will have to read the latest research on conditions and treatments and take medical notes according to established medical codes. A lawyer must review casebooks and legal authorities as they prepare contracts and court documents. No wonder, then, that *literacy*---or the ability to read and write---is often a matter of national concern.

When a group of people seem to lack basic reading and writing skills, we have what is called a *literacy crisis*. As the quotes that began this chapter attest, people have complained about the state of student writing for a long time. The first quote comes from recent concerns that social media negatively impacts students’ ability to write and perform well on writing exams (maybe you’ve heard this one before). The next one comes from the 1970s, another instance of social panic over test scores. And the third quote, although it comes from a bit further back in time, reflects similar anxieties about the role of media on writing performance. Literacy crises often arise in discussions of testing and new communication technologies.

In many ways, the history of Writing Studies is a history of our perceptions of and responses to literacy crises that arise from changes in student demographics, economies, and technologies. Each time someone cries “we have a literacy crisis!” there is a heightened awareness of and concern for what writing teachers are doing in their classrooms. This chapter explores three related questions: Where does Writing Studies come from? What have writing scholars studied and why? What kinds of teaching practices come out of these eras? To explore these questions, this chapter presents three historical case studies of American literacy crises that highlight ways in which concern for literacy has shaped the field of Writing Studies---as well as how Writing Studies has begun to shape our own understanding of literacy crises. Through these case studies, you---as a student in a college writing classroom---can begin to recognize how our questions about writing are influenced by and respond to literacy crises.

# Case Study #1: *Why Johnny Can’t Read*

When the United States entered World War II, the Army experienced a surge in new recruits from a variety of backgrounds. To the surprise of the government, many applicants were initially turned away because they couldn’t pass the Army’s reading test (Varnum 153). Leaders were not only concerned that recruits were being turned away; they were also concerned whether recruits could follow basic instructions and avoid falling for enemy propaganda (Varnum 154). In response, the Army, and eventually the whole country, became very interested in basic literacy, developing programs such as the Army Specialized Training Program and V-12 Navy College Training Program to train officers and soldiers in communication skills (Crowley 92). The G.I. Bill, which provided veterans with tuition assistance, also led to greater numbers of students with diverse literacy backgrounds and new literacy needs and expectations.

As an example of what this growth looked like, consider that the student population at the University of Wisconsin Madison ballooned from 7,643 in 1945 to 15,475 in 1946 (Fleming 44). Can you imagine how hard it must have been to find student housing?! To support the literacy development of so many students, several organizations developed during this era, including the National Society for the Study of Communication (now the International Communication Association), the International Council for the Improvement of Reading Instruction (now the International Literacy Association), the Society of Technical Writers (now the Society for Technical Communication), and, importantly, the Conference on College Composition and Communication (CCCC), an outgrowth of the National Council of Teachers of English (NCTE).

The CCCC was an early champion of Writing Studies. The CCCC brought together public speaking, media, reading, and writing teachers who wanted to collaborate to develop more effective composition and communication courses that could support this new population of students. This organization provided a perfect place for collaboration (Gerber 12). For example, let’s look at a selection of panel and workshop reports from some of their early conferences listed in the journal *College Composition and Communication*. As we do so, note how the titles in Table 1 reflect the concerns of the postwar and early Cold War literacy crisis.

Several of these titles address issues of literacy, including grammar, General Semantics (e.g., propaganda analysis), reading, grading, testing, writing labs (early writing centers), and reading clinics (early reading centers). Other titles, such as the one about administration of these courses and “The Problem of Numbers,” address concerns with the growing student population. In later conferences, there are also increasingly more workshops focused on specific kinds of students, such as students who are testing lower and students for whom English is not their first language. There is also a turn to other fields such as linguistics as composition instructors sought “a theoretical foundation for the study and teaching of writing,” whether by turning to linguistics, to psychology, or to rhetoric (Nelms and Goggin 11). Some of the practices that come out of this era are still used to this day. For example, you may have had a writing teacher who has asked you to analyze advertisements and propaganda (perhaps using Laura Bolin Carroll’s *Writing Spaces* chapter “Backpacks vs. Briefcases: Steps toward Rhetorical Analysis”), or a writing teacher who encouraged you to go to the writing center (such as Ben Rafoth in “Why Visit Your Campus Writing Center?”). Other practices, such as televising lectures, have changed as educational technology has improved. This literacy crisis and the development of the CCCC jumpstarted Writing Studies as we know it. Understanding these connections can show us that the ways we’ve learned to write were not always that way; the ways we learn to write are influenced by larger changes in culture.

# Case Study #2: “Why Johnny Can’t Write”

From the inception of the CCCC in 1949 and onward, Writing Studies would continue to grow. The initial literacy crisis inspired by the G.I. Bill would reach a fever pitch in the fall of 1957, when the United States freaked out over the Russian satellite Sputnik. The Space Race was on! Almost overnight, the government passed the National Defense Education Act and began pouring money into research and development for all fields. Writing teachers and researchers through the 1960s continued to turn to linguistics, psychology, and rhetoric for their inspiration and prestige value, but also began to further develop their own research priorities (Nystrand, Greene, and Wiemelt).

As a result of the Civil Rights era and calls for greater educational access, many universities in the early 1970s shifted to an Open Admissions policy, which allowed students to enroll in colleges and universities without taking placement tests. In 1971, for example, the City University of New York (CUNY) expanded its educational opportunity programs to allow many more students to attend. For a sense of scale, consider the following stats: between 1969 and 1972, total CUNY freshmen increased from 19,959 to 38,256; Black students increased from 16,529 to 44,031; Puerto Rican students from 4,723 to 13,563; and White students from 106,523 to 125,804 (Steinberg). All in all, student enrollment at CUNY went from 174,000 in 1969 to 266,000 in 1975 (Varnum 157). The Open Admissions movement led to an incredibly larger and more diverse student body than CUNY or any similar institution had had before. Between 1960 and 1974, the overall percentage of American adults with four or more years of college doubled from 7.7% to 13.3% (Varnum 157). Open Admissions represented a significant shift in the intellectual life of the American public.

These new populations of students brought unique challenges not only in terms of scale but also in terms of language. The title of Mina P. Shaughnessy’s 1977 book *Errors and Expectations* sums up that challenge: there was a clear mismatch between how these students communicated and what their writing teachers expected. Many teachers got caught up in the need to correct what they perceived to be grammatical errors. A famous piece in *Newsweek* magazine titled “Why Johnny Can’t Write” shows how this was a national concern. Researchers, however, were already aware of these challenges and were proactive in addressing them. These writing researchers had already begun in the 1960s to have conversations about the value of multiple dialects of English and the ways in which different dialects were not a sign of cognitive impairment but rather of human ingenuity. The same educational opportunity programs that led to Open Admissions at CUNY had also conducted rigorous research to develop policies and teaching materials that would support differently prepared students (Wible 446). Their best-known document, “Students’ Right to Their Own Language,” outlined an ahead-of-its-time invitation to welcome different dialects of English rather than unfairly insist on a single standard.

Writing researchers didn’t stop there---they also continued to develop their own concerns about writing. Rather than focus solely on grammatical correctness, they wanted to better understand how writing comes to be. Their investigations led to what we now call the “Process Revolution,” a shift in thinking about writing as the end result (the “product”) to how that writing came to be (the “process”). You might think of it as the difference between being graded only on your grammar vs. being graded on your ideas that you’ve gathered and shared through your writing. This shift began as early as 1961, when Priscilla Tyler taught a course at Harvard about the writing process. Tyler’s student, Janet Emig, would go on to publish one of the most important studies of the writing process in 1971: *The Composing Process of Twelfth Graders.* With this growing interest in the writing process, the field of Writing Studies shifted even further from focusing only on the writing itself to also focusing on the person who writes. These shifts in scholarly attention show us how our understanding of writing shifts from just being a general problem of how to teach writing to the formation of a group of scholars who dedicate themselves to exploring where these questions might take us.

# Case Study #3: *A Nation at Risk*

In addition to changes in higher education, the United States suffered many other crises in the late 1970s. 1979, in particular, was a very hard year. The ongoing Iranian Revolution led to the 1979 Oil Crisis and the Iran Hostage Crisis, there was a nuclear meltdown at the Three Mile Island power plant outside of Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, and, right before Christmas, the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan. With the possible exception of Pink Floyd’s album *The Wall*, it was not a very good year. These larger concerns, coupled with continued unease with the state of education, would continue to fester into the 1980s in the form of the “Back-to-the-Basics” movement---a call to teach the “basics” of writing such as grammar, vocabulary, and spelling, or, in other words, to teach Standard Written English.

The Back-to-the-Basics movement, which started in the late 1970s, rose to prominence during the Reagan Administration. In 1983, the Reagan Administration released the report *A Nation at Risk: The Imperative for* *Educational Reform.* Among other things, this report focused on drops in SAT scores from 1963 to 1980. The report argued that “Nearly 40 percent [of 17-year-olds] cannot draw inferences from written material; only onefifth can write a persuasive essay” and that “The Department of the Navy... reported to the Commission that one-quarter of its recent recruits cannot read at the ninth grade level, the minimum needed simply to understand written safety instructions” (9). Sound familiar? Further, this report argues that literacy especially matters because “computers and computer-controlled equipment are penetrating every aspect of our lives---homes, factories, and offices” (10). Finally, the report recommends a return to the basics, including teaching students about “our literary heritage and how it enhances imagination and ethical understanding” (33). All in all, *A Nation* *at Risk* called for a return to teaching the basics of grammar and reading.

While some writing scholars did take up this call, many more argued that there was a better way. Writing scholars who relied on linguistics, for example, turned to sentence-combining, a teaching method that improved students’ reading and writing abilities by having them intentionally combine shorter sentences into longer sentences. Whereas this approach might have received more welcome in earlier decades, writing researchers were critical of such moves. In her 1981 article “Closing the Books on Alchemy,” Martha Kolln compares traditional grammar instruction to outdated chemistry. Michael Holzman, too, disparages sentence combining specifically in his 1983 article “Scientism and Sentence-Combining.” Patrick Hartwell’s 1985 “Grammar, Grammars, and the Teaching of Grammar” was the icing on the cake. This article compiled research that suggested that traditional grammar instruction (i.e., learning the parts of speech and diagramming sentences) did not improve students’ grammar at all. While there were perhaps more effective ways to teach grammar such as Kolln’s later book *Rhetorical Grammar*, writing scholars in the 1980s were more confident to question popular beliefs about how we should teach writing and argue for superior teaching methods.

A key ingredient in this episode was a growing awareness that fears of literacy crises were nothing new. Writing scholars began to more consistently turn to the deep history of writing instruction, where they found that every time there was increased access to education and/or new technologies there was popular concern about what to teach. Robert J. Connors, for example, published several important papers detailing where and when certain approaches to teaching writing arose. His 1985 article “Mechanical Correctness as a Focus in Composition Instruction,” for instance, shows that grammar instruction wasn’t just a concern in the 1980s; in fact, grammar instruction had been a concern at least since the 1840s and up through the creation of the college composition course at Harvard in the 1870s. Indeed, when writing teachers in the 1890s were faced with their own influx of new students, they created rubrics that were really just checklists of grammatical issues they could skim for without really engaging the content of students’ writing (67). Robin Varnum’s “From Crisis to Crisis” (cited above) also notes that literacy crises have happened multiple times before the 1980s. Harvey J. Graff’s *The Literacy Myth,* too, explores literacy crises in the 1800s and early 1900s. Grant M. Boswell’s 1986 article “The Rhetoric of Pedagogy: Changing Assumptions in Seventeenth-Century English Rhetorical Education” looked even further back to concerns about English boys having trouble learning Latin grammar in the 1600s. And, eventually, it became commonplace to look all the way back to the rise of Athenian democracy and the role of rhetorical education in citizenship (Graff and Duffy 37, 40-41). Armed with this deeper sense of the discipline, the field of Writing Studies could better set the research agenda rather than have an agenda handed to them by non-experts. These articles suggest how research in the beginning of the 1980s continued a focus on the writing process and ended with more engagement with historical concepts (like writing exercises, imitation, and digression) as well as direct responses to the cultural literacy expectations of critics Alan Bloom and E.D. Hirsch. We can also note further development of the field of Writing Studies, with more discussions of graduate programs that train future writing researchers and teachers. This case study can help us begin to recognize how knowing about Writing Studies can help us not only respond to literacy crises but also to more fully address the assumptions that drive these crises.

# Conclusion: Literacy Crises, Literacy Crises Everywhere

These three case studies present key episodes in the history of Writing Studies where there were public concerns about the state of reading and writing instruction. Often, these literacy crises coincided with new student populations who did not communicate in expected ways. While in earlier episodes, Writing Studies faculty mostly accepted the charge to go back to the basics, we can also see that researchers began to recognize that there was more at play than grammatical mistakes or writing ability. Rather, their research suggested that these literacy crises might instead be crises of social expectations---an entirely different kind of crisis (Babb; Graff; Trimbur; Williams).

Through these case studies, we also get a sense of what studying the history of Writing Studies can offer us. From studying this history, we can learn more about where Writing Studies has been and, more importantly, where it is going; we can learn more about the ways we have been taught to write and place ourselves in a better position to assess whether these approaches accurately represent our current understanding and needs. We can also learn how to analyze the assignments and activities we encounter in our own writing courses, whether we’re being asked to compose in different genres, to do research, or to analyze---each of these teaching methods has a history that either confirms its use or puts it into question. And, ultimately, to know our history is to know who we are and what we stand for.

# Teacher Resources

# Teaching Rationale

The field of Writing Studies is blessed with many histories of the discipline.[^tr-1] We have histories that outline and critique major trends in the field as well as histories that focus on specific institutions and individuals in the history of writing instruction. The typical writing course, however, will rightly focus more on providing students with opportunities to engage in the threshold concepts of Writing Studies and highlight key terms for transfer. How can the history of the field fit into that effort?

Elizabeth Wardle and Linda Adler-Kassner’s *(Re)Considering What We* *Know: Learning Thresholds in Writing, Composition, Rhetoric, and Literacy* identifies “Literacy Is a Sociohistoric Phenomenon with the Potential to Liberate or Oppress” as another key threshold concept. This threshold concept involves more than recognizing that literacy matters; rather, it also involves developing a historical understanding of our literacy tools, be they conceptual heuristics like a research paper or a technology such as AI. Just as a technical writer can benefit from understanding where a genre comes from so that they can better critique and improve that genre, so too can a writing student benefit from learning that the way we write and learn to write has a history. A goal for introducing the history of Writing Studies, then, is to provide students with opportunities for getting a sense of that history.

## Option A: Exploring the Case Studies

To help learners get a sense of how demographics, technology, and literacy intersect, consider the following questions when reading this chapter or as part of a group activity:

1. How did each of these literacy crises start? Who raised concerns about literacy and why?

2. What role do changes in technology play in these literacy crises?

3. In what ways did writing scholars draw on previous research to develop their response to each literacy crisis?

4. How did writing scholars respond differently to the literacy crises in each of these episodes? Why do you think they responded in these ways?

5. What sort of literacy crises are we facing today? How should we adapt our approaches to teaching writing to meet these needs? How might Writing Studies already have some answers to help us make sense of these changes?

## Option B: Researching the History of a Teaching Tool

As a longer assignment, learners could be invited to write reports exploring the history of specific concepts in Writing Studies. Below is an assignment description for such a report:

# History of a Teaching Tool

I. Why This Assignment? The purpose of this assignment is to help you gain a sense of how a teaching tool has changed over time. This historical awareness will also help you as you critique your own literacy experiences and analyze rhetorical situations.

II. What Do I Need to Do? This assignment consists of two parts: 1) a report and 2) a reflective memo.

A. *Report*. Write a 750-word report in which you briefly explore the history of one of the following concepts:

> i. Five-paragraph essays (also known as “five-paragraph themes”) ii. Paragraphs iii. Rubrics and grading iv. Personal writing assignments v. Handbooks vi. Citation Systems vii. Composition Textbooks viii. (Other concepts appropriate to the course, such as specific genres or concepts in technical writing)

For your concept, identify and cite *at least three* relevant scholarly sources using MLA formatting conventions. As you read these sources, consider the following:

- When did this concept emerge? What elements of a literacy crisis may have been at play at that point in history?
- How is this concept similar or different from how we think about it today?
- How has this concept been critiqued?
- In what ways is this concept still relevant to us today, and in what ways might we move beyond it?

You can organize your report around these questions, or use the following template to guide you:

1. Definition of the concept

2. Original context of the concept

3. How the concept has been critiqued and changed over time

4. How we use this concept today

B. *Reflective Memo*. Write a 6-8 sentence memo in which you reflect on what you learned from this assignment. As you compose your memo, consider the following:

- What was your process for researching and composing this report?
- What is something you learned from this report that surprised you? Why?
- What was the hardest part of composing this report? Why?
- Why is it important to understand the history of a concept rather than just accept the concept at face value?

III. How Will I Be Evaluated? Your report will be evaluated based on the following criteria:

## Report

- Focus. The writer presents a single writing concept, providing key definitions and historical context. The writer also compares historical with contemporary descriptions of their concept.
- Development. The writer provides a reasonable amount of detail in their description of the concept. The writer supports historical claims with appropriate evidence and reasoning.
- Source Use. The writer draws on at least three sources to demonstrate and support their knowledge of the concept. All summaries, paraphrases, and/or quotes are effectively contextualized, introduced, and integrated into the report.
- Delivery. The writer formats citations using MLA format conventions. Paragraphing, font, and other textual design choices are accessible to readers. There are no noticeable typographical errors.

## Reflective Memo

- Focus. The writer has a clear sense of purpose as evidenced by the presence of clear openings and closings and connections between sections. The writer examines their research and writing process, showing what they learned, how they learned it, and how this new knowledge connects to what they already know. The writer also provides excellent focus on each point, anticipating reader expectations.
- Supporting Evidence. The writer includes detailed examples and descriptions from their research and composing process. It is clear how each example ties back to the central focus of the memo.
