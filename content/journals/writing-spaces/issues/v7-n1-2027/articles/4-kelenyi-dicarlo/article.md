# Introduction

How many times has a writing instructor asked you to *imagine a* *particular audience* for a writing assignment? Perhaps you were given an audience to imagine or you got to choose your own. Sometimes called audience awareness, the role of *knowing* one’s audience (or at least pretending to) in building writerly ability is well-documented and often discussed in writing classrooms: as you write, you learn how to

“conjure” your audience. That is, you craft and adapt your language to help your readers understand how *you* understand the needs and expectations of the audience *you* imagined for the piece. Your ability to do this grows through experience with addressing similar audiences---through trial and error---and/or through feedback you’ve received from a member of your audience while writing (Ede & Lunsford).

For example, right now, I (Prof. Kelenyi) am imagining trying to explain audience awareness to undergraduates in my classes. As I write this, we’re in week three of the semester, so we’re still getting to know one another. I might ask my students to imagine how their on-the-spot writing about what they read for class that day would be different if they were writing it to a kindergartener, a grandparent, or a professor in a different department. Imagining in the extremes helps me make my point that audience matters. It can also help students identify a middle ground for what their audience may or may not know … sometimes.

I say sometimes because how many times have you received feedback on your writing that indicated you missed an important detail about your audience? Perhaps you misjudged their previous knowledge, vocabulary level, or what they might want (or need) to know. It can be frustrating and sometimes overwhelming to receive feedback asking you to revise your writing in consideration of some imagined audience’s interests, preferences, and needs, when what really happened is you imagined your audience differently from the instructor or peer providing you with feedback. Why can’t *your* imagined audience be the “correct” or at least the “good enough” imagined audience?

The long and short of strong audience awareness is encapsulated in the adage *two heads are better than one*. For example, while I drafted this introduction, my undergraduate co-author Aidan read through it and provided feedback in order to help it better reach the intended audience of *Writing* *Spaces*---college students from diverse backgrounds and contexts. A strong connection to one’s audience awareness makes writing better. So, feedback that helps writers reach a broader, more diverse audience is important because it can help them strengthen their ability to write for multiple audiences in various academic, professional, and community contexts.

In professional and community-engaged writing, the difference between a correct and misinformed understanding of the audience is particularly important since it can impact who has equitable access to what information (Chenowith). Intentionally thinking about how to relate to your audience can help you write with care for power dynamics and the accessibility of your piece (Esposito). In knowing your audience for professional and community-engaged writing, you can competently value and elevate community members’ knowledge and expertise (Iglesias).

But all of this requires *you* to do all the imagining, or at the very least, a lot of research. What would change if you were writing and receiving feedback from *authentic* audiences---as in, IRL[^2]? In the community-engaged writing class Aidan writes about below, we think a lot about how to build meaningful relationships with community partner organizations as we work on projects for their intended audiences. Each organization with which the course partners has a distinct audience they’re trying to reach, so it is important for student teams to spend a significant amount of time getting to know their community organizations and that organization’s patrons. We begin with a lot of research and a lot of listening in order to help ensure the writing we produce for our community partners is accurate, helpful, and effective. In this essay, we explain how “community listening,” or listening across differences (Jackson & DeLaune; Rowan & Cavallaro), is a strategy that can be used to get to know authentic audiences better *and* improve your ability to accurately imagine audiences for professional and community-engaged writing. We believe that applying this community engagement best practice to writing can help foster a positive attitude toward audience awareness and feedback on your writing.

# Community Listening

Community listening is “a literate act that engages listeners as collaborators in meaning making across multiple sites” where “listeners work together with storytellers to construct and sustain cultural knowledge by building storied connections across difference” (Jackson & DeLaune 41). This means understanding listening as a collaborative act: we all engage in listening practices in order to better understand how our message did or did not come across, and we respond accordingly by moving on or restating something differently or using a new example. That is, if how we respond to one another indicates a *difference* in understanding, we build on what’s been said to clarify and develop connections between those differences, thereby making those differences smaller. I like to think about it as listening *across* differences rather than *to* differences. We’re trying to find ways to bridge gaps.

By acknowledging and even leveraging the different intersectional positions that listeners and speakers come from, everyone can arrive at a fuller, deeper understanding of the topic at hand. However, in community-engaged listening, it is important to prioritize a community’s values rather than outside narratives (Rowan & Cavallaro). This helps avoid making unfounded assumptions and avoid bias in our writing because we “revise our understandings of our communities and our work as we learn more” (Rowan & Cavallaro 34).

Community listening can help us remain open to feedback, and help us receive it generously and with enthusiasm, because it helps us see how our writing can be more effective as a result. There are “discursive, material, political, economic, and educational dynamics that shape” our community partners and their relationships with their patrons; our writing must be a collaborative endeavor in order to better address all of those dynamics (Rowan & Cavallaro 34). In short, it’s quite hard to compose really effective writing alone. Community listening is a strategy for collecting and productively incorporating feedback on our writing because it helps us continuously raise our audience awareness, as Aidan’s story below seeks to demonstrate.

Aidan’s narrative is organized around the iterative phases of community engagement: entering the community, working with the community, ethically withdrawing from the work with the community, and reflecting on the work with the community (Flower). She shows how community listening, and therefore enhanced audience awareness, can be achieved by engaging with these community engagement phases.

# Community Listening in Practice: Aidan’s Story

In Spring 2023, I took a writing and community engagement class. It was a whole new experience in English Studies for me. Throughout the semester, I worked in a small group to alter client-facing materials for a local food bank to be more accessible to communities with low literacy, or low ability to read and use food bank materials such as fliers.

Engaging with a community outside of the college as part of this project allowed me to see firsthand how feedback can inform community-engaged projects and truly allow stakeholders’ voices to be heard in an equitable, inclusive manner. Specifically, below I describe how my group and I engaged in community listening while visiting the food bank and how what we heard influenced the information we left the food bank with at the end of the project; I also describe how my group went out of our way to seek feedback directly from patrons and incorporated that feedback to help our project be more effective. The community listening we practiced throughout the various phases of our community-engaged project, as described below, helped our project reflect both sets of stakeholders---the food bank and its patrons.

Writing for and with an authentic community audience has significantly changed the way I think about feedback and even how I conceptualize audiences when writing: I am more involved in the conversation about what needs to be revised, edited, or added and why. This relationship that I have forged with feedback allows me to minimize the gaps in understanding between myself as the writer and my audience, which requires considerate, generous, and equitable listening to all stakeholders involved. I have also honed the skill of being more open minded about what a given audience may or may not need. Prior to this project, I mostly wrote with my professor or my boss in mind; now, I write with a more diverse audience in mind---one that may not have all of the information my professor or boss does. I feel that I can conceptualize audiences better since completing this class, allowing my writing to be stronger and more personally tailored to the demands of the work.

The concept of community listening from Jackson and DeLaune and Rowan and Cavallaro is crucial when working closely with a community partner and is largely responsible for the shift in my orientation to feedback on my writing. Building an effective relationship with a community requires you to listen to stakeholders’ backstories and all of the valuable information that they have to share regarding their unique personal experiences. Actively listening and interacting with a partner or a whole community helps forge a reciprocal relationship built on empathy and genuine connection.

After reading the articles on community listening by Jackson and De-Laune and Rowan and Cavallaro for class, I was excited to put community listening into practice with the food bank in order to build an authentic relationship based on trust, empathy, and an open mind. I didn’t know that it would also change how I engage with feedback and conceptualize audiences for writing beyond this class, but I reflect on that below, too. By sharing my story, I hope to help you see how community listening can expand how you think about audience and feedback in writing.

# Entering the Community

Early in the semester, my group and I visited our community partner, the local food bank, to meet with the marketing personnel and volunteers as well as tour the facility. This experience helped us understand their mission in more depth. First, we took a tour of the 65,000-square-foot space, which helped us put the scope and importance of the food bank’s work into perspective. Then, we spoke face-to-face with the marketing staff about their previous experiences designing fliers for food pantries and other services to better understand their end goal for our partnership.

During this meeting we learned that previous tests on the food bank’s marketing materials revealed that community members with low literacy were having trouble understanding what steps they needed to take in order to get the help they needed. For older individuals, the text on the fliers was too small, for speakers of other languages, some of the more advanced words were confusing, and for individuals with visual impairments, the colors were too bright. Furthermore, the marketing personnel shared that some of the pictures on the fliers were arbitrary and confused community members who couldn’t read. For example, on one flier that asked viewers to contact the food bank to learn more about how to receive donated food, there was an image of a child eating a strawberry. While the image served aesthetic purposes, it did not help community members with low literacy understand how to engage with the food bank’s services.

When we met with our community partner at the food bank, we *entered* their community both literally and figuratively. And we engaged in community listening throughout our visit by treating it as entering into a conversation that had already started. We listened attentively to the voices already in the room, paying attention to our own reactions to what was said, developing our own thoughts and contributions to share, and choosing an appropriate point to join in the conversation. With writing, we follow a similar process by determining what’s already being said about a topic (e.g. performing research), noticing how what’s already being said aligns with or diverges from our own ideas, opinions, and/or experiences, using that to frame what we’re going to write, and then writing in the genre, tone, style, etc. that matches the conversation we want to join.

The entire visit helped us think about how we could make the materials for the food bank more personal to their mission, and listening to their marketing staff and volunteers reflect on their previous materials helped inform our initial ideas for revising their fliers to better serve the food bank’s patrons. The authentic conversations we had with patrons on the ground at the food bank helped us clarify both *what* we would be writing, for *whom*, and helped us personally connect to the overall importance of our project.

Even without an audience, however, community listening at the beginning of a writing endeavor (that is to say, when *entering* a writing situation) is still valuable. For example, as a marketing intern during the summer after taking this course, I was not given a specific audience to write and create materials for, nor did I interact directly with potential clients. However, I found I could better envision an audience by conducting research on the products and services I was advertising and by entering into an online community of people already using the same or similar products and services. Listening to (or reading) the conversations already taking place about these products and services helped me understand which features to highlight for which audiences---how to tailor my marketing for various potential consumers. Community listening can happen with authentic audience interaction or via research on the topic at hand, providing valuable insight into the writing you are completing and the audience it’s for.

# Working with the Community

The practice of community listening ultimately solidified my group’s decision to test the food bank materials we created in order to gauge their effectiveness. So, with just a few weeks left in the semester, we visited a local food pantry (that was served by our community partner, the food bank). We devised a list of questions to ask community members that would help us gather authentic feedback on our revised fliers.

In a different class, for a different project, I’m not sure my group and I would’ve taken this extra step, but our relationship with the food bank and our belief in their mission made it so that we were deeply invested in the viability of our materials. And what we were learning about community engagement and community listening inspired us to think about how we could best work *with* the community we were serving via the food bank. In other words, while we had already been exchanging emails and meeting virtually with representatives from the food bank since our in-person visit, we knew that they weren’t the actual audience for our marketing materials. Local food pantry patrons with low literacy were.

When we work *with* a community, we listen carefully to responses to our contributions, which influence our responses in turn; we honor the expertise and experiences in the room, including the differences those might highlight; we collaborate on ideas, solutions, and/or strategies that reflect and accommodate those differences of expertise and experience. This meant that the only way we could be sure our materials would work for our audience was to test them in the field. It was cool that the structure of the class allowed for this type of field work because it proved to be an indispensable aspect of our process: we gained valuable insight and feedback that profoundly impacted our materials, prompting us to further revise our fliers.

Specifically, a community member told me that they preferred yellow coloring over green, as the yellow provided better contrast, making reading the words on the page easier. Unanimously, the community members I spoke with preferred the same flier out of the three versions that I had created. Another community member mentioned that government-issued phones could not scan QR codes, making the ones we had included on the fliers useless. It would be better to write out the URLs. Lastly, another community member suggested providing phone *and* email options for food bank services, as speaking to a stranger on the phone about their personal situation could be daunting and might hinder individuals from reaching out for the help they need. Our final draft fliers were deeply informed by the feedback from the working class and elderly community members’ personal experiences, barriers, knowledge, preferences, and profound insights. Listening across differences helped us turn their experiences and feedback into effective changes for each of the three fliers we tested. In turn, this helped the food bank reach more patrons with important information about their services.

While this step in our project involved working in person with real patrons, this step can also be valuable to projects where an authentic audience is unavailable. As writers, we benefit from listening to understand how readers are interpreting our writing, and knowing that even if that interpretation doesn’t match our intention, we revise our writing with those different potential interpretations in mind. We can tailor our writing to leverage or avoid those interpretations once we know about them. In doing so, our writing becomes better suited to our audience because it is more clearly achieving our intended purpose for our intended audience.

Similar to how we worked *with* the community by field testing our fliers at the local food pantry, I’ve sometimes field tested some of my ideas for papers on social media, in group texts with peers, and with peer writing consultants at school. Listening to how others react to my ideas---the parts they agree with and the objections they pose---offers insight into the different perspectives I might be overlooking, which might point to flaws in my reasoning, assumptions I’m making, or related issues I’m forgetting. When I can test my ideas with someone else in the class I’m writing for, working *with* my community of thinkers helps me develop a final product that reflects or anticipates the voices of that community.

# Ethically Withdrawing from the Community

Our semester-long project culminated in a final presentation to our class and our community partner during the last week of the semester that outlined all of the steps we took to complete our project as well as a summary of our research into writing best practices for people with low literacy. As mentioned above, during our initial site visit, we learned about the difficulties the food bank was having with their marketing materials. This information correlated directly to the subsequent research we conducted on accessible writing for communities with low literacy.

With the help of one of the college’s research librarians, we acquired technical and professional writing sources that were beneficial to both us and our community partner’s goals to reach more of their patrons. The resources we found helped us understand how to employ technical writing techniques in marketing materials and offered various strategies for creating print materials for readers with low literacy. Specifically, our research recommended simplifying the font, enlarging the text, using shorter words and phrases, and including pictures that better depicted the actions that the food bank wanted the community to take to receive services. As mentioned in the previous section, we formulated multiple versions of the materials with different color schemes, font sizes, and images in order to best assess what worked and what did not. After we tested the fliers at the food pantry, we sorted and synthesized this research to develop a low literacy revision checklist that the food bank marketing staff could use for future marketing materials.

Conducting this research after the tour allowed us to tailor our best practices research directly to what the food bank stated they needed. Community listening not only helped us revise the client-facing materials for the food bank but also think about how leaving our community partner with our compiled research, our reference sheet, and examples could facilitate continuing the work that we had started without our involvement. And presenting our project’s process and deliverables to our class and community partner helped us bring it to a satisfactory close. By listening for next steps and outcomes of the project along the way and then acting upon what we heard in collaboration with our community partner, we were able to ethically withdraw from our work with them.

Finally, this phase in the community engagement process also allowed me to begin the process of reflection on the experience as a whole and how it changed my perspective academically, personally, and professionally. Now, beyond the class, I can see how we ethically withdraw from our audience in writing as well: we want to leave our readers with a clear understanding of what was accomplished in a piece of writing and how. This sometimes comes in the form of a traditional conclusion that recaps what the essay was about or it can come in the form of a call to action that encourages the reader to take a next step. Community listening has helped me become more aware of what kind of closing my audience expects, needs, or wants for a particular piece of writing. It helps me determine what’s appropriate and write in a way that is kind to my reader and honors the relationship I’ve forged with them in my paper (Fischer & Nobis).

# Reflecting on the Work with the Community

When I reflect on this experience, it becomes clear that interacting with an authentic audience allowed me to experience a type of feedback that was different from that of a professor or peer. As an undergraduate student, I’ve found it challenging to conduct projects such as this with only an imagined audience in mind. Not only must you fabricate your audience and materials, but with an imagined audience you will only receive feedback from your peers and professors based on the content of your written work, and not so much on ways that can improve the project itself in terms of usability or effectiveness for the community that it is *meant* to serve. In other words, your peers and your professor are imagining the audience, too, when they’re giving you feedback in these situations. With an authentic audience, like my community partner for this project, I felt like I could trust the feedback I was receiving more because I knew it would truly help make my writing more effective.

Furthermore, reflection on my work with the community helped me notice how daunting it can be to receive feedback from professors and peers in a strictly academic manner. When feedback is received from peers and professors, it can seem as if you have done something wrong because of the power dynamics at play with professors (they’re in charge of your grade) and the focus on grammar and mechanics with my peers (instead of what I wrote, the focus is on how I wrote it). In this case, however, since the feedback was provided to us from a stakeholder outside of academia and our class, it felt more exciting and empowering, as it made the work more tangible and significant.

Through the lens of community listening with an authentic audience and community partner, we were able to successfully seek out and implement meaningful feedback on our work to tailor it directly to the needs of the community. Furthermore, the feedback we received from the community was rooted in their real-life experiences, and it was actionable. It was not manufactured in the way that writing for a strictly academic audience may sometimes feel.

This experience of receiving feedback from our community partner and their patrons felt more authentic and useful for the writing and revising I will do after college. Being able to develop a more positive and empowering orientation to feedback on my writing as an undergraduate student has put me in a position to confidently enter professional spaces and make a true impact along my chosen career path in marketing. I learned that instead of viewing the audience through your own eyes, you can use the skill of community listening to meaningfully interact with your audiences---imagined or otherwise---in order to serve them best. I viewed the feedback I received through the eyes of my community audience for this project to make it more meaningful.

The step of reflecting on the work with the community allowed me to visualize the ways in which this experience changed how I now view all of my work and partnerships. It allowed me to see the profound transformation in how I visualize my audience when writing for other courses or internships, as well as how I receive and interact with feedback on my work. I haven’t had another opportunity to directly interact with an authentic audience for my writing beyond professors or peers since this class, except if you count writing this article.

Upon receiving feedback from the *Writing Spaces* editors about my narrative for this piece, I recognized the familiar distrust or hesitancy that I experience when receiving feedback from my peers and professors in classes. How do the editors really know what undergraduate students from diverse backgrounds and contexts really want or need, anyway? But then the community listening skills that I honed during Prof. Kelenyi’s class kicked in. Community listening encourages me to honor the experience and expertise of the editors in my revision and understand how my perspective as an undergraduate student isn’t the only one I’m writing for here. So, I’m confident that their feedback will help me reach my goal to share my experience with other students---both the ones like me and the ones different from me.

From this writing and community engagement class, I know we reflect on the work with the community by thinking about the process of community engagement and what went well and what could be improved, which involves listening to feedback. We do this so that we can improve the process for the next project and strengthen our relationship with community partners. In writing, we can reflect on how well we anticipate audience needs and desires from our writing and take that into consideration for our next writing endeavors. We find out for ourselves what sentence structures, organizations, transition words, etc. work best for which situations and add those to our writer’s toolkits. Reflection also helps us understand what didn’t work well in our writing for a particular audience, and why, so that we can try something different next time. As I practice that here, I hope I’ve listened well enough to have reached you.

# Conclusion

Aidan’s description of her team’s project above illustrates how community listening was an iterative process that informed their project from beginning to end. She demonstrates how she was able to use the practice of community listening as a way to focus on the resources of the food bank and its patrons by examining their cultural knowledge, skills, behaviors, and challenges and seeking to develop materials that targeted the interrelationship between them (Rowan & Cavallaro 34). Aidan’s group began by learning as much as they could about the food bank and its needs, even traveling to the physical space to develop a well-rounded understanding of the organization; then, they performed research on the food bank’s stated needs to complement what they had learned firsthand; finally, after developing initial drafts, they brought their marketing materials directly to patrons and prioritized their preferences and feedback to guide their revisions. Aidan and her peers were energized by the positive and constructive suggestions they received because they knew it would make their materials more useful and effective on a broader scale. This is in large part because of the positive orientation to feedback that community listening encourages.

You may be thinking that Aidan’s example of iteratively building audience awareness through the practice of community listening is unique because she and her team had authentic audiences to work with throughout the writing process. It’s true that not everyone can work directly with members of their audience as we do in the community-engaged course. However, as Aidan points out in her examples from beyond the class, online writing and research offer opportunities to practice community listening and engage with authentic feedback. The internet connects “people from various demographic backgrounds and locations and provid[es] numerous opportunities to compose in many genres with various degrees of formality” (Taylor 11). You may already be practicing and developing your audience awareness as you compose online product reviews, how-to articles, and blogs, not to mention social media posts.

To practice community listening while writing online, you would still begin with a lot of research and a lot of listening. For example, you might engage in research about the product, process, topics, etc. and its users, practitioners, followers, etc. in order to develop an initial understanding of the online rhetorical situation. You might come away from such research with a better understanding of the purpose of your product review, the context of your review (as in, on a large online marketplace site or on the manufacturer’s website; is this a new version of the product or a base-line model?), and the intended and unintended consumers. Aidan mentions doing something similar for her marketing internship.

As you learn more, and perhaps read other reviews on the same or similar sites of the same or similar products and responses to them, you are listening across differences between various stakeholders: consumers, manufacturers, competitors, etc. Such a practice not only helps you get to know authentic audiences for your review better, but also improves your ability to accurately imagine audiences for similar writing. When you publish your review, you could receive authentic feedback on your writing from other consumers (many review pages ask if particular reviews were helpful) and the manufacturer (businesses like to engage with their customers via review pages). This feedback gives you ways to edit and revise your review and/or make different authorial decisions for your next one. Aidan follows a similar process when she field-tests her ideas with peers online, via text, or during in-person peer writing consultations.

In conclusion, interacting with an authentic audience can expose us to feedback on our work that can feel more exciting and empowering than the sometimes-daunting feedback from peers and professors that can make us feel we’ve done something wrong (Grauman). This is because we can hear first-hand experiences from the individuals we are writing for/with that no one else would be able to relay to us. Such audience interactions allow us to incorporate meaningful feedback into our writing, and constitute the difference between imagined and authentic audiences.

For Aidan, asking folks who used local food pantries about the effectiveness of her team’s draft marketing materials provided valuable feedback that helped the group successfully tailor their final products to their intended audience. This experience writing for and with an authentic community audience significantly changed the way she thinks about feedback and even how she conceptualizes the audience when writing for other purposes.

We believe that viewing feedback through the lens of community listening can change the way you relate to feedback on your writing and conceptualize future audiences. Aidan’s experience illustrates how community listening can be a valuable skill to hone as an undergraduate writer as you prepare to transfer the writing skills you’ve acquired to professional roles. As she wrote above, being able to develop a more positive and empowering orientation to feedback on your writing as an undergraduate puts you in a position to confidently enter professional spaces.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

This essay is taught when faculty are discussing audience awareness and feedback with students, especially in community-engaged writing scenarios. It may be used to help students think critically about audience and how they relate to feedback from different audiences.[^tr-1] The essay demonstrates that even when authentic community audiences are unavailable to students, engaging in “community listening,” or listening across differences (Jackson & DeLaune; Rowan & Cavallaro), can help undergraduate writers orient themselves differently to feedback on their writing.

# Discussion Questions

1. Do you have a regular audience you imagine for your writing? How would you describe this audience? How did you learn to think about your audience when writing?

2. Are there other instances where community listening might be helpful or effective, such as for particular research projects or methods or in workplace settings? Explain.

3. What kind of authentic audience feedback do you receive about online writing? Do likes, comments, reposts, etc. count as authentic audience feedback? Why or why not?

4. Given the examples from this article, what strategies can students and instructors use to step back from having a primarily writer-focused approach to feedback and instead have a more reader-focused approach to feedback on their writing?

5. In what ways can readers ensure they provide feedback to writers in a way that fosters a focus on community listening and audience orientation rather than feedback that is rooted in standardized academic writing practices?
