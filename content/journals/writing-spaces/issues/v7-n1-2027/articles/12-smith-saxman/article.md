From perspectives on a topic to interactions in class, emotions shape writing experiences from beginning to end. This chapter begins with definitions of emotion and emotion in the writing process and provides examples of emotion in writing. What follows is a discussion of writing studies scholarship that traces and examines the role of emotion in writing. # Introduction

When you think about emotions in writing, what comes to mind? Poetry that moves you to tears? Stories that make you laugh or mourn the death of fictional characters? Celebration posts on social media or angry replies in comment sections? When you think about using emotions in persuasive writing, what descriptions come to mind? Irrational? Illogical? Biased? Maybe you have heard of using emotions in writing to persuade, oftentimes referred to as “pathos,” described as “appealing to the reader’s emotions” (Warrington et al. 191). This might look like using a photo of a sad-looking dog for an animal rescue adoption campaign or referencing an audience’s values or beliefs to motivate them into action for a particular purpose. Along with what you may have learned about pathos, you might have been told about the “danger” or “manipulation” that comes with using emotional appeals to make arguments. We are taught that emotions can be powerful tools for persuading an audience and that we ought to be careful when doing so because emotions are “subjective” and “irrational.” Because of this, oftentimes, discussions about the role of emotions in writing start and end there.

But consider all the ways emotions factor into your writing. If you go through a breakup, you might turn to journaling or texting a friend. In this way, emotion inspires you to write, either for yourself or to a friend to process how you are feeling. In what other ways have emotion moved you to write something? When you engage in writing now, whether on your own or in a class setting, you carry your past experiences with writing with you. If your past writing experiences have been negative, you might carry those feelings of frustration or anxiety into other writing classes. How have your prior experiences shaped your feelings about writing now? When we write, we cannot just leave our emotions and feelings at the door. From beginning to middle to end, emotions play a role in the writing process.

# Understanding Emotion

Before thinking about emotions when it comes to writing, it is helpful to start first with the concept of emotion. So, what are emotions? Emotions are cognitive (in your mind), corporeal (in your body), and social (in your interactions) responses to internal and external events. When you are thinking about all the homework you need to work on, that may make you experience anxiety or fear (cognitive). That fear and anxiety can then be experienced physically as a stomachache or headache (corporeal). A friend you speak with about your anxiety with your workload might offer support and understanding, making you feel calmer and more optimistic (social).

Sociologists Jeff Goodwin, James Jasper, and Francesca Polletta explain that “Emotions are part of the ‘stuff’ connecting human beings to each other and the world around them, like an unseen lens that colors all our thoughts, actions, perceptions, and judgements” (10). Our emotions shape what we view as “positive” and “negative” or “good” and “bad.” Emotions provide us with information about how we feel and respond to something. For example, if a friend jumps out and scares you, your initial reaction might be fear or excitement, which then you use to inform your response that might be anger (negative) at them for doing that or joviality (positive) at their action of surprising you. Educator Carola Steinberg explains how “Emotions provide information about our relationship with the situation that evokes the emotion” (43). Our emotional responses shape how we view the world around us. While emotions are often talked about apart from “rational” thought, emotions and thoughts (cognitions), emotions and the body (corporeal), and emotions and arguments (pathos) are intertwined. So, when it comes to writing, emotions play a role whether we acknowledge it or not.

# Emotions in the Writing Process

Writing Studies scholar Alice Brand describes the role of emotions in writing by explaining how “Emotions influence not only what we write and how we write, but how we view the process and how it shapes our thinking” (6). Other scholars also point to the role of emotion in writing. Interviewing six undergraduate and eleven graduate students, researchers Bruce Ballenger and Kelly Myers investigated the emotional work students engaged in during the process of revision in writing. They found that students experienced fear, vulnerability, and anxiety when tasked with revision. Some students found that when they cared about their topic, receiving feedback and making changes can feel vulnerable. The process of revision also made some students feel fearful. Going from the theory of crafting effective writing to the practice of actually doing the work can test your knowledge and ability. Other students felt motivated by the anxiety of “failing” to revise, while others coped with the negative emotions brought forth by revision by lowering their expectations of themselves. Revision is an emotional process, opening yourself up to critique and putting your “writing abilities” to the test. And while there are some negative emotions you can experience while writing, there are also positive emotions that can happen because of revising. Ballenger and Myers noted that students also demonstrated resiliency and coping mechanisms while doing the challenging emotional work that comes with revisions. These students conversed and collaborated with emotions like fear and shame to rewrite some of the negative self-talk that can happen during revision (e.g., “Why did you write it this way? How could you forget to add X?”). As students reflected on these emotions, they talked about what they could do in response to thoughts like “Why did you write it this way” and shifted to productive questions like “How can you further clarify this now?” This is to say that the emotional work that comes with revisions is not insurmountable and that you are both capable and up for the task. It can just take acknowledging those emotions to help you through the process.

Composition teacher Diann Baecker, in an article meant for other educators, talked specifically about the role of anger in writing. Backer explains that:

> We don’t often talk to our students about the chaos that comes with trying to communicate our thoughts to other people, nor do we talk about the emotions that attempting to work through that chaos creates, especially if that emotion is anger. (par. 3)

Let’s pause on the notion of the “chaos” of expressing ourselves through writing. Do you relate to that? The hours alone drafting, the conferences with teachers to receive feedback, the anxiety of sharing our writing in peer review, the uncertainty when we feel lost with how to approach a writing assignment. Writing often requires a lot of time and emotional investment when we care about our writing or grade. This is not to say that the hard work and passion you put into your writing are only negatives. Think about the feeling of the “Aha!” moment when you are stuck and make a breakthrough. Consider the confidence you feel when you finish a research paper and expand your knowledge of a topic you care about. Those can also be the wonderful parts about writing. It is just important to acknowledge the challenging feelings, like anger, that can come up when navigating the chaos. That is the writing process!

Writing Studies researchers Dana Lynn Driscoll and Roger Powell conducted a five-year longitudinal (repeated observations over a long period of time) study of 13 college writers’ emotional experiences with writing. Driscoll and Powell broke down three broad categories of emotions that can occur when writing: generative, disruptive, and circumstantial. Generative refers to emotions that have a positive impact on learning (like confidence and enjoyment). Think about someone who is a poet getting to include their poetry in a writing assignment. This can make them feel confident and make the learning in the writing assignment positive and, therefore, productive. Disruptive refers to emotions that have a negative impact on learning (like boredom, hate, and fear). With these emotions, think about when fear of receiving harsh critiques might have kept you from participating in peer review. But without the opportunity to hear feedback from other readers, you would not have the same chance to improve your writing, making that emotion of fear disruptive to your learning. Then there is circumstantial, which refers to either positive or negative emotions that impact learning (like frustration, anxiety, and confusion) and occur in response to a situation or action. For this one, you might think about confusion and how when you are first learning a new concept, you might experience a state of confusion. Then, after asking questions or engaging more with the concept, you come to understand it. In this case, the circumstantial emotion of confusion was positive because it pushed you to a state of understanding. Overall, Driscoll and Powell found that students’ emotions during the writing process had an impact on their learning development and transfer of learning (taking that learning and applying it in another context). They found, unsurprisingly, that generative and circumstantial emotions can be beneficial to student writers’ learning. From this research and scholarship, a view of emotions as an always-present dimension of writing starts to emerge. However, knowing that emotion is a dimension of writing and knowing what to do with that knowledge are two different things. So, how might we take this knowledge about emotion and apply it to understanding writing and the writing process better?

# Strategies and Approaches for Navigating Emotion in the Writing Process

In this section, I provide a few strategies and approaches for navigating emotion in the writing process. Keep in mind that these are not the *only* methods. As you engage with writing, consider what strategies and approaches to your emotions in writing you already use or think of as you read this chapter. As Driscoll and Powell noted in their research of college writers’ emotional experiences, student writers are resilient, innovative, and thoughtful. So, as you read the approaches, think about your own strategies and how those might connect and add to the ones below.

# DTR (Define the Relationship): You and Writing

One strategy for navigating emotions in the writing process is to take time and define your relationship with writing. All of us have some kind of history with writing that shapes our current approaches to it. That history is made up of experiences, prior knowledge, theories, and beliefs of writing. Emotion is weaved throughout each of those. How you felt in past writing experiences (anxious, relaxed, excited, bored), how you categorize certain writing (“good” or “bad”), the principles of writing you adhere to (“writing always includes X” or “writing should never do Y”), and the personal ideas of writing you hold (“I’m a bad writer” or “I know everything I need to know about writing”) are all informed by emotion. Emotion, as defined earlier, is that “unseen lens that colors all our thoughts, actions, perceptions, and judgements” (Goodwin et al., 10). Emotions are how we filter information and determine our relationships with the things around us.

Irene Crawford uses the term “affective stances” to describe how emotions form our understandings and provide a “vantage point from which to make meaning of the world around us” (678). We all have different points of view when it comes to writing. That difference is a strength in many ways. It allows you to engage with other writers and their different points of view and consider how their ideas might impact or shape your writing. Those differences can also be challenging as differing beliefs and approaches to writing can clash in the classroom. So, when engaging in writing, it can be useful to reflect on your beliefs about writing.

To open ourselves up to learning and growing as writers, defining what it is we believe and feel about writing can help us either build those beliefs and feelings or potentially change them. For example, based on your prior experiences, maybe you view the exercise of peer review as useless busy work. By defining and articulating that feeling about peer review, you then can create an opportunity to address that view of writing. Perhaps you express to your teacher that your past experiences with peer review have been where students “correct each other’s grammar” and give harsh critiques that feel subjective. This opens the conversation around peer review in this class and how it might differ from your past experiences. This is why I begin each class in which we do peer review with the question, “What are your experiences with peer review?” As a class, we spend time talking about our feelings toward peer review and how each person views its purpose in writing. Now, with those feelings out there, we can work together to form our class view of peer review (a collaborative exercise that allows writers to hear directly from readers) and directly address past negative feelings around it. It is challenging to grow as a writer if we do not work first to articulate our feelings and beliefs about writing.

Here are some brief exercises you might do to define your relationship with writing:

- DTR introduction: As if you were introducing a friend, create an introduction to your “friend” writing. When did you first meet? How has your relationship been over the years? What are your favorite things or least favorite things about your relationship? This can get you started on thinking more specifically about how you relate to writing.
- Create an analogy: Creating your own analogy can be a helpful exercise in thinking through your feelings toward a person, place, or thing. An analogy is a comparison between two things that provide clarification or explanation. For example, “Talking with you is as enjoyable as stepping on nails.” From this analogy, you can understand that conversing with this person is not a particularly pleasurable experience. The analogy helps explain how the person feels about talking with this other person. Let’s try it with writing. Take writing and finish the sentence “Writing is like…” or “My relationship with writing feels like…” Once you create the “thing” you use to describe writing or your relationship to it, add to it. Like, “Writing is like a roller coaster. There are a lot of ups and downs, but I generally enjoy the ride.” See how much detail you can add to your analogy and then reflect on it.

# Metacognitive Practices

A strategy for students navigating the “complex emotional landscape of writing in higher education” that Driscoll and Powell found in their study was something called “metacognitive practices” (par. 1). As I mentioned earlier, cognition deals with our thinking. *Metacognition* describes thinking about one’s thinking. For example, when you are walking, your mind and body communicate and work to put one foot in front of the other (cognition). Then, as you’re walking, your friend says, “Have you ever noticed how you walk?” Now, you’re walking, but you’re conscious of *how* you’re walking while you are doing it (metacognition). So, in writing and learning about writing, it is useful to think about how you are approaching writing before, during, and after you do it. In this way, metacognition can help you reflect on what approaches were helpful to you, what tasks you found challenging and why, and what new ways of thinking might be useful to you when approaching a similar writing task.

Specifically, when it comes to emotion, Driscoll and Powell highlight two sub concepts of metacognition, called monitoring and control, that can be helpful for students navigating the emotional dimension of writing. Monitoring, they describe, “occurs when students actively evaluate their efforts concerning a task” (par. 13). Control “occurs when action is taken as a direct result of that monitoring” (par. 13). Consider, for example, you have been assigned a research essay in your writing class. From prior experience and your reflection on your current feelings toward this task, you assess that assignments like these can make you anxious and stressed (monitoring). Because of your assessment based on your past and present feelings toward the assignment, you schedule a writing center appointment to brainstorm ideas and create an outline (control). When it comes to managing the emotional aspect of writing, these tools of monitoring and control can be helpful. So, for this strategy, next time you are faced with a writing task, you might try the following:

1. Monitor: Pause and evaluate the task and your past experiences with it. What emotions does it bring up? How do you feel about the writing task? You could use the following activities to support you in this: a Write it out: Jot down some notes about your immediate reaction to the assignment, how it makes you feel, what you are excited about, and what you might be nervous about. Read that back and reflect on what emotions emerge. b. Talk it out: Find a classmate and talk about the writing task. What are your feelings toward the assignments, and what have your past experiences been with assignments like this?

2. Control: Consider first what is in your control around your emotional experience at this moment. What action could you take to help manage the emotions that come up for you with this assignment? You might consider the following activities to support you in this: a. Use the writing center: If your institution has one, go to the writing center to talk aloud with someone else about the assignment. This can help you create a game plan which can help manage some of the feelings of being overwhelmed or anxious in having to begin a new writing assignment. b. Seek out feedback: Especially when anxiety and fear are at play, procrastination and writer’s block can occur. Be proactive in communication with your teacher. Seek out their expertise and advice for approaching the assignments.

# Emotions as Information

Another strategy you might consider for navigating emotions when writing is to view your emotions as valuable sources of information. Researcher Alice Brand describes how emotions can provide helpful “cues” about writing: “[Writers] should become familiar with the emotional as well as the intellectual cues that tell them they are ready to write, ready to stop, and ready to do a number of things in between” (441). That frustration and desire to throw your computer across the room might be an emotional cue telling you to take a break before trying again to write. That excitement and passion you feel when reading sources for a topic you are writing about might be an emotional cue telling you to keep going and get writing. The dread and fear you feel before peer review might be telling you that past experiences sharing work have been stressful, and you may need to do some metacognitive control. Ballenger and Myers even talk about how writers’ emotional responses to their writing can be used to point them to places where they want to revise their writing. One student who participated in their study employed a strategy where they reread their work for “cringe moments.” These moments describe sentences or ideas in their writing that felt wrong or weird, and so the student marked them for revision. This is an example of how emotions can be valuable sources of information to you in the writing process.

Emotions can also cue you into the writing challenges and strengths you have as a writer. For example, if completing a first draft of a writing task makes you anxious and avoidant, that might signal to you that the infamous “blank page and blinking cursor” is a challenge. With this information, you can then seek out other approaches for developing first drafts. If peer review is something that excites you because you enjoy providing feedback to your peers or you are interested in what readers have to say about your writing, this is helpful information that a strength in your writing process is collaboration and reader feedback. Maybe the next time you are feeling stuck on an assignment, you will seek out peers to review your writing and discuss ideas together. Considering your emotions as information can help you navigate the complex feelings that can come up in the writing process. Here are some questions you can ask to help you navigate your emotional experience while writing:

- What am I feeling? Can I name the emotions?
- Have I felt this way before? When did it happen last, and can I remember why?
- When I think about the writing stage I am in (brainstorming, drafting, revising, etc.), how have these stages made me feel in the past?
- In which parts of the writing process do I usually feel disruptive emotions? In what parts of the writing process do I feel generative emotions?
- What emotions can I tap into to support me through this writing task?
- How does this draft or portion of writing make me feel? Why do I think that is?

It is important to note that not all emotions fit neatly into one category, and not every emotion is a statement about your abilities or circumstance (e.g., “I feel hopeless; therefore, this assignment is hopeful.”). But, if we view our emotions generally as information, it can be useful to consider what we are responding to (the task, stage of writing, anxiety or excitement about writing, etc.) and, when we can, reflect on why we responded in that way (fear of bad grades, passion about a topic, etc.) and what that might be telling us.

# Conclusion

A last consideration for thinking about emotion and the writing process is less about what you do about your emotions and more about *making space* for your emotions. While navigating and managing your emotions during the writing process can be useful, making sure not to ignore or avoid your emotions can be just as important. Because writing, and learning to write, can create and tap into a lot of strong emotions, it can be easy to frame emotions as “bad” and in need of suppressing. A core argument of this chapter is not that we should add emotions to writing or that emotions should be minimized during the writing process, but that emotions are, whether we acknowledge it or not, a part of writing. Emotions inform, shape, and inspire our writing. It can look different for each person, but emotions are always there, giving us information about how to feel, how to respond, and how to act. As Baecker explains, “When we make room for emotion in the classroom…we give [writers] tools for negotiating the writing process” (par. 37).

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

This chapter introduces to students the idea that emotions are an ever-present dimension of their writing process.[^tr-1] As they learn and practice the above strategies for navigating their emotions in writing, teachers play an essential role in that process. Instructors set the tone for the emotional climate of the class and can model ways for students to navigate the emotional dimension of writing. This can happen through creating space for emotion in the design your writing assignments (including reflective writing that asks students to monitor their emotion) or engaging in class discussions about students’ prior experiences, knowledge, and emotion with a writing task or process (activating their prior experience and articulate their emotions through shared experiences and creating a class list of “what we know/have experienced with X writing task” to then revise and revisit throughout the lesson).

# Discussion Questions

Here are some discussion questions you might use along with this reading:

1. What part of the writing process tends to bring up the most positive or negative emotions for you?

2. Have you experienced the feelings of anger and chaos discussed in this chapter in your writing process? If so, how did you move through it?

3. How would you describe the way writing makes you feel? How might those feelings impact how you approach writing?

4. What strategies have you used to navigate your emotions during the writing process?

5. Throughout the essay, emotions are described as an “unseen lens that colors all our thoughts, actions, perceptions, and judgements” (Goodwin et al., 10). How have you observed or experienced emotions shaping thoughts, actions, perceptions, and judgments? What are some examples?
