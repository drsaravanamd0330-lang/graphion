# Introduction

In the first volume of *Writing Spaces*, Ben Rafoth, a former writing center director at Indiana University of Pennsylvania and a prominent writing center studies scholar, published an essay titled *Why Visit Your*

*Campus Writing Center*, in which he explained some benefits of visiting the writing center---tutors can “create idea-rich conversations” with students by playing multiple roles involving readers, navigators, and discussion partners (54). This claim should make sense to you if you have ever visited the campus writing center as a student. It is a misconception that the writing center is merely an editing service for your writing assignments; rather, it looks more like a conversation center, as talking is one of the core aspects of the writing center’s work (North 443--44). In the conversation, you were probably placed in a “negotiating group” where your tutor collaborated with you to identify and solve the problems in your writing (Lunsford 8). In this way, the tutor avoided behaving like an editor and instead worked as a peer to encourage the construction of voice and knowledge through conversation and discussion. In addition to the strong emphasis on talking, your tutor probably advised you to (re)visit the writing center at any point in your writing project because the writing center advocates a process-oriented approach. Writing tutors, for example, can support you from the initial brainstorming stage to the final refining stage. They can even read the assignment guidelines and professors’ comments together (Wilson-Carter 87). Of course, the writing center’s capability is not limited to course papers. In *Writing Center as Genre Microcosm*, Margaret Ervin (a writing center director at West Chester University), Gabrielle Stanley, and Olivia Mathers (both of whom were former tutor alumni from West Chester University) reported that students brought not only writing assignments, but also cover letters, resumes, and personal statements to apply for internships, scholarships, and graduate schools (Ervin et al. 16). The same report also noted that students were from various disciplines (e.g., humanities, health sciences, social sciences, education, etc.) (17). The writing center is not only a valuable resource for you as a student writer, but also beneficial for tutors who work there as well. We want to emphasize that there are a variety of benefits for you in becoming a writing center tutor (Figure 1). You will develop writing and tutoring strategies. You will be able to acquire some skills that can be helpful in your classes and also useful in your job hunting and career development. You may not become a teacher in the future, but some skills and knowledge gained in the writing center can also be helpful for a prospective teacher (most likely a teacher of writing). In this essay, we will go more in-depth on the variety of skills and benefits you can obtain through becoming a writing center tutor.

# Potential Benefits: Improving your Skills and Knowledge about Writing and Tutoring

A significant selling point for becoming a writing center tutor is the skills and knowledge you acquire and hone through undergoing tutor training. The benefit of training is the fact that it is institutionally-specific, as well as focuses on the ability to develop the skills of listening, problem-solving, communicate effectively, and broaden your ability to empathize and be patient (Hays 47; Saydam 1; Ryan and Earles 2). However, the formats of writing center tutor training and the development of tutors’ skills and knowledge come in many forms. Some of these forms include: (1) seminars and workshops, (2) mentor or partnership pairings between a new and experienced writing center tutor, (3) being assigned writing center scholarship to read, (4) weekly/monthly staff meetings, (5) group discussions and role-playing practice, (6) self-chosen writing center projects, (7) taking an online or in-person writing center course (general or genre-specific tutor training), and (8) attending a writing center conference (Boddy 22; Bell et al. 151; Louissaint 29; Malenke et al. 91; Jiang et al. 39; Firdaus 337-338). While the format of tutor training may vary from institution to institution, tutors should develop and obtain a common set of skills and knowledge.

# Impact of Various Tutor Training on Overall Tutor Performance

Through taking part in writing center training, you’ll begin to develop a toolkit of knowledge, expertise, and abilities that allow you to adequately tutor tutees within a consultation. When it comes to tutors taking part in seminars or being assigned writing center scholarship, it gives you an opportunity to reflect on your own tutoring practices (what’s working/not working), needs of the tutees, compare tutoring approaches and perspectives with other tutors in a large group setting, and discussing how to incorporate what you read/learned into real life tutoring practice (Boddy 23; Bell et al. 151; Govender and Ndadziyira 6). As a tutor, you’ll also begin to develop leadership qualities in the form of initiative taking through the writing center tutor training, such as providing personal input on major writing center decisions, presenting research or workshops at writing center conferences, classes, or meetings, and providing mentorship to new or less experienced tutors (Louissaint 29-33; Govender and Ndadziyira 7). Additionally, you’ll have the opportunity to develop and improve upon your communication skills. For example, tutors are often assigned to a course and work with students in discussing the steps to improve their writing, providing mentorship to new or less experienced tutors, and not being afraid to seek out help from others (more experienced tutors, director, staff, etc.) if they were unsure about something (Louissaint 32; Jiang et al. 39).

# Improvement or Development of Tutor’s Tutoring Abilities

One of the other benefits of becoming a writing center tutor is the variety of skills and knowledge that you obtain, develop, or improve upon while tutoring. For example, these skills and knowledge come in the form of (1) active listening skills, (2) translating or putting tutees’ thoughts and ideas into words, (3) developing the ability to adapt your tutoring approaches to a variety of learning styles (visual, verbal, kinesthetic, etc.), (4) helping tutees understand professors’ feedback on assignments, (5) developing a comfort with working with tutees from diverse backgrounds (e.g. tutees with disability, first-generation students, multilingual learners, etc.), (6) aiding tutees with developing their own writing process (pre-writing, reading aloud, organizing), (7) pointing out patterns or common writing errors and showing tutees how to self-correct these mistakes, and (8) facilitating your self-reflection on your own writing process and decisions made during a session by you playing the role of a trusted and critical friend, an individual who provides critiques, questions, and an outside point of view in a non-adversarial manner (Lundin et al. 15-16; Wilson-Carter 83; Gardener 9-10; Bell et al. 148; Carlse 192). In addition to obtaining such tutoring skills, you will likely have some opportunities to learn about effective use of generative AI in the writing center; for example, some writing center studies reported that tutors (1) use AI to understand major or field-specific language (that the tutors are not familiar with), (2) focus on global issues in tutees’ writing because AI can address local problems like grammar or syntax, (3) discuss with tutees about the ethical issues of using AI, etc (Mutsvairigwa et al. 538; Eleftheriou et al. 9-12). As these examples show, tutoring skills are what you can improve or develop either through tutor training or through your own experiences tutoring at the writing center.

# Improvement of Tutor’s Own Writing Performance

The writing skills and knowledge that tutors develop as part of working in the writing center not only benefit the tutees they work with, but the tutors themselves when it comes to their own writing performance. Through reviewing previous writing center studies, we have found that tutors have developed an increased sense of self-confidence in their own writing abilities due to (1) utilizing the same writing resources and suggestions they offer to students for themselves, (2) identifying common errors in a tutee’s writing that they themselves now watch out for in their own writing, (3) understanding the value of the process of writing (prewriting, drafting, editing), and (4) developing a deeper level of patience by recognizing that writing is not a one-size-fits-all process (Louissaint 50; Gardener 8-19). Tutors also stated that their own interest in writing was increased through conversations with their tutees. For instance, during a tutoring session, some tutees pose the question of “why” a grammatical rule is the way it is. This kind of question increases tutors’ own curiosity and interest in grammar and the skill of writing (Gardener 16). Lastly, through working with tutees from different majors, you’ll be exposed to different genres and modes of writing. When working with tutees, you’ll encounter writing that you were never exposed to, felt was worthwhile, or were fascinated by due to the unique characteristics attributed to a style of writing. Through these experiences, like the tutors in Gardener’s study, you’ll become more receptive and willing to try new genres and approaches of writing and be more self-aware of the choices you and tutees have as writers to convey meaning in the manner they see fit (17-18).

# Improvement or Development of Tutor’s Personal Abilities

Working in the writing center provides writing center tutors an opportunity to develop and refine a variety of personalized skills not just related to writing. Tutors in Louissaint’s *Undergraduate Perception of Peer Leadership in the Writing Center* have highlighted the positive, nurturing, and supportive nature of the writing center, arguing that it provides an ideal environment for developing the ability to be able to make mistakes while learning from them, as well as the ability to ask questions to faculty or tutors if you don’t know something without fear of judgement (37-38). The relationships built with faculty, staff, and peers also provides a personal benefit for you as a tutor. Writing tutors in previous studies have said that these relationships were positively impacted through (1) receiving helpful feedback, (2) learning to be better leaders either through working with peers or being mentored, and (3) upholding a high level of professionalism and performance within the writing center (tutoring ability, professionalism, timeliness, involvement in tutor training) due to the workplace expectations set forth by the faculty and staff (Bell et al. 147-150; Louissaint 62). You, as a tutor, can also develop the ability to learn in a non-classroom environment. For example, when working on a peer tutor project, a tutor in Bell et al.’s *Peer Tutoring as a High-Impact Practice: Understanding Writing Center Tutors as Learners* highlighted how the assignment “felt higher stakes than just doing a project in a class where you’re turning it in for a grade, because I’m developing something that’s going to be used later on” and have a long-term impact (151). By becoming a peer tutor, you can be involved in personal projects through which you can learn in alternative and effective ways. The opportunity to choose the direction and topics allows you to take part in meaningful and practical projects for the improvement of the writing center and for the students who use this resource.

# Potential Benefits for Job Application and Career Development

By becoming a writing center tutor, you’ll also develop job-related skills that can help you in your future career (Alamri 72-75; DeFeo and Caparas 154-155; Dinitz and Kiedasch 4). As writing center researchers have highlighted, one example of this can be seen through the interpersonal communication skills tutors developed within the writing center and transferred to situations within their future career or during the job application process. Due to the one-on-one setting of tutoring, tutors mentioned how they were able to (1) improve their semi-formal conversation skills, (2) analyze problems and compose replies to questions quickly, (3) provide balanced feedback, (4) develop social-emotional skills (compassion, patience, confidence), and (5) create a welcoming atmosphere for the tutee by being more personable (Alamri 70-72; Bradley et al. 33-34; Dinitz and Kiedaisch 4). Additionally, due to working with a diverse array of students, you’ll develop skills such as active listening, utilizing critical thinking skills as a way to adapt to different learning styles, and a level of comfortability in communicating with international students, students with disabilities, and students of varying majors (Alamri 70). The following list[^2] briefly summarizes the transferable skills.

A. Interpersonal Skills

> a. Tutors can develop the skill of listening attentively and communicating effectively to help a variety of students (e.g., students with disabilities, international students) achieve goals. b. Tutors can develop the skill of empathy in communication that comes in the form of thinking from someone else’s perspective and understanding where writers are coming from. c. Tutors can develop the skill of being welcoming and personable to make students feel comfortable. d. Tutors can develop balanced writing feedback strategies (positive/constructive feedback).

B. Familiarity with Job Application Genre

> a. Tutors can develop writing skills advantageous to job

> hunting (e.g., resumes, cover letters, and application essays). b. Tutors can seek out careers related to writing and editing (e.g., newspapers or magazines, publishing houses, businesses, professional organizations, radio, television, and film). c. Tutors can identify attractive aspects of a writing center tutoring job and look for similar future careers that utilize similar skills. d. Tutors can have tutoring experience cemented one’s desire to put writing at the center of their professional ambitions. e. Tutors can gain a first taste or a first experience into teaching.

C. Mentoring and Feedback Skills

> a. Tutors can develop the skill of patience when working with students who didn’t enjoy writing, had different learning style, or views on writing. b. Tutors can develop ability to diversify lessons to meet each learner’s needs. c. Tutors can develop ability to build confidence in others. d. Tutors can develop ability to coach and guide students (not show/tell). e. Tutors can have an ability to seek out and accept criticism.

D. Stress Management

> a. Tutors can feel it is okay to say “no,” draw tutor-writer (professional) boundaries, emotional boundaries, and opt for a specific tutoring style (directive vs. minimalist) in and beyond writing center sessions for the sake of one’s mental health/self-care. b. Tutors can establish boundary-setting in the form of taking a short break mid-session to center and compose themselves. c. Tutors can seek out and pursue professional counseling services or student counseling services on campus. d. Tutors can debrief the nature of the trauma-based tutoring session informally with someone (e.g., spouse/partner, colleague, or manager).

E. Management and Collaboration Skills

> a. Tutors can develop skill of effectively interacting and collaborating with others in academic setting. b. Tutors can develop skill of managing consultation appointments, making consultations more accessible (disabilities/special needs students), and identifying student needs. c. Tutors can tailor appointment needs to that of a specific individual(s). d. Tutors can develop the skill of working collaboratively with others to find solutions, achieve goals, and seek out second opinions to any issues. e. Tutors can be familiar with determining purpose, genre, and intended audience during the revision process of my own or other writers.

The knowledge and skills that you hone within the writing center can also help you develop a familiarity with the genre of job application materials. Through working on writing documents with tutees such as cover letters, resumes, and application essays, you’ll develop a familiarity and ability to effectively write your own job application materials (Dinitz and Kiedaisch 4). Additionally, tutors in previous research studies further developed their active listening skills, as well as improved their self-confidence when it came to their calmness and conversation skills during a job interview through their tutoring experiences (Bradley et al. 35; Dinitz and Kiedaisch 4). In Dinitz and Kiedaisch’s study titled *Tutoring Writing as Career Development*, 73% of their writing center tutor alumni who attended their research agreed that the skills and experiences developed within the writing center played a role in their future decision for graduate school or career, with one participant stating, “I loved my work at the writing center and sought to duplicate parts of it in any new position” (4). Many tutors highlighted how through their tutoring experience, it helped them to identify aspects of their tutoring position that they found attractive (e.g., writing and editing) and sought them out in their future careers related to writing, editing, or education (publishing, newspapers, film) and it also gave tutors a first taste of teaching (Bradley et al. 31-34; Dinitz and Kiedaisch 4).

# Mentoring and Feedback Skills in General

The connection between a tutor’s job-related and career development skills and the skills and knowledge developed during their time as a writing center tutor is also reflected through their mentoring and feedback skills. As a tutor, you’ll make strategic decisions to utilize the skill of patience or vary your tutoring approaches and lessons proved useful when working with tutees who may not have enjoyed writing or had varying learning styles (DeFeo and Caparas 156). In Alamri’s study, he emphasized that tutors developed skills advantageous for being a mentor, which came in the form of (1) instilling confidence within a tutee during their tutoring session, (2) developing the ability to coach and guide a tutee versus telling/ showing them, and (3) expanding their own socio-behavioral skills (e.g., welcoming, empowering, engaging, etc.) (73). Moreover, through working in the writing center, former tutors found that they had an increased desire to seek out and receive constructive criticism as a way to continually self-improve (Bradley et al. 24-25).

# Ability to Manage and Collaborate with Others

The ability to manage and collaborate with others are what can be transferable from a tutor’s experience as a writing center tutor to their future career. Alamri, as well as DeFeo and Caparas both highlighted the importance of collaboration and working effectively with others as a skill for former tutors, with Alamri highlighting this point within the context of a writing center when talking about the importance of “approaching the director, staff members or other consultants to find solutions or second opinions for any issues that they might need to work collaboratively to solve” (76). In their future careers, tutors may find that working collaboratively with others is beneficial for completing tasks, getting second opinions, obtaining solutions for issues, as well as minimizing issues. Former tutors in Alamri’s and Bradley et al.’s studies also found that the skills they developed were easily transferable for managerial tasks such as (1) determining the intended audience and purpose of their own and others’ writing, (2) identifying student needs, (3) making consultations accessible to all (e.g., individuals with disabilities or special needs), and (4) adjusting appointments to the needs of the individual (Alamri 75; Bradley et al. 31-32).

# Possible Application of Writing Center Experience: Stress Management

Working with tutees can be stressful; however, as a tutor, you’ll develop approaches to manage your stress, which can be beneficial in your future career. Although the studies we reviewed do not specifically mention stress management as a transferable skill, we believe that it is a valuable and transferable asset that writing center tutors gain through their work experience. For example, from previous studies, we’ve learned that tutors (1) set expectation guidelines or rules for the session, (2) clearly define their (tutor’s) responsibilities, and (3) set not just professional boundaries, but emotional ones as well (as a matter of self-care) by having the ability to say “No” to certain requests or taking short breaks to compose oneself during particularly stressful tutoring sessions (Im et al. 219; Parsons 27; Hargreaves and Jaber 27). Another popular approach utilized by tutors to de-stress from difficult sessions that can be advantageous to them in their future career includes talking to others about the source of their stress, whether that be professional or student counseling services on campus (Hargreaves and Jaber 28) or individuals close to them such as a spouse/partner, colleague, or manager (Im et al. 219; Hargreaves and Jaber 28). In our own personal experiences as writing center tutors, the strategies introduced here are possibly applicable in other contexts outside of the writing center.

# Potential Benefits for Future Career as Writing Teachers

The knowledge and skills (or training experiences) gained at the writing center are also beneficial for those planning to become writing teachers. Benefits include tutors’ firsthand opportunities to observe and interact with real students (Broder 38; Clark 347) and to experiment and witness the connections between composition theories and pedagogies (Alsup et al. 334). Additionally, tutors can gain some knowledge and skills transferable to an actual classroom setting. In particular, we want to introduce three categories of these: (1) teaching preparation in general, (2) teaching strategies in the classroom, and (3) attitude toward students and their writing.

# Teaching Preparation in General

First, writing tutors can face different assignments and diverse students with various backgrounds, which can help the tutors be prepared for their teaching jobs. Every day, students visit the writing center seeking some writing tutorial. Those students bring different kinds of writing assignments; as a result, you, as a tutor, can observe differences in writing assignments, professors’ expectations, grading criteria among courses and departments, etc. Additionally, you’ll have opportunities to work with some non-traditional students or nonnative speakers of English at the writing center. Working with students from academically and socioculturally diverse backgrounds can help you (as a prospective teacher) have practical opportunities to (1) work with real students, (2) observe students’ process of writing, and (3) see diverse learning situations, writing assignments, and grading norms, all of which can eventually help you foresee your future teaching situation (e.g., Alsup et al. 332; Broder 38, 42; Clark 347, 350; Harris 197, 200, 201; Zelenak et al. 29-30).

# Teaching Strategies in the Classroom

Second, tutors can use their tutoring strategies in a real writing education context. A good example should be a process approach in writing education. Following recent composition theories, you will most likely focus on the process over the finished product as a writing teacher, even though you, as a prospective teacher, may not have many chances to observe students’ actual writing process. By tutoring students, you can witness what is happening when struggling students are working on their writing projects. Also, by directly observing “when and why writers get stuck and can’t proceed to work on a draft that is not yet successful” (Harris 197), you’ll become more empathetic toward students (Cogie 80; Zelenak et al. 29-30).

# Attitude toward Students and their Writing

Finally, as prospective writing teachers, tutors can become more empathetic and understanding toward students’ writing and their individual differences through tutoring experiences. Zelenak and colleagues, for example, reported that “[r]eferences to improved empathy for and understanding of students was, in fact, a key thread that unified many respondents’ answers” (30). Direct observation and seeing real students will help you enhance your awareness of students’ diversity, which in turn will help you increase your sense of empathy toward students and their writing processes.

# A Suggestion to You as a Prospective Writing Center Tutor

By reading this essay, we hope that you noticed two things: (1) you can improve your writing skills and tutoring skills through the writing center training and your own tutoring experience, and (2) your skills, knowledge, and/or experiences gained in the writing center can be applicable in various academic and/or professional contexts. In particular, we wanted to emphasize that you do not need to be a perfect writer or tutor from the beginning. We ourselves were former writing center tutors and agree that only through tutor training and firsthand tutoring experience did we develop our skills and knowledge as tutors. However, we want to reiterate that we did not start out with these abilities and expertise; we all had to start somewhere. The major takeaway from our essay is that the writing center will help you improve and/or develop your writing skills and knowledge in the shortterm to benefit you as a tutor and in the long-term to benefit you in your future career.

# Discussion Questions

1. Think about all the good mentors you have had throughout your life (e.g., coaches, teachers/professors, parents, etc.).[^tr-1] What qualities and characteristics made them effective mentors?

2. Let’s imagine you’ve been tasked with the responsibility of helping classmates or friends on their writing assignments. What strengths or skills do you believe you have that would be beneficial for not only assisting your classmates or students to complete the task?

3. After reading this essay, what skills or knowledge do you feel you would benefit from further writing center training to make you a better writer or a better tutor?

4. This discussion question can be optional for those who are in the English education program. What are some examples within your life that you can think of where you’ve had to mentor, coach, and guide individuals to complete a task?
