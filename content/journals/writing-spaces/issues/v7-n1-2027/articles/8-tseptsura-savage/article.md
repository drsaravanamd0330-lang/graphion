# Introduction

How do writers get better at what they do? If you ask professional writers, one piece of advice you will hear most often is to seek and use feedback. However, when it comes to receiving feedback from their classmates, most students have mixed feelings. We anticipated mixed responses when, in the Fall of 2023, our Online Writing Program (which Mariya directs and Isadora was teaching in) surveyed students who were using a new digital peer review tool[^a1]. What we did not anticipate was learning how many of these mixed or negative responses seemed to be grounded in common but mistaken beliefs. We found ourselves wishing we could have a conversation with our anonymous student respondents in order to dispel some of the problematic ideas they held, especially in terms of what their instructor’s role in peer review should be and what roles they themselves play as writers and reviewers. This chapter is our way of having that conversation with students (and your instructors) who might feel frustrated, disappointed, or uncertain about peer reviews.

We have selected five common ideas about peer review that have persisted among students and instructors alike. In what follows, we examine each of these ideas and suggest alternative ways of thinking about them. All these ideas are interconnected; in fact, you will probably notice how two of them in particular lay the foundation for the other three. Each section opens with direct quotes from our student survey participants that exemplify each idea.

We believe that peer review can be a much more productive and enjoyable process for everyone involved if we revise or retire these widely spread but flawed ideas, This chapter follows some of the recent calls in the writing studies field to reconsider the purpose of peer review, and we hope that it can spark fruitful conversations between our student and instructor readers.

# Misconception #1: “I’m not qualified to give feedback on my peers’ writing.”

> “I don’t feel like I should really be the one giving feedback and [instead] the instructor should be the one reviewing someone’s work.”

> “I would hate to provide feedback, but it could be the wrong feedback for my peers, and they receive a poor grade.”

Have you ever doubted your own writing skills or felt like you might not be the best writer in your class? The students who shared the comments you see above certainly have. You might not feel entirely confident in your grammar skills, or you might worry that you don’t fully understand the assignment you are working on. You might also assume that because you don’t actually grade your peers’ papers, your opinion doesn’t matter. Whatever the root of these doubts may be, many of us have felt like we were not qualified to give feedback on our peers’ writing. When you don’t feel confident as a writer, you might avoid sharing any suggestions altogether. This approach, in turn, leads to dissatisfied writers who feel like you did not put enough effort into your review.

This lack of confidence is the result of a common assumption that a reviewer should be an authority on writing and the English language. It is true that when academic writers submit their work for peer review, they get feedback from other researchers who are knowledgeable in their field. However, you do not need to have a PhD in English or become a *New York* *Times* best-selling author to be a good reviewer for your classmates. Your role is simply to share your thoughts as a real reader---nothing more and nothing less. You might be asked to review your peers’ draft based on a set of criteria such as content or organization, but it is still not your job to judge their draft as if you were their teacher. As a peer reviewer, your job is to notice and share your response to your peers’ writing, both positive and critical.

You might be wondering what such a response might look like. First, as you read your peers’ drafts, notice your reactions: what ideas or passages do you find intriguing, or what keeps your interest? What strikes you as important or especially relevant? Where do you have to stop and re-read a passage because it is difficult to follow, and what makes it difficult? It might help to read the draft twice: read first just to grasp its main ideas, and then read it again to take more detailed notes on what you notice about it. Mike Bunn, a professor of writing at the University of Southern California, in his *Writing Spaces* chapter on reading also suggests paying attention to the choices the writer made: would you make the same choices in terms of the writer’s stance on the topic, their opening or closing moves, their tone and choice of words or organization pattern? You are perfectly qualified to share your reactions as a reader as long as you put honest effort into reading your peers’ drafts attentively. And if you are not sure how to translate your thoughts into productive feedback, we highly recommend writing scholar Ron DePeter’s *Writing Spaces* chapter on crafting positive and helpful peer review comments. To be a good reviewer, try to be as detailed and specific in your feedback as possible; the most common complaint our student respondents voiced was getting short, vague comments that did not provide enough detail or depth.

# Misconception #2: “All feedback is either correct or incorrect”

> “I am liking the peer reviews, but what is missing in the editing and evolution of our drafts is the *right* answers.”

Have you ever wondered if the feedback you received on your draft was “right” or “correct”? For many students, the “right” answers are connected to a grade, or how the instructor judges their writing. For other students, the “right” answers may be a matter of grammatical correctness and formatting. If you have ever been in a graded class with rigid rubrics and expectations, you might be convinced that there is one correct way of writing.

This black and white thinking may make us feel there is a certain, quantifiable, and reliable way to assess anyone’s writing. However, there are very few moments in writing where “right” or “wrong” comes into play. This is because correct and incorrect categories in writing can only be applied to certain---though not all---aspects like mechanics, formatting, and some grammatical issues (e.g., APA and MLA guidelines, capitalization, or verb tense formation). Language is not a constant, unalterable system; it is always changing and evolving, and grammar rules change with it. What was considered a grave mistake 50 years ago is now common and accepted practice; as an example, think about rules like avoiding split infinitives (e.g., “to quietly sing” instead of “to sing quietly”) at all costs or using “he/ she” instead of singular “they.” Some choices that appear to break grammatical rules might also be stylistic in nature, a way to establish the author’s voice, or a common practice in a specific genre.

But even if you can identify some black and white rules that should be followed for a specific writing task, peer review is not about finding those formal, surface-level errors. In fact, we completely agree with one of our student respondents who said, rather begrudgingly, that peer review “does not help with grammatical errors.” Unless you are an expert editor, you probably should not focus on mechanics and grammar when reviewing a peer’s writing. If anything, most AI-powered tools would give you more reliable feedback on your punctuation and formatting. What these AI tools can’t provide you is an actual human reader’s reaction to your writing, and that’s why peer review is so useful.

Peer review is about formulating and sharing your reaction as a reader. There might be bad ways to *share* your reaction (e.g., leaving mean-spirited comments), but there is no such thing as a “wrong” or “incorrect” *reaction*. Individual readers will interpret texts differently based on their tastes, preferences, and past experiences. Second language writing scholars Robert Kohls and Christine Pearson Casanave dedicated a whole book to trying to formulate what “good writing” is from the linguistic point of view, and multiple studies have found that the same student paper can receive vastly different grades from different instructors. Even well-established authors get negative feedback on their writing; in her chapter on peer review, writing scholar Erin E. Kelly recalls anecdotes from widely published colleagues who received starkly negative reviewer comments; e.g., one reviewer simply said, “I don’t think the author can write” (304). Were those reviewers incorrect? Sharing mean comments is definitely not a helpful move. However, the fact that even well-established writers habitually receive negative feedback shows that there is always a wide range of reactions to any given piece of writing. The “reviewers #2” in Kelly’s examples approached their review task with a different set of ideas and expectations that did not match with what the writer was trying to do.

When you receive negative feedback, your task is to distinguish which comments or suggestions align with your own vision for your paper and which ones you disagree with and why. When giving feedback, we encourage you to focus on the “big picture” aspects of writing which cannot be reduced to right and wrong. Worrying about giving correct feedback will only create a paralyzing effect where you don’t know what to say. Instead of thinking you must share “correct” feedback, try to come up with feedback that describes your reaction to the draft with as much accuracy, honesty, and kindness as possible.

# Misconception #3: “I can’t trust my peers’ feedback”

> “So far, it’s nice to get feedback, but sometimes it’s hard to trust feedback from peers.”

If you have ever wondered if you could trust your peers to give the “correct” feedback, you are likely not alone. At a basic level, trust is about believing that someone is honest and truthful. As writing instructors with over 20 years of experience between us, we are surprised when students say, as one of our survey participants did, that instructors should “monitor the peer reviews just in case peers intentionally give bad feedback.” It is quite difficult for us to imagine students scheming in the shadows, waiting to deliver intentionally misleading feedback. We are sure such cases of pure malice are quite rare! However, another student voiced a different side of the trust issue when they said, “It’s difficult to trust that they [peer reviewers] know what they are talking about without being professors.” For this student, their difficulty in trusting peer feedback is rooted in the belief that only those with enough expertise are qualified to give feedback. In some ways, this mistrust is the flip side of the lack of confidence in your own abilities as a reviewer (see our Misconception #1). If you also believe that all feedback is either correct or incorrect (see Misconception #2), you might worry about getting “incorrect” advice. But what does “trust” actually mean in the context of peer review? And how can you trust your peers’ feedback?

The beauty of any writing class is in the wide spectrum of diverse experiences and mindsets that your peers bring. The value of peer review lies in getting multiple unique perspectives on your writing and getting to see different drafts by other students. When you receive feedback on your writing, you will likely not connect or agree with all the comments you get. This is normal! What’s important is that you can discern between more and less helpful feedback based on your own understanding of your writing task. Part of learning to become a better writer is learning to accept or reject feedback, as well as to articulate why you decide not to follow certain suggestions.

You should trust in the process of peer review: your peers are here to share their opinions of your writing according to the best of their understanding of your draft. You can almost certainly trust that they are trying to be honest and not giving you intentionally bad advice. But you do not need to use or agree with every comment you get; you might receive some suggestions that run contrary to what you envision as the main point of your draft, or your reviewers might have different preferences in terms of stylistic or design choices. You should also reflect on what it is about your draft that led them to have that response: were you not clear enough in stating your main claim? Or are your design choices perhaps too bold for some readers? As you read feedback, try to ask yourself why certain comments feel more helpful or trustworthy, and when you find yourself disagreeing with any particular comment, try to reflect on why the reviewer might have left it.

It is ok to disagree with your peers’ comments as long as you trust that their feedback is based on good intentions. When we trust in the peer review process, we can see how it helps us develop our critical eye, both as reviewers and receivers of feedback - a skill that we can carry with us into many facets of writing in any future profession.

# Misconception #4: “Instructors should monitor and check peer responses at all times”

> “I think the instructors’ role should be validating the information peers give to ensure we are not misguiding each other.”

> “I think the instructor should be a guide as well as a referee and arbitrator.”

You might think that because peer reviews are a common practice across most writing classes, your instructor should offer some guidance on how to improve your reviewing skills. You are not wrong! Most writing instructors would offer advice and guidance for how to read and respond to peers’ work. However, when it comes to reviewing and giving feedback on the actual comments you exchange with your peers, not many writing instructors do that. When Lynn Belcher, a writing scholar, surveyed writing instructors on whether or not they reviewed and graded peer feedback, over half of the respondents said no. These instructors cited different reasons, from not having enough time to purposefully staying out of what is supposed to be peer work. But the fact remains: most instructors practice a hands-off approach when it comes to peer comments. If peer reviews are conducted via hand-written comments on paper drafts or in-person small group discussions, it might be even more difficult for instructors to monitor peer feedback.

However, because the student respondents in our study were all coming from online writing classes, all of the peer review work they completed was always visible to their instructor. More and more in-person classes use digital tools to conduct peer review too, whether it’s through Google docs, your university’s online learning platform (such as Blackboard or Canvas), or through a dedicated tool like Eli Review or Turnitin. This increased visibility of peer comments seems to impact students’ views on how involved instructors should be in peer reviews: we were struck by how many of our student respondents viewed their instructors as a kind of online forum moderators who should review peer comments for accuracy and “correctness.”

Some students suggested that instructors “should supervise the peer reviews and randomly assess for quality control,” while others also added that instructors should be “validating the information peers give” during peer review. Remembering Misconception #2, you might see the problem with these attitudes: if peer feedback is supposed to be about sharing your reactions to your peers’ writing, how can it be “validated” or assessed for “accuracy”?

Consider the following comment that one of our study participants left on a peer’s draft: “The description of you going through defenders and striking the ball - I can just see the ball curve into the net. Beautiful description.” We have no doubt that the reviewer was genuinely impressed by the description. Can another reviewer disagree with this comment? Absolutely. A professional sports news writer might say that the description was too detailed or flowery, while a professional memoir writer might say that the description lacked emotional depth or sensory details. Your instructor might or might not find the description engaging, depending on their own preferences, background, and expectations for the assignment. Whether you receive praise or criticism, you should reflect on your reviewers’ feedback and consider where they might be coming from. If you are not sure how to interpret a peer’s comment, you can always check in with your peer or your instructor, but ultimately, it is up to you to be the judge of the feedback you get and how you want to use it.

# Misconception #5: “Peer review is only useful as long as it helps me get a better grade on my paper”

> “The point of the peer review is to improve the final product for the instructor to grade.”

> “[Peer review] has not really helped me at all in fixing my paper because the students aren’t as critical of the assignment as the professor.”

At the beginning of every semester, I (Mariya) ask my students to reflect on what they want to gain in our class. Inevitably, a few of them would say something along the lines of “earning an A.” At that point, I don’t ask my students what they’d like to get out of peer reviews, but I suspect many of them would say something like “a better grade on my final draft.” I can see why many students would think so, too: don’t we usually say that the goal of peer review is to “improve your paper”? One common way instructors try to help students improve each other’s writing is by using the grading rubric during peer review, hoping that they would be able to point out problematic areas or catch major issues that would affect their grade. However, this well-intentioned approach can create false expectations for students and instructors alike. Phoebe Jackson, a writing scholar, describes how we teachers often have similar expectations for our students: “Acting as a proxy to the teacher, we hope our students will create a similar model of response that might mirror what we had to say about a given paper, even though most of us have spent years responding to student papers. When students are unable to replicate this response model, we throw up our hands in despair and disparage the peer review process” (205). Using rubrics and prompts during peer review can be helpful in laying out a path toward improvement, but your peers will not evaluate your paper the same way your instructor would. Unlike your peers, your instructor has probably read hundreds or thousands of student papers, and their mindset is trained for reading through a more analytical lens. Studies published as far back as the 1980s demonstrated that “students and instructors use different criteria and stances in judging student work” (Newkirk 309). Moreover, even when students try to help a writer “fix” their writing, they tend to leave unsatisfactory comments on surface-level issues like grammar or mechanics. But if your peers’ feedback will rarely align with your instructor’s and cannot guarantee you a better grade, what is the point of peer review?

In recent years, there has been pushback among writing scholars against the “improvement imperative” of peer review (a term coined in 2020 by a UMass Boston professor of English Timothy Oleksiak). When researchers tried to examine what students actually gain from peer review, it turned out that students gain a lot more by *giving* feedback than by receiving it and using it to improve their papers. By providing feedback, students learn to read other’s writing with a critical eye, looking for weak points and coming up with ways to address them - these are all cognitive skills that improve your own abilities as a writer. In fact, when researchers compared writing skills improvement over time for students who gave but did not receive feedback VS those who did not engage in peer review at all, the first group showed stronger progress. In her overview of research on this topic, Phoebe Jackson concludes that “the real value of peer review is not in its outcomes, i.e., the comments to improve a peer’s essay, but *what students* *learn by engaging in the process of peer review itself*” (209).

When you engage in peer review, keep in mind that the goal is not to get foolproof suggestions for how to improve your paper. The goal of peer review is to practice how to understand and critically examine a peer’s project, how to formulate and share helpful feedback, and how to reflect on your own writing in light of what you learn from engaging with other people’s work.

# Concluding Thoughts

Despite being a common practice across English courses for over 50 years, peer review has frequently received mixed reviews (pun intended) from instructors and students alike. We believe that the common assumptions that we labeled in this chapter as “misconceptions” are to blame for many of the lukewarm or outright negative attitudes toward peer review. You might have noticed how two of these misconceptions in particular affect everything else: if you believe that feedback can either be correct or incorrect (Misconception #2), and if you think that peer review’s only purpose is in getting you a better grade on your paper (Misconception #5), then naturally you’d discard your peers’ opinion and not value your own. But if we continued that mindset to its logical conclusion, we would probably have to drop peer review from our pedagogical toolbox altogether; indeed, it might be your opinion that peer reviews are a waste of time. Yet dozens of our student participants were adamant that peer review helped them learn and grow as writers, regardless of what grade they ended up receiving on their paper. As we mentioned in our discussion of Misconception #5, research shows that the simple act of giving feedback on a peer’s draft improves your critical thinking, reading, and writing skills: you grow as a writer by reading and responding to other people’s work. Moreover, our student participants said they appreciated hearing someone else’s perspective on their ideas; they also noted that receiving positive, detailed, honest, and kind feedback helped them just as much as getting concrete suggestions for improvement.

We believe that peer review as a practice has great potential; however, to realize that potential, we need to reconsider what we see as its main purpose and how we evaluate peer feedback. We expect that not all of our readers will be fully on board with our argument; indeed, the “improvement imperative” has been ingrained in our work as writing teachers for such a long time that retiring it is no easy task. We hope that this chapter will serve as a conversation-starter to help us improve our understanding of peer review and our roles in it.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

This chapter was created in response to real student comments that we received from almost 400 participants in our research project.[^tr-1] We believe that the assumptions discussed in this chapter are fairly common and that students can benefit greatly from an open discussion about what peer review is (and is not) and what roles they should play in it. In fact, many of our student respondents mentioned that they appreciated having a conversation about peer review prior to engaging in their first peer review activity, and many also reported that prior to taking one of our classes, they had never had teachers explain to them what peer review was supposed to be about. This chapter can serve as a conversation opener to set some ground rules and expectations about peer review that all students in a given class agree to follow.

We also suggest that students can benefit from doing a “trial” peer review to test their understanding of the process and learn what helpful and less helpful feedback looks like and how to be a more helpful reviewer. We recommend that such a trial run be conducted via an online tool so that instructor and students have easier access to peer feedback (for more about online peer reviews, we recommend Part 4 of the excellent collection *Rethinking Peer Review: Critical Reflections on a Pedagogical Practice*, available online). Students can read this chapter either before or after the trial activity; the chapter can serve as a framework for having a conversation about their experience and for looking into students’ comments more closely. Some peer review tools allow for peer evaluation of feedback where the writers can rate their reviewers’ feedback; such a review can also be paired with a discussion of why some comments might seem more valuable than others, or whether students always trust each other’s feedback. This chapter can help students formulate their ideas and figure out more productive ways to think about peer feedback.

If you are using this chapter for a discussion about a trial (or a recent) peer review activity, you can ask students to evaluate a sample set of peer comments for how helpful or not helpful they might be to the writer; you can then ask students if any of the assumptions discussed in this chapter might explain either what works or does not work in the sample feedback or their attitudes toward it.

# Discussion Questions

Because this chapter is designed to interrogate some common assumptions, it might be helpful to give students some questions to answer before they start reading it. Consider keeping a record of what students thought about peer review prior to reading the chapter, whether it is through written responses, a survey, or an online discussion.

Questions to ask prior to reading the chapter:

- What is your attitude toward peer review? What has your experience been with it in past writing classes? Do you find peer reviews helpful at all, and in what way?
- What do you see as the main benefit of peer reviews?
- What do you think the instructor’s role should be in a peer review activity, and why?
- What do you consider as “good” peer feedback, and why? What would you call “bad” feedback, and why?

Questions to ask after reading the chapter:

- Did any of the student quotes resonate with you while you were reading the chapter, and why? Have you had similar experiences or thoughts during a peer review?
- How has your understanding of or attitude toward peer review changed after reading this chapter, if at all?
- Which misconception does not actually sound so bad to you? You do not have to agree with everything this chapter says; there is definitely room for debate!
- During our next peer review, would you read your peers’ draft any differently, and how?
- What kind of feedback would you like to give and get on your draft during our next peer review? What kinds of comments do you personally find most helpful?
