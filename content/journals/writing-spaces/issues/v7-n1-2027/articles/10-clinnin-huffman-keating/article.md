If you’re reading this, you have probably been assigned to give and receive peer feedback (also commonly referred to as peer review or peer workshop). In this chapter, we focus on an important but often overlooked part of the peer feedback process: reading. We believe that intentional reading is fundamental to delivering the kind of supportive, actionable feedback writers seek. However, based on our years of experience teaching college writing across the country, we (Kaitlin, Mellisa, and Ben) have found that many of our students are unsure how to read drafts for the purpose of peer review because they have never been taught how to do so. We believe that with the right tools (what we call reading filters) and some practice, you will be able to read your peers’ work in different ways that will set you up to give constructive feedback.

One of Mellisa’s recent teaching experiences illustrates the uncertainty that many students feel when it comes to reading for peer feedback. It’s the first time students in her writing class will give peer feedback. The class has discussed the benefits and challenges of peer review, and Mellisa has presented the instructions for that day’s workshop. “Any questions?” she asks. Silence. “No? Let’s get started!” Ten minutes later she notices a student staring at his computer screen, a bewildered expression on his face. “How’s it going?” she asks. He replies, “I get that you want us to give each other comments, but what am I reading this draft for?”

The student’s question is simple, yet within it are multiple, implicit questions that reveal the confusion students frequently experience when they begin reading a peer’s draft. What’s the purpose of reading a peer’s draft? Why should you read this paper and not someone else’s? What can you offer your peer? How does reading the draft help you become a better reader and writer? What reading method should you use to give useful feedback? These are great questions to unpack to clarify the confusion surrounding reading in peer feedback and, we hope, to inspire you to learn new ways to read peers’ work.

Let’s start with the question of why you are being asked to read a peer’s draft. Many students recognize there are other sources of feedback for a writer such as a friend, writing center consultant, instructor, or even generative AI. You too may believe these other sources are more expert readers and writers who can provide better feedback. One way to answer Mellisa’s student’s question is to clarify the role of the peer reader in peer review. When reading a draft, you may be tempted to read from the perspective of a “teacher surrogate.” Scholar-teacher Karen Spear explains that a “teacher surrogate” reads to assess someone’s work, hunts for errors, and tells writers how to “fix” their draft. But in the peer review situation, you are not the teacher, you are a peer! Instead, Spear suggests students participate in peer review as a “peer collaborator.” The peer collaborator reads for the purpose of helping the writer achieve their goals in a draft. In this role, you use your knowledge and experiences to share information and resources with the writer so that they can progress on their draft. Isn’t it comforting to know your job is to read with the intent of supporting your peers rather than “fixing” or “grading” their pieces?

Another question Mellisa’s student had was why he should spend his time reading this draft. Like you, he has a busy schedule and needs to make enough time for other classes, extracurriculars, work, family, friends, and sleep. Carefully reading a draft for peer feedback may not seem like a high priority. We get it. However, we believe reading and giving feedback on a draft will help you and your peers grow as writers and thinkers.

Writers need readers to ensure that their work is communicating their intended message and achieving their desired purpose. When you read your peer’s draft, you are able to provide a perspective on their work that no one else can based on your identity, experiences, and knowledge. You can connect intellectually or emotionally to a topic or idea and can offer a personal response relevant to the writer’s ideas. Additionally, as a member of the same class, you have a more specific understanding of the assignment context than someone who is outside of the classroom community. Ultimately, you are one of the most qualified people to read and give feedback on your peer’s writing!

In addition to helping your peers, spending time intentionally reading peer drafts can help you become a better writer. By reading your peers’ work, you can determine if your draft meets the assignment criteria. You can also learn new writing strategies like how other writers approach an idea, how others make an argument, and how others experience the world. Your peers may have different writing practices or techniques that you can try out to see if they work for you.

Finally, Mellisa’s student was asking how he should read his peer’s draft. The student recognizes that, like writing, reading is rhetorical. You read in different ways depending on your purpose for reading and the genre you are reading (Rosenberg 212). Reading to provide feedback is a rhetorical situation that requires different strategies than reading for information or pleasure. In the rest of this chapter, we share different ways you can read a draft using reading filters. These filters will help you to read a draft more effectively and efficiently so that you can give your peers more useful feedback.

# Reading Filters

You are probably familiar with the concept of filters from social media. When you take a picture or video with your phone, you can apply a filter to it. Different filters on the same image can accentuate particular aspects of an image and can hide or minimize other elements. Like social media filters, reading filters are ways of “seeing” a text that can reveal new insights into a text. You can see all of the reading filters in

Table 1. You can apply different reading filters to a single draft to notice different aspects of a text. You will likely need to read each draft at least twice, possibly more, to use the reading filters. Not all reading filters will be appropriate in all contexts; you may find that one filter does not reveal anything new or interesting about a text. The reading filters are presented individually, but you will most often use them together. You may find that you can use multiple filters when you read through a draft, but that other filters require more focus and are best used on their own.

# Reading Filter: Understanding

When you first read a draft, we encourage you to read for understanding. You are not yet evaluating or critiquing the draft; you are simply becoming familiar with it. You develop a thorough understanding of the draft overall, including the content of the draft (the topic, thesis, main ideas, evidence, etc.) and how the content is presented (also known as structure).

The “Understanding” filter helps you practice your reading comprehension skills, which can benefit you as a reader and a writer. As you read for understanding, you will notice aspects of writing like rhetorical strategies, structure, style, and vocabulary, which you may eventually want to apply in your own writing. The “Understanding” filter will help you decide which reading filters to use next and which filters may not be applicable in this context.

# Practice: Writing Reading Notes

Describe what each paragraph or section is about in the margins of the draft, on a separate sheet of paper, or in a digital document. This is known as annotating a text. Annotations are a record of your thoughts and experience when reading. Your notes may be written in complete sentences or short phrases. These notes reflect what you think the draft is about and can be the basis for your feedback later on. For example, your understanding of the draft may not match what the writer thinks, or you may have a difficult time describing content in each paragraph or section. These reading notes can help you give the writer more effective feedback to guide their revision.

# Reading Filter: The Rhetorical Situation

When using the “Rhetorical Situation” filter, you read for how well the draft responds to the writer’s rhetorical situation. Remember writing is rhetorical, meaning the writer is producing a draft for a specific audience to achieve a particular purpose (see Carroll’s “Backpacks vs Briefcases” for more on the rhetorical situation). The rhetorical situation for the draft that you are reading will most likely be explained in an assignment prompt. You may want to review the assignment prompt so that you are familiar with the expected purpose, audience, genre, and other aspects of the rhetorical situation. If the assignment prompt does not provide this information or you still are not sure, then you may want to pick a different filter to use.

Once you understand the rhetorical situation, you can apply the “Rhetorical Situation” reading filter. This filter identifies areas where the writer can more effectively respond to the assignment prompt and its rhetorical situation. Let’s say the assignment prompt states the audience should be a public audience, but when you read through the draft you encounter a lot of technical language that is not explained. Noting this disconnection is useful feedback to help the writer revise their draft. The “Rhetorical Situation” filter can also help you as a writer identify how your own draft does or does not effectively respond to the assignment’s rhetorical situation.

# Practice: Rhetorically Analyzing

The following questions about the rhetorical situation of the draft and its effectiveness can provide useful information to the writer as they revise their draft. Try to provide concrete details from the draft so that your feedback will be specific.

# Questions about Purpose

- According to the assignment prompt, what is the writer’s purpose? What does it ask the writer to do?
- Based on the draft, what do you think is the writer’s purpose?
- Does the writer’s purpose match with the assignment prompt?

# Questions about Audience

- According to the assignment prompt, who is the audience?
- Based on the draft, who do you think is the writer’s intended audience?
- Does the writer’s audience match with the assignment prompt?
- How does the draft address the audience? Does the draft meet the audience’s expectations?

# Question about the Writer

- According to the assignment prompt, how should the writer represent themself?
- How does the writer’s tone and language choices affect the text?

# Questions about Text

- According to the assignment prompt, what is the expected genre?
- What genre is the draft? How does the draft meet the genre conventions?
- According to the assignment prompt, what content should be included in this draft?

How does the text’s structure or organization respond to the assignment prompt? Does the structure or organization detract from the text’s effectiveness?

# Reading Filter: Structure

When you read for structure, you pay close attention to how ideas are presented, organized, and supported throughout the draft as a whole and within specific sections. Structure helps readers follow ideas throughout a draft and keeps them engaged from beginning to end. Keep in mind that there is not a single “correct” structure; structure will vary based on the rhetorical situation including the genre and the writer’s purpose. Some aspects of the draft structure to pay attention to:

- The order in which ideas are presented
- How the ideas are developed throughout the draft
- How evidence and examples are used to support the main ideas
- How the draft conforms to genre conventions
- The use of transition words and phrases (first, next, as a result, etc.) to connect ideas
- The use of design elements (headings, bulleted lists, fonts, etc.)

As a peer collaborator, your role is to provide the writer with feedback on how well the existing draft structure presents their ideas. Your experience as a reader provides meaningful feedback about how the writer can better organize their work and support their readers throughout the draft. You will also learn different ways that writers can structure their ideas, whether by presenting ideas chronologically, thematically, or hierarchically, or using different academic genres like a science lab report or a literary analysis. As a writer, you may want to try some of these different structures in your own draft.

# Practice: Reverse Outlining

A reverse outline illustrates the structure of a completed draft, including the order in which these ideas are presented, and how each section, paragraph, and sentence relates to the draft’s overall purpose.

To create a reverse outline, write the main idea of each paragraph next to the paragraph in the margins. Then use the following questions to evaluate the draft’s structure:

- Does each paragraph have a clear idea? Are there too many or too few ideas presented in the paragraph?
- What is the thesis statement or main idea of the draft? How does the reverse outline relate to that main idea or thesis? Are there any ideas in the body paragraphs that are unrelated to the thesis?
- What is the purpose of each paragraph within the draft? For example, does the paragraph provide background information, summarize a source, introduce a new concept or event, provide a counterargument, etc.?
- Does the order of ideas in the draft make sense? Is there a more logical organizational structure?

# Reading Filter: Questions

Sometimes the best feedback comes from posing a thoughtful question about a peer’s work. A question can be related to an existing filter, such as structure, or it can be about the content of the draft. Use your experience as a reader and a writer to pose questions that require the writer to reflect on their choices and to consider alternative paths the draft may take.

# Practice: Asking Questions

As you read through the draft, only write questions about the draft. Here are some types of questions that you may ask:

- Understanding: Questions to better understand the text. “How did you come to this conclusion?” “Why is this issue significant?”
- Seeking: Questions to gain additional information; these may be the journalistic questions (who, what, when, where, why). “Who is this person in your narrative?” “What does this word mean?”
- Clarifying: Questions to better understand the text or the writer’s purpose. “What are you trying to say here?” “What does this sentence mean?”
- Countering: Questions that present a different perspective. “Have you considered this alternative idea?” “How would you respond to this statement. . .?”
- Wondering: Questions that offer alternative possibilities. “What if you tried this. . .?” “What if you provided an example from another source?”

# Reading Filter: The Writer’s Concerns

When you read for the writer’s concerns, you prioritize the writer’s questions about their draft or areas where they want reader feedback. Reading for the writer’s concerns enables you as a peer collaborator to review the draft based on the writer’s assessment of their work, which may be similar to or different from your initial reading. The writer may also provide more context about their draft that can help you provide more effective feedback (for example, the writer may share a struggle to find sources related to their thesis). Keeping the writer’s concerns in mind will help you eventually provide the writer with more effective feedback. You may be able to ease the writer’s mind about a worrisome aspect of their draft, or you can suggest ways to revise the draft.

# Practice: Reading for Concerns

To address the writer’s concerns, you first need to know what they are. Depending on how you are conducting peer review, you may be able to ask the writer directly what their concerns are, or they may share a letter or note. Sometimes you may not know the writer’s concerns or have the opportunity to ask, in which case, this filter won’t be applicable.

If possible, listen to or read the writer’s concerns about their draft. As you read, pay special attention to these concerns and note elements in their draft that relate to them. It is possible that the reader’s concerns overlap with a different filter you’ve already chosen to use.

# Reading Filter: Successes

Reading for successes means identifying and encouraging aspects of the draft that are working well. Sometimes reviewers forget to read a draft for its successes, but identifying the positives is just as important as critique. (See DePeter’s “How to Write Meaningful Peer Response Praise” for more about the importance of praise.) A success does not have to be perfectly accomplished; it may be a sentence that you find insightful, an idea that is beginning to emerge, or an attempt at trying something different. Identifying successes helps the writer learn what aspects of the draft they should maintain or expand upon as they revise. As you look for areas to encourage, consider exactly what you like and why you like it. Being specific about what you think is successful will help you provide more effective feedback to the writer so that they understand exactly what they are doing well. As a bonus, you may want to try these successful strategies in your own writing!

# Practice: Celebrating Successes

List the successes in the draft as you read using various filters. Try to be as specific as possible about what you see working well in the draft. The specificity will help you provide more effective feedback to the writer so they understand exactly what they are doing well. You may want to organize your feedback using the table structure below.

# Reading Filter: Connections

The “connections” filter entails putting the writer’s draft into conversation with other ideas, texts, and situations. Something in the draft may remind you of a text, whether that is based on a similarity or difference. Some assignments may require you to make explicit course connections (like using class readings as sources), but drafts can benefit from connections even when they are not required. Offering a connection can inspire the writer to think differently about their ideas, their draft, or their writing overall; you may help your peer by providing a relevant source or new perspective on their topic.

In addition to reading for course connections, you can also read for personal connections to relate your experiences and thoughts to the draft. The personal connection may help you understand the draft in a new way and offer the writer different perspectives on their draft. Keep in mind that your personal connections may align with the writer’s draft, but they may also differ. These differences can be productive opportunities to learn new things or to engage with new perspectives even if it feels uncomfortable. In those moments, it can be helpful to remember that your goal as a peer collaborator is to help the writer achieve their goals in the draft. You may want to return to the “Understanding” reading filter to ensure that you are engaging with the draft as it exists rather than responding based on discomfort. You may not want to apply the “personal connections” filter if these connections will keep you from collaborating effectively with the writer on their draft. However, if you encounter drafts that make you feel unsafe, you should talk to your instructor as soon as possible. Your instructor will be able to help you navigate the situation more specifically.

# Practice: Making Connections

As you read, connect the draft to other texts or concepts. Here are some sample connecting questions:

- What are the key concepts, ideas, or themes in the draft?
- Do any of the course materials address the same ideas?
- Do you know of any other materials from outside of your class (from other classes, popular sources, media, etc.) that address the same ideas?
- How does the other material connect to the draft?
- Why should the writer make the connection between their draft and the other material? How will this contextual connection change or strengthen the draft?
- How do you suggest that the writer integrate the connection into their draft?

Some questions to prompt personal connections:

- What memories, thoughts, or feelings does the draft evoke for you?
- How do your personal experiences connect to the draft?
- Is your personal connection beneficial to share with the writer to help them with their project? (For example, does the connection offer a new perspective, or does it provide additional support?).

# Reading Filter: Lower-Order Concerns

Applying the lower-order concerns reading filter means focusing on sentence-level details such as unity within a section or paragraph, sentence ordering, grammar and usage, word choice, and spelling. Reading for lower-order concerns is often not the most useful reading filter to provide the writer with feedback to help them revise. If your feedback is solely about commas and spellings, the writer is missing out on important feedback about their overall message, the structure and organization of their ideas, and other constructive criticism. The writer can change all of the commas and spelling mistakes, but the bigger picture concerns will still remain.

It is also possible that what you think is an error is actually an intentional choice made by the writer. Language or style choices may express the identity, voice, or tone that the writer wants to convey in their writing. Be careful that your reading of a peer’s work is not based on limited ideas about what elements constitute universally good writing (Pattanayak; Sayed). The author could be defying these limitations by meshing various styles (Sanchez-Martin), using multiple or marginalized dialects or languages (Alvarez; Cunningham), or breaking genre conventions (Jacobson, Pawlowski, and Tardy). Using the “Rhetorical Situation” reading filter, especially focusing on the writer’s purpose, can help you understand the writer’s goal with language and style in their draft.

# Practice: Slowing Down

When you encounter punctuation, grammar, or spelling that you have a question about, try the following process:

- Identify the specific words/phrases that are catching your attention.
- Reflect on your reaction to the sentence. What beliefs or ideas about writing does this bring up for you (ex: something that a previous teacher told you about writing? A personal preference?)
- Evaluate the impact of the way the sentence is constructed. Are you able to understand the writer’s meaning with a little effort?
- Leave a comment if you think it would be helpful to the writer to know a reader’s experience.
- Move on. Continue to engage with the writer’s draft in a positive and constructive way.

# Conclusion: From Reading to Feedback and Reflection

After you read your peer’s draft using the “Understanding” reading filter and one or more others, you will now have a deeper knowledge of the draft and can give the writer constructive feedback on their work. Your reading notes will be the foundation of your feedback. A next step might be to identify your most important annotations and use them to craft feedback.

Your reading experience can also help you become a better reader and writer. Consider the reading filters that you used. Think about which of the reading filters were most useful, most confusing, most productive, or most fun for you. This reflection may offer insight into your strengths as a reader as well as reading strategies that you can draw on in future reading situations. Reflect on how you can further develop as a writer based on your experience having read your peer’s draft. Perhaps your peer’s draft prompted you to think differently about a topic or you identified a writing strategy in their work that you want to try yourself. You can incorporate what you’ve learned from reading your peer’s draft into your future writing. You can also use the reading filters in the future when reading someone else’s work or your own.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Theoretical Concepts

Peer review is a fundamental practice in many writing classes, yet students often struggle to realize the many benefits peer feedback can provide to them as writers and readers.[^tr-1] Peer review is a complex process that requires students to change roles (from writer to reader to writer again) and engage in many activities (understanding assignment prompts, drafting, reading a peer’s draft, analyzing and annotating a peer’s draft, composing feedback to a peer, reading feedback from a peer, revising based on feedback). However, peer review is often presented in class as something students should be able to execute with little instruction or practice. This chapter takes a closer look at one part of the peer review process, reading, to help students understand their purpose in reading a peer’s draft and to provide them with specific strategies they can use to read and eventually respond to their peer’s work.

The reading strategies, which we refer to as reading filters, presented in this chapter are grounded in a rhetorical and collaborative understanding of reading and peer review. Below we explain these concepts about reading and peer review and suggest how instructors can integrate these concepts into coursework. We believe it will be most beneficial to scaffold them into the curriculum prior to assigning the chapter “What am I reading this draft for?” so that students are expanding on their understanding of reading and peer review rather than encountering these possibly new ideas for the first time.

The first concept is that reading is rhetorical. As discussed in the chapter, people read in different contexts for different purposes and therefore use different reading strategies in different situations. Instructors can introduce “reading as rhetorical” by assigning other *Writing Spaces* readings on reading such as Mike Bunn’s “Reading Like a Writer” or Karen Rosenberg’s “Reading Games: Strategies for Reading Academic Sources.” The peer feedback reading filters from this chapter will build on students’ existing reading strategies and provide another set to use when reading drafts for peer review. Instructors can use the pre-reading discussion questions listed below to help students connect their previous reading experiences with the reading that they will do during peer review.

A second major concept is that peer review is a collaborative process. Collaboration can be challenging for anyone. Instructors can assign readings that help students understand the purpose of collaboration during the writing process and how to establish successful collaborative relationships. Some recommended readings from *Writing Spaces* include Rebecca Ingall’s “Writing ‘Eyeball to Eyeball’: Building a Successful Collaboration” and Laurence José’s “Stronger Together: Collaborative Work in the Technical Writing Classroom.”

Collaborating during the peer review process can be especially difficult for students who may feel uncertain about sharing their writing with others or critiquing a peer’s work. Often students do not understand their role during peer review, so they adopt what Karen Spear refers to as the “Teacher Surrogate.” As a Teacher Surrogate, students act as evaluators of peer drafts and provide directive feedback based on what the Teacher Surrogate believes the peer should do. There are some obvious problems with the Teacher Surrogate role, namely that the Teacher Surrogate’s perspective on the draft overpowers the writer’s goals for the draft. Writers may feel like they need to adhere to the Teacher Surrogate’s feedback to “correct” their draft. Instead of performing as Teacher Surrogates, Spear suggests that students act as “Peer Collaborators” during the peer review process. A Peer Collaborator works with the writer to help the writer achieve their goals for a draft. The Peer Collaborator’s goal is to assist the writer by providing them with the information and resources they need to continue working on their draft.

Although we highly recommend Karen Spear’s work in *Sharing Writing: Peer Response Groups in English Classes*, her book can be difficult to locate. To help students translate the reading filters our chapter offers into useful feedback, instructors can, instead, assign readings like “How to Write Meaningful Peer Response Praise” by Ron DePeter, which models the type of feedback provided by a Peer Collaborator. Another useful reading is Erin E. Kelly’s “The Good, The Bad, and the Ugly of Peer Review.” Kelly presents peer review personas, like the dreaded Reviewer 2, and suggestions for how to reframe initial reactions into more constructive, collaborative feedback.

One of the challenges associated with teaching students to read for peer review is disabusing them of the notion that it primarily consists of hunting for errors or pinpointing deviations from internalized grammar handbook rules based on a standardized dialect of English. Our chapter offers the “Lower-Order Concerns” filter as one of many available to students rather than as their primary filter. This filter involves noticing how or if the writer is intentionally remixing languaging practices that defy supposed rules for a larger rhetorical purpose. This filter encourages students to deemphasize their impulse to unreflectively mark perceived “errors.” To teach students about internalized language myths and how these shape their reading of peers’ texts, couple our chapter with Cristina Sanchez-Martin’s “Beyond Language Difference in Writing: Investigating Complex and Equitable Language Practices” or “Writing Counterstories: Ways to Challenge Dominant Narratives in FYC” by Sana Sayed.

# Discussion Questions

To help students apply the concepts from this chapter, we offer the following discussion questions. The questions are divided into before reading and after reading questions. These questions may be used as a homework assignment, in-class writing, or small group or class discussion.

## Before reading questions:

- What is your typical reading process when you read for personal reasons (ex: a text message from a friend, a blog post, pleasure reading)?
- What is your typical reading process when you read for academic reasons (ex: an article assigned for class, a textbook chapter)?
- What role do you think reading plays in the peer feedback process?

## After reading questions:

- After reviewing the different reading filters, which filters have you used before when you’ve read texts? Which filters do you feel most comfortable using? Which filters feel least familiar or uncomfortable to you? How may your comfort or experience using these different filters affect the peer feedback that you give?
- What other ways can you read a text? Try coming up with your own reading filter. What does your filter help you learn about a text?
- How do you believe these reading filters will help you provide feedback to your peer?

# Activity #1: Practicing Reading Filters

Each of the reading filters in this chapter is accompanied by an activity. Students can use these activities to apply the filter while reading, and the activities can also be used in class. Instructors can identify the most applicable filters given the assignment prompt or method of peer review (in-person or online, synchronous or asynchronous, anonymous or identified). Another option is to survey the class to see which filters students are most familiar or comfortable with. Facilitate the activities associated with students’ least familiar or uncomfortable reading filters.

# Activity #2: Practice Synthesizing Filters

Reading for peer feedback is a process that takes practice. We recommend facilitating a class practice round of using the reading filters before students start their own peer review. Instructors can assign a sample draft for students to read using the filters. Instructors may ask students to apply all or some of the reading filters or assign each student a different filter. Students should annotate the draft using their filter. After annotating the draft, instructors can facilitate a class discussion about what each of the filters helped the readers to understand about the draft.
