The concept of professionalism can conjure a lot of different ideas. For some, it brings to mind visions of a suit and tie or office cubicles; for others, greasy work gloves and a factory floor. Overwhelmingly, though, the concept of professionalism has become synonymous with white-collar work environments, spaces that tend to value a culture that is predominantly straight, upper/middle-class, and white. As such, many of us can feel unwelcome, uncomfortable, or uncertain navigating professional expectations, much of which are introduced to us for the first time in academic settings.

These concerns are at the heart of this piece, coauthored by a graduate student in Composition and Rhetoric (Brandon), an undergraduate student majoring in Professional Writing and Rhetoric (Skye), and two professors of writing studies (Cynthia and Bridget). We believe individual experience is central to understanding how professional boundaries impact working people. Therefore, Brandon and Skye offer their own experiences as emerging professionals as a way for you to think more concretely about ways to navigate professional settings you may find yourself in as a writer, intern, or employee.

Brandon is a graduate student in Composition and Rhetoric at the University of Central Oklahoma, where he also serves as a teaching assistant. As a queer Afrolatino, first-generation college student, Brandon is passionate about exploring the intersections of culture and rhetoric and how they affect him as a student, teacher, and scholar. Dedicated to promoting equity in academia, he also studies composition strategies that support students from diverse backgrounds.

Skye grew up in Big Pine, California, a small town on a reservation in the Owens Valley, before moving to the San Francisco Bay Area. They are currently an undergraduate student majoring in Professional Writing and Rhetoric and are actively involved in professional opportunities on campus, including serving as an editor for the English Department’s literary magazine. Beyond academia, Skye has worked in a variety of professional settings---from corporate offices to startups---and currently works as a barista and mixologist for a gourmet coffee roaster and craft cocktail bar. Their diverse experiences shape their perspective on workplace norms and the evolving definition of professionalism.

Brandon and Skye, while coming from different backgrounds and perspectives, share many commonalities when it comes to encountering professionalism in classrooms or workplaces. What they share throughout this chapter helps us to critique forms of professionalism that exclude or compromise certain identities and is an extension of recent scholarship that does the same, such as the 2020 special issue of the *Journal of Multimodal* *Rhetorics* on “Dress Practices as Embodied Multimodal Rhetorics” (Manthey) or the 2024 special issue of *Technical Communication Quarterly* on “(Re)Defining ‘Professional’ in Technical and Professional Communication” (Gelms and Johnson).

As emerging professionals in writing studies, Brandon and Skye have both encountered situations where they’ve felt boxed in or excluded by professional expectations:

Brandon: Situations constantly arise that challenge what I view as

> professionalism and how I see myself as a professional. The most prominent experience I constantly have to deal with is my volume. I am a very easily excitable person, and I love to talk about writing with my students and peers. This can cause my voice to carry and get louder than I intend to, often showing my passion for whatever the thing is that I am doing. However, there have been multiple occasions where my loudness was treated with disdain, often being labeled as “loud” or “ghetto,” and I was asked to quiet down when teaching or conferencing with my students. This issue begs the question of how “professionalism” operates as a system. If I do not fit the hegemony that is professionalism---can I queer it to fit me?

Skye: Many of us pursue careers in fields we love, spending years

> earning an education only to enter the workforce and feel instantly out of place. We work hard to build our qualifications, yet once we step into professional spaces, we realize that competence alone isn’t enough---there are unspoken expectations. But what happens when simply being yourself is seen as unprofessional? Professionalism is often framed as a neutral measure of workplace readiness, but in reality, it has been shaped by norms that dictate everything from dress and speech to work style and demeanor. These expectations disproportionately burden marginalized groups, forcing them to assimilate in order to be taken seriously. Instead of signaling qualifications, professionalism becomes a tool of exclusion---one that limits opportunities, scrutinizes differences, and erases authenticity. If professionalism is meant to measure capability, why does it so often function as a barrier? And if these standards don’t serve everyone, why do we still reinforce them?

# Overview

The questions that Brandon and Skye posit here form a starting place for us to think about how the concept of professionalism can be challenged or critiqued in ways that serve those who are new to professional spaces. In the following, we overview some of the professional expectations you might encounter in the classroom and beyond, particularly in regard to language, dress, and behavior. In sharing Brandon and Skye’s experiences with these aspects of professionalism, we aim to help you better understand what emerging professionals encounter in the settings students and new graduates often find themselves in and hope these narratives can be used as a catalyst for reflecting on your own rhetorical strategies for navigating professional expectations.

While this chapter is meant to help you think through your own approaches to professional expectations, it is not a how-to guide. Navigating professional expectations is a highly personal, contextual, and rhetorical act, meaning it forces us to consider the nuances of audience, purpose, context, power, and identity (for more *Writing Spaces* pieces on rhetoric, see the chapters by Blakesley; Boyd; Carroll; and Duffy). In *Technical Communication After the Social Justice Turn: Building Coalitions for Action*, Rebecca Walton, Kristen Moore, and Natasha Jones ask us to consider how we can advance social justice in our work through the three Ps: positionality, privilege, and power (61). These three Ps can be immensely important factors in determining individual approaches to professional contexts. How does our own positionality, privilege, and power affect our work, mindset, or how we orient ourselves to an unfamiliar space? How do these things intersect (or collide) with the positionality, privilege, and power of others we share the professional space with? As you encounter the stories shared in the remainder of this chapter, we encourage you to consider how the three Ps come to bear on Brandon’s and Skye’s experiences in workplace settings.

# Language: Navigating Professional Authenticity and Building Community

Language is political, cultural, communal, and a major aspect of how individuals express and mark their own identities (see, for example, Alvarez et al.; Sánchez-Martín; Tremain). Language is also not neutral, meaning it is connected to vastly rich histories and contexts that may not be immediately apparent (Bailey et al.). In light of this, language is oftentimes the biggest marker of what is (and what isn’t) considered appropriate in professional settings (see, for example, Vershawn Ashanti Young et al.’s work on code-meshing and code-switching). To make things even trickier, professional language is typically defined and upheld by community norms that only become clear to someone who has had the opportunity to participate in that community long enough to catch on.

Even then, mimicry is sometimes necessary to reflect the kinds of language that is valued in professional spaces, leaving us feeling inauthentic or unsure about our place in these situations. For example, Juval V. Racelis coins the phrase “professional ventriloquy” (258) to describe how job seekers oftentimes have to appropriate dominant language practices to get an interview, demonstrating just how consequential language is to being accepted into professional spaces, let alone being able to stay in them as our full, authentic selves. Here, we reflect on these kinds of issues, noting times when we’ve had to pay particularly close attention to our use of language in order to fit professional expectations:

# Skye: Language Is a Tool for Survival and Success . . .

. . . but it’s also a marker of who is “allowed” to belong in a professional space. I’ve navigated vastly different work environments---from a laid-back cannabis warehouse with a built-in stoner culture to the rigid professionalism of a dental office, where restraint was not just expected but necessary. These experiences taught me how language expectations shift dramatically depending on context, and how mastering this shift is often essential to thriving. This kind of language shifting is called “code-switching.”

At the cannabis dispensary, professionalism was defined by relaxed norms: a lax dress code, playlists blasting hip hop or indie rock, and conversations often laced with slang. Speaking casually was part of the culture, and my language adapted to fit this rhythm effortlessly. In contrast, the dental office operated within stricter language and behavioral norms, where professional boundaries were more rigid, and my coworkers were older. I studied the tone, topics, and behaviors of others before speaking. Mimicry allowed me to adapt, gradually integrating my own voice without disrupting the office dynamic.

# Brandon: Language Is a Form of Community-Building and Resistance

Generally, my language can be somewhat rash or abrupt, and I say whatever comes to my mind. I have been labeled “ghetto” for the way that I choose to speak---sometimes being called “the English teacher with no grammar when he speaks.” With how I grew up, it did not matter the way I spoke, but what I said. I have always been valued for the words that I can convey to whomever I speak, yet as I got older, I noticed some people would laugh more at what I say (even when I am not aiming to be humorous) or be taken back by my bluntness or choice of language.

I generally do not try to elevate my language to be more sophisticated when I teach. I feel there is already enough new that the students are having to deal with. For many of my students, this is their first foray into college, they are often alone, and most of my students are first-generation. So even when my colleagues make comments about the way I choose to conduct myself, I always let them know I am the way I am so I can be more accessible to my students. I want them to have a good introduction into college. As I am the first professor for many of them, I do not want them to feel this disheartened and terrifying feeling of learning college and its syntax and grammars. This is not to say I never use some of my favorite words, like *hegemony, heteronormativity, queer, rhetoric*, or many others. Instead, I want them to feel inclined to ask me questions, create dialogue, and form a community in the language they know so we can grow together.

# Self-Reflection

In thinking about Brandon’s and Skye’s experiences, we can glean that being able to switch between discourse communities without fully erasing oneself is a valuable skill that takes awareness and practice. The key is knowing when to adapt and recognizing when the situation calls for boundary pushing. Choosing when and where to adapt can be an important act of resistance as we maintain our sense of self and authenticity. What’s more, understanding language not as a necessarily exclusionary aspect of professional spaces, but as a form of community-building, can help us embrace our familiar language practices that seemingly exist outside of what we see as professional boundaries. Language is contextual, but we also have agency to shape and influence our own contexts.

Considering your own experiences with language, reflect:

- Was there ever a time you had to pay particular attention to your use of language (written or spoken) to fit professional expectations? How did that make you feel? What strategies did you use to navigate that situation?
- Was there ever a time you found yourself mimicking a language, tone, or style of communication to better fit a professional expectation? How did you know what or how to mimic? What did you find challenging and/or useful about that experience?
- Have you ever encountered two contexts with vastly different professional expectations, such as Skye’s experience at the dispensary versus the dentist’s office? How did you navigate between them? What does this tell you about how professional expectations form?

# Dress: Expressions of Identity in Professional Realms

Dress practices are another area in which notions of professionalism are unduly present. In a special issue of the *Journal of Multimodal Rhetorics*, editor Katie Manthey points to “many examples of how oppression manifests through dress codes in the workplace” (5). For example, disability studies scholar Ada Hubrig discusses how their use of a colostomy bag means professional dress is simply not an option for them (143). Brittany Hull et al. further share their experiences as Black women navigating expectations of professional dress, stating, “Because minority bodies are always, already under scrutiny and subject to explanation and qualification, they are often conditioned to be aware of and responsive to the presumed standards of professionalism just to survive” (7). Much has also been written about how professional dress codes often exclude gender non-conforming and nonbinary people. For example, in their op-ed about students’ experiences with dress codes in and out of school, Nathaniel George notes that students need to learn and grow in environments that value their individual selves. What’s more, “It is imperative for these same students to be met with a familiar sense of belonging and inclusion when they enter the workforce, with professional standards that allow them to be who they are” (George). What these experiences demonstrate is that stereotypical expectations of professional dress are simply not accessible or equitable to everyone, as is also represented in Skye’s and Brandon’s experiences.

# Brandon: Queering Style

I have always tried to dress to fit what is professional. I generally prefer the aesthetic of dark academia or just the nice look in general---preferring polos and slim fit slacks/khakis to regular clothes. However, as I was growing up, one thing that always bothered me when I wanted to dress this way was being labeled “too white” or trying to act outside of my race, even to the point people claimed that “the style doesn’t fit me.” This generally caused me to falter in my identity and feel there was no way I could know who I was or what style fit me. Then, in only seeing the adult men around me wearing either extremely casual clothes or suits/slacks, I could not see what I wanted reflected in who I saw. I seek to circumvent this by just queering the way I dress. As my dad would say, “You might as well let them know how gay you are the first day,” because I try to wear a lot of colorful shirts, shirts that have several designs on them, and I love a heeled dress boot. While this may seem out of the ordinary, I want my appearance to match the way I choose to present to the world and the way I choose to teach and engage.

# Skye: It’s Not Just About the Clothes

I’ve always known that dress codes are never just about the clothes---they’re about perception, power, and whose identities are deemed “professional” or acceptable. As a non-binary person who primarily presents as female, navigating workplace attire often feels like walking a tightrope. I’m expected to embody a polished, hyper-feminine image while maintaining the functionality required to do my job. My boss at my bartending job purchased me a dress for a 1920s-themed cocktail night---a tight, rhinestone-covered flapper dress that sparkled under the lights but felt like a straitjacket. The fabric clung to my body, restricting movement and making it nearly impossible to bend, carry trays, or mix drinks. More than that, the hyper-feminine design left me feeling out of place in my own skin. When my boss handed me the dress and said, “This will look amazing on you,” I smiled, said thank you, and put it on without complaint.

Why didn’t I say no? Because marginalized professionals often feel the need to be agreeable, to avoid being labeled difficult or ungrateful. As someone with a naturally warm, cheery disposition, I worried that asserting myself would damage my relationship with my boss. But as I struggled through that shift, I realized how much of myself I was sacrificing---not just physically, but emotionally, trying to fit into a version of professionalism that wasn’t designed for people like me. After that night, I began to challenge these expectations in small but intentional ways. I curated a wardrobe of dress pants, button-downs, and flats that aligned with both my identity and practical needs. I framed conversations with my boss around functionality, saying, “Dresses like this make it hard to do my job efficiently.” Over time, those conversations reshaped what was considered “professional” in my workplace.

# Self-Reflection

As Brandon and Skye demonstrate, dress represents so much more than just clothes or their embedded cultural connotations; they’re a way for us to represent ourselves to others around us and communicate important aspects of our personalities and identities. Professionalism should never require compromising your identity to prove competence, skill, or conformity. Resisting restrictive dress codes isn’t just about rejecting a single outfit or type of style that is typical in a particular professional space---it’s about challenging broader systems that expect marginalized people to conform, disappear, and prioritize appearance over performance.

Considering your own experiences with dress and cultural identity, reflect:

- Was there ever a time you had to pay particular attention to your dress or general appearance to fit professional expectations? How did that make you feel? What strategies did you use to navigate that situation?
- In what ways, if any, does dress have to do with professionalism? What are we actually evaluating when we evaluate someone’s dress? In thinking about Brandon’s reflections on how his sense of style reflects his personality and identity in specific ways, what are some ways you communicate identity through how you choose to dress?

# Behavior: Learning the Hidden Curriculum

Professionalism can also become a way to enforce particular behaviors beyond language and dress. It can dictate the ways we carry ourselves (e.g., sit up straight!) or navigate a space (e.g., reserve the head of the table). However, many of these expectations are not always clearly articulated, making them part of what is often called the “hidden curriculum,” or social expectations we are simply expected to know in order to succeed. For example, in their *Writing Spaces* chapter, Sarah Seeley, Kelly Xu, & Matthew Chen discuss the challenges of students adapting to new social contexts and discourse communities, which include “learning a ‘hidden’ lexicon [. . .] and navigating tacit power hierarchies” (281). Because so many social expectations aren’t explicitly communicated, we often must “read the room” to understand how we are expected to behave.

When these expectations *are* communicated, they often come in the written form of syllabus policy statements (i.e., attendance policies, late work policies, cell phone policies) or employee handbooks in the workplace. These documents become sites of tension as they work to both make expectations transparent, while also enforcing those expectations, which might not be inclusive or equitable to everyone. Such policies can embody and enforce what Asao Inoue, in his work on racialized identities and writing, identifies as the *white racial habitus*, or a culture that privileges white, heteronormative, ableist, middle-class, and masculine dispositions. To further complicate things, what is considered “respectful” or “engaged” behavior in one setting may be completely different in another. Skye and Brandon reflect on these tensions:

# Skye: “Acting” the Part

People love to say “just be yourself,” but what they really mean is: *be the* *version of yourself that makes everyone else comfortable*. It isn’t just about how well you do the job but about how you carry yourself, who you align with, what you choose to speak up about, and when you choose to stay silent. Professionalism is a game of optics. The expectation to conform and be agreeable to “fit the culture,” is relentless. Hierarchies in professional settings demand compliance, even in workplaces that claim to be progressive or collaborative. It’s not just about how you dress or how you speak, but how much space you take up, and whether or not you adhere to the unspoken rules that govern a workplace.

Throughout my career, I’ve learned how to read these environments and adjust accordingly. When I moved into management positions, I felt the immediate tension of what it meant to “act the part.” As a bartender, I had built bonds with my coworkers through shared struggles. Long shifts, late nights, inside jokes. But when I was promoted to a managerial role, those relationships changed overnight. Suddenly, the same camaraderie that made work enjoyable became a liability. Joking too much could undermine my authority. Showing frustration could make me seem unprofessional. I was expected to maintain a level of detachment from the people I used to be on equal footing with, and failing to do so would make my leadership seem weak.

# Brandon: Professionalism is Personal

One way my friends have always described me as is “pretty confrontational” (though I think of it as standing up for myself no matter what). This has created several issues, with the biggest being the way people misunderstand my bluntness when speaking up about things I believe to be manipulative or exploitative. For instance, when starting my first semester as a teaching assistant and navigating the world of academia as both instructor and student, I ran into an issue with one of my supervisors who had a slightly different teaching philosophy than what I had held during my year of teaching high school. I am mostly focused on helping my students achieve the best writing they can produce at the level I meet them---not some ulterior idea of what an “A” or “B” student should be. Some in the department felt there should be a stronger connection to grading philosophies with grammar and teaching all of our classes in a homogenized way, yet that completely conflicted with my identity I had created for myself as a teacher. When I attempted to speak up about this, I was deemed unprofessional, my knowledge and experience in teaching was diminished if not erased, and the lack of attentiveness to the culture I was aiming to foster in my classroom was neglected. I was othered by simply wanting to be the thing I never got to see or experience in school.

When I was first attending college, I was very similar to the students I teach now: first-generation, isolated, poor, working full-time, and I did not see many professors who looked or acted like me. Instead of getting bogged down by it, I decided that I would be the person for younger me by growing into the man I could have used when I was younger. While I did not know how serious I would take that with becoming a teacher, I set myself on a path to achieve the goal no matter the obstacles. I lived my true and authentic self despite what others thought about or told me. There is a hidden curriculum I’ve had to navigate as both a former student and a present teacher---one that will also, no doubt, alter the future me.

# Self-Reflection

In both Skye and Brandon’s experiences, we see how the concept of what is deemed “appropriate” in one setting can come into conflict with other contexts or even how we as people naturally exist in the world, especially when it comes to emotions. When our behavior is policed or scrutinized for how well it fits into a limited concept of “professional,” we can feel like our humanity gets diminished or lost in the process. It can be an exercise in curiosity to learn how professionalism is being defined *for* you. These expectations may not be fair or even universal, as norms are often shaped by imbalanced power dynamics, biases, and outdated expectations.

Considering your own experiences with expectations about behavior, reflect:

- Was there ever a time you had to pay particular attention to your behavior to fit professional expectations? How did that make you feel? What strategies did you use to navigate that situation?
- What have you encountered as part of the “hidden curriculum” referenced in this section?
- What written policies have you encountered in a class, internship, or workplace that specifically address professionalism? In what ways do these policies help or hinder your work? What policies would you like to see represented in classes or workplaces that would help neutralize the way we feel constrained by certain professional expectations?
- Was there ever a time you felt pressured to conform to professional expectations to be eligible for a job or opportunity? How did that make you feel? What strategies did you use to navigate that situation?

# Defining Professionalism for Ourselves

The issues we’ve reflected on throughout this chapter---the ways in which language, dress, and behavior are used as mechanisms for defining expectations around professionalism---raise complicated questions about how we should represent ourselves in professional situations. As we’ve reflected on throughout this chapter, we recognize how common it is for both new and seasoned professionals to grapple with how best to represent themselves in professional settings while remaining true to their own sense of personal identity. Are we meant to hide certain aspects of our identities, language, or experiences to better appeal to professional “fit,” or should we prioritize authenticity as a way to resist these structural issues? Of course, determining how and where to separate our personal personas from our professional ones can be a fraught process that may feel uncomfortable. We invite you to further reflect:

- Where (if at all) in this chapter did you see your own experience reflected? Did you identify with anything Brandon and Skye shared about their own journeys as students and professionals?
- What other professional issues have you encountered in school, an internship, or workplace that aren’t represented in this chapter?
- How do *you* define what professional dress, language, or behavior looks like in your chosen career path? Where does that overlap or diverge from what is conventionally expected?

It’s important to note that regardless of what stage you are at in your academic or professional career, feelings of uncertainty or discomfort are common. All four of us, at some point in our professional lives, have had feelings of being an outsider. Such feelings can also inspire a belief that you’re underqualified or that you don’t belong. We’ve also learned that feeling underqualified or that you don’t belong doesn’t mean you actually are or don’t. Despite how it’s often discussed, professionalism *is* personal, and therefore, our goal shouldn’t be to figure out how to alter ourselves to fit a predefined version of professionalism. Rather, we should determine how we can define professionalism for ourselves and our communities.

# Teacher Resources

# Teaching with This Chapter

Regardless of where you are in your professional career, or what your path to teaching looked like, we imagine you might be able to personally identify with some of the experiences Brandon and Skye share in this chapter.[^tr-1] For many of us teachers, our trajectory toward our careers can look similar to where our students come to us from. Even now, whether you’re new to the profession of teaching or are a seasoned pedagogue, perhaps you find yourself currently struggling with professional expectations in your own workplace settings. What’s more, many of us feel immense tension between a want to prepare students for the professional world and the knowledge that the professional world is one that can be harsh, exclusive, and unfair. Should we advise jobseekers to obfuscate their true identities and experiences when they go against the professional norms, or should we encourage them to resist this reality in hopes that the structural issues will one day change?

We hope this chapter can serve as a conversation with your students about these types of issues within the realm of professionalism, particularly if you’re teaching professional writing or career-planning courses, or if you work in a mentoring capacity with undergraduate or graduate students preparing to transition into a profession. This conversation also holds a lot of relevance for working with first-year students who are learning to navigate professionalism within the academy. To do so, you may invite students to reflect on their own experiences much in the same way that Brandon and Skye have in this chapter. Being able to put themselves in conversation with Brandon, Skye, and the issues present throughout the chapter may help students feel less alone in their apprehensions or anxieties about encountering issues of “professionalism” in their academic careers or workplaces, while empowering them to discover how they might navigate unfamiliar contexts or resist certain expectations.

Throughout the chapter, we’ve included a series of reflective questions students can use to begin to map their own experiences onto larger patterns. These can be used as a homework assignment to complete along with reading the chapter or as freewriting exercises in class. We encourage you to frame these prompts not as an invitation to lay bare all of the traumas students may have faced in the past, but rather as opportunities to make sense of how topics such as language, dress, and behavior shape how we understand professionalism as a collective culture and, potentially, the problems that arise as a result of those set boundaries.

You may find that some students feel naturally protective over the professional boundaries that others find exclusionary. For many students, these professional expectations have been ingrained via years of classist, ableist, heteronormative, and white supremacist attitudes that permeate US American culture. What’s more, international students may have come from cultures that have an entirely different orientation to professionalism than what is represented in this chapter or their classmates’ experiences. In both cases, asking students to question their preconceived notions of professionalism can feel destabilizing. In these moments, it can be helpful to trace with students the origins of these norms, who they were developed for and by, and the ways they may or may not continue to serve communities. As always, it’s important to exercise patience as some students grapple with what might be personally challenging ideas for the first time.

We also encourage you to reflect on these questions right alongside students. There is wisdom in experience, and sharing your own struggles and successes with professional expectations can help neutralize feelings of inadequacy in students, particularly when we model the vulnerability that reflective work often requires. We believe that this act, in and of itself, is a professional one.
