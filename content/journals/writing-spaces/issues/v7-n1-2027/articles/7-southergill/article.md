# Introduction

This article examines synthesis---the making of something new by the ethical manipulation of what’s already available. To begin, imagine standing before a simple interactive art exhibit which, at first glance, looks like nothing more than a pile of broken colored glass such as in Figure 1:

![Photograph of an art installation seen from below: thousands of small iridescent cut-outs shaped like butterflies hang in a dark space, catching pinpoint lights and glinting green, gold, pink and violet.](assets/figure-p002-1.jpg)\
Figure 1. Broken Glass. Photo by Jack Van Hel on Unsplash.

However, next to it, you notice instructions that challenge you to create something new by sorting, sifting, and rearranging the pieces. You acquire some gloves and carefully pick up one piece and look at it closely---noting its shape, weight, color, and imperfections. As you study the pieces and move them around, new configurations emerge. Perhaps you look closely at the size of pieces, and see how they form a sense of weight by how you work symmetrically (or not). Or perhaps you look at the colors---the greens next to the reds seem to dance in the light.

With each new placement of glass, you create a new experience for the next viewer. As you do so, you become more calculating. What would you like them to see, and why? Moving the pieces and making new connections offers an indefinite number of possible new combinations. You as the creator soon move from simply moving glass to planning an exhibit for your viewer. You could, if asked, talk about your choices. What did you do, and perhaps more importantly, why did you do it? At some point, you moved beyond moving a shard of glass to seeing where the glass fit in a larger whole. Simply put, you synthesized pieces into a whole. This article seeks to make you an artist not with glass, but with prose---first by defining by example and then discussing your process.

# Defining by Examples

When you moved the glass to create your art, you performed synthesis. By looking at pieces, interpreting their possible connections, seeing new patterns, and purposefully creating a new whole, you replicated what writing in college often does---it ethically applies existing texts to new and thoughtful ones. Courtesy of the University Writing Center at Brandeis University, let’s examine the following research paragraph:

> Commentators have conventionally approached ethnic jokes using the superiority theory of humor, which claims that people laugh when a joke makes them feel above the object of ridicule. Thomas Hobbes characterizes this emotion as “sudden glory arising from some sudden conception of some eminency in ourselves, by comparison with the infirmity of others, or with our own formerly” (47). Applying this concept to ethnic humor, John Morreall states that people derive this “sudden glory” from “mocking [immigrants] in great detail about their race, accent, clothing, ugliness, etc.” (10). Leon Rappoport further clarifies how stereotypes and ethnicity-based mockery embody the superiority theory by explaining that these disparaging jokes often employ “polar opposite adjectives...[so that] only [the] negative end of the pair is emphasized [and] the positive end always remains implicitly understood as characteristic of the ‘superior’ joke teller” (33). With these jokes, the overt debasement of immigrants simultaneously elevates the person making the joke. Consequently, the opposing adjectives noted by Rappoport suggest a hierarchy between the person who tells the joke and the people at the butt of the joke.

The author wishes to make a claim that answers why people would engage in unacceptable behavior (making ethic jokes). They do so by synthesizing from texts composed by very different authors:

- Thomas Hobbes was a 17th century English philosopher known for his work *Leviathan*.
- John Morreall is a contemporary Emeritus Professor of Religious Studies at the College of William and Mary.
- Leon Rappoport, for whom the Leon Rappoport Memorial Lobby at Kansas State University was named, was a Professor of Psychology who actively published scholarship until the early 21st century.

Beyond the varied authorship cited, an astute reader may note other signs of synthesis:

- The text begins with a statement that combines multiple perspectives into one: a statement that “conventional” approaches to understanding ethnic humor stem from a “superiority theory.”
- Each piece of evidence receives an introduction in the form of a verb. The authors use “characterizes,” “applying,” and “further clarifying” to help create connections between each piece of evidence and retain the reader’s focus on the central claim.
- In the final two sentences, the authors go beyond simply restating the main claim. They introduce “hierarchy” as a next step in their analysis. Upcoming paragraphs can explore and connect that concept to their writing.

For further demonstration, here’s an excerpt from Stephen Graham John’s “Foreword” to *Never Whistle at Night: An Indigenous Dark Fiction* *Anthology*. John, a Blackfeet Native American horror and science fiction author, orients readers to the collection of short stories as he writes:

> There’s scary stuff in stories, sure, there’s stuff that keeps you up at night, there’s stuff that makes you watch the darkness you’re driving through that much closer. But there’s hope, too. Just---some nights you have to wade through a lot of blood to get there. So, for these next 392 pages, let these twenty-six writers take you by the hand, lead you into the darkness at the heart of---let me put some quotation marks around it---“America.” (xvi).

Think of John’s example less as synthesis itself than an invitation to synthesize by taking their concepts and turning them into questions. Which stories offer hope? How do they do it through the “scary stuff” of blood and darkness? And what do the stories say about “America?” From these questions, you could perform a synthesis of your own. You could draw from the collective wisdom of the authors to help you see something new. Then, you can craft writing that helps the reader understand your claim, how the evidence connects, and then what bigger picture your claim moves toward.

In summary, college writers synthesize to create something new. Rather than asking readers to simply read what others have written, synthesis seeks to generate new experiences. What we now need, having defined synthesis through example, is a method for doing it.

How to Synthesize

Knowing what something is and knowing how to make something represent are very different domains of knowledge. A driver may know how to operate a car but not how to replace a part or change oil. We may have a sense in principle of what creates a sound text, but now how do we actually do the things . . .the things that create good texts . . . the things that impress our reader . . . the things that leave us very pleased with what we’ve accomplished in our writing? Knowing what synthesis is and why we do it wouldn’t be enough on their own to understand how to synthesize.

Naturally, how best to populate the far-left column may rise to the surface as a concern. Afterall, there are many ways to read across multiple sources. Here we’ll focus on three ways in which to build relationships across texts so you can fill out your matrix:

1. Ask inquiry questions.

2. Align by action.

3. Select a stance.

### Ask Inquiry Questions

When reading your sources (again) you can pick questions to brainstorm possible connections between them. The questions you ask allow you to interpret the source text for a nuance otherwise not as easily seen. For instance, it may be productive to ask where one source disagrees with something. Or perhaps you may ask something like what does this source do particularly well? Inquiry questions often help writers flesh out their ideas early in their writing processes. Here, they also can serve to engage with texts to spot connective tissues. The University of Illinois at Springfield categorizes inquiry questions into four major topics: similarity, contrast, accumulation, and causation.

- Similarity: how do sources support one another?
- Contrast: how do sources disagree with one another?
- Accumulation: how have sources used other sources?
- Causation: how do sources consider the impact of other sources?

These four major questions permit writers to more readily spot areas that can become entries on the left-side of a matrix table.

### Align by Action

As you contemplate ways to read across texts in your matrix, another approach available to you surrounds identifying the *actions* an author takes. Here, a style analysis may prove especially insightful.

Start by closely reading the source text with a very careful eye to the verbs used by the original author. Then begin to categorize statements by what work they do. For instance, where in the source do you see recommendations as evidenced by “advises,” “urges,” and similar language? Table 1, verb choices, provides a basis through which you can look closely at the source piece for what it *does* using what it *says* as a guide.

In essence, reading for action statements allows you to note commonplaces across texts. For instance, you may spot how two sources make similar recommendations. Alternatively, both sources may disagree in similar ways to a common idea. Reading for verbs can help you see these similarities and differences.

### Listen to and for Open Spaces

In another strategy, read and think in terms of space. Space in this sense has nothing to do with physical distance. Rather, metaphorically, space represents the degree of agreement between authors. They as independent authors may not explicitly say they agree or disagree with each other. You as a writer can identify the degree of agreement.

Let’s say that your writing process leads to suggesting sources that disagree with each other. In this case, you’ve set up a dispute that your writing can address. Then the contested space could invite a form of reconciliation. Perhaps there’s an agreement that reconciles the difference. Writing and explaining the newfound accord permits you to enter the space you have created. A spatial interpretation may also note a high degree of agreement between two sources, in which case there’s no dispute to address. Now, your writing could take a line of thought further. If sources agree, what then can happen? Defining agreements between multiple reliable sources gives academic writers authority to build well-supported ideas.

Space, or overlap between sources, can generate a foundation from which to build or conflict to resolve and then grant you vocabulary for writing a matrix.

# Second Step: Manage Sources

In the preceding section, we discussed our first step and recognized how inquiry questions, style analysis, and opening space support our efforts. Now in this second step, we turn our attention to tactics for bringing sources into our writing.

## Quotes, Paraphrases, and Summaries: Our Raw Materials

Existing materials can be brought into a new work through one of three ways: quotes, paraphrases, and summaries. Each approach translates an existing author’s ideas into a new text.

- *Quotes* retain the original language of the source material. A quotation can take only a word or part of a sentence from the original source.
- *Summaries* condense larger pieces of prose into smaller chunks and may include both quotations and paraphrases.
- *Paraphrases* present ideas from another author using entirely new language.

Armed with these definitions, the questions then become: when and why should I quote, paraphrase, or summarize? As a general guideline, more advanced writers quote sparingly and only when a substitution of language compromises the value of the original work. Common reasons to quote include the literary value of the source language, accuracy of definitions, and correctness of exact statistics. Otherwise, summarizing and paraphrasing tends to work well.

Summaries condense broad swaths of text into smaller chunks. Proceed first by looking for major claims and conclusions. Separate out evidence, examples, and rebuttals. Then rewrite your notes on the major claims and conclusions in your own words. Be careful that, should any original language appear in your summary, you attribute it with quotation marks (as longer quotes that require block style quotation generally do not appear in summaries). Then look closely at your summary to ensure it retains the major ideas of the original piece.

The remaining option (paraphrasing) follows a similar process. After having closely reviewed and read the source, highlight then the elements of the source material most significant to your intended synthesis. Remain mindful that breaking a part of a text from its original context requires careful consideration (to which the upcoming ICE steps can respond). Rewrite the content of interest in your own words. Remember, you can directly quote within a paraphrase if a word or small number of words demands retention. More often than not, the paraphrase uses entirely your own words to convey the ideas of the original author.

Once we have selected the strategy that best fits our needs at a given moment, our attention turns to placing the source material in our text in a method called “ICE.”

### Introduce---Cite---Explain (ICE)

Whether quoting, summarizing, or paraphrasing, good writing takes more than simply placing the source material. We will draw here from a helpful acronym suggested by the University of Arizona Global Campus Writing Center to consider how to place an existing text in our own writing:

- I = Introduce
- C = Cite
- E = Explain

### I = Introduce

The time has come and words must be put to the page. Through your hard work thus far, you know your source material and what ideas you wish to relay. You’ve picked your raw material and decided what form to use (quotes, summaries, and paraphrases). The readership next needs some form of introduction to the source material itself. Before they encounter it, they need to know the material’s relationship to the paragraph in which it is housed.

Often, college writers draw from two methods to introducing their sources. One such approach uses verbs. Consider referencing Table 2 to help craft your sentence. A second tool consists of prose you write between the quote and the major claim. Use narration to tell the reader what the material means in the context of your work. Often verb choices and narration work together to form a clear introduction.

To see these two approaches in action, let’s look at a published work written by Annika Hodges as a student at Eastern Oregon University:

> There is evidence suggesting that being exposed to sexually objectifying advertisements leads to the development of anti-woman attitudes. In their study, “Images of Women in Advertisements: Effects on Attitudes Related to Sexual Aggression,” Kyra Lanis and Katherine Covell found that men exposed to advertisements in which women were objectified, compared to men shown more humanizing images of women, were “significantly more accepting of rape-supportive attitudes,” while women exposed to the more humanizing images of women “were less accepting of such attitudes” (639). . . .

Hodges uses both techniques to introduce their source:

1. Verb choice: “found” serves to state a conclusion.

2. Prose: The first statement classifies the quoted text as “evidence suggesting,” thereby giving a framework on the evidence itself.

### C = Cite

When done correctly, proper citation ensures that the reader can see where another author’s ideas have been incorporated into the text and can find the original source for themselves. As a cautionary note, please be sure to check which standard is expected for your assignment as there are several systems often used in college (such as Modern Language Association, or “MLA,” and American Psychological Association, or “APA”).

Figure 4, below, provides an example of an APA in-text citation. APA uses the author’s last name, year of publication, and a locator (often a page number). The author-year can serve as the subject of the sentence (“narrative” in-text citations), such as illustrated below. Note that the name, year of publication, and locator are shaded. Also note that the paraphrased material is sandwiched within the parts of the in-text citation, which is a common practice both for appropriate citation and clarity of precisely which material has been paraphrased.

![A three-line sample sentence on a light grey band: Webster-Stratton (2016) described a case example of a 4-year-old girl who showed an insecure attachment to her mother; in working with the family dyad, the therapist focused on increasing the mother’s empathy for her child (pp. 152–153). The author and year at the start and the page numbers at the end are highlighted in blue.](assets/figure-p012-1.png)\
Figure 4. Sample APA In-Text Citation. Adapted from *Paraphrases.*

APA citation also requires a reference list that provides all the information your readers will need to find the source. The full reference list entry starts with the exact same information as the in-text citation: the author (or in this case authors) and date of publication. For a basic journal article, the entry would look like Figure 5:

> Grady, J. S., Her, M., Moreno, G., Perez, C., & Jelinek, J. (2019). Emotions in storybooks: A comparison of storybooks that represent ethnic and racial groups in the United States. *Psychology of Popular Media Culture, 8*(3), 207--217. https://doi.org/10.1037/ppm0000185

Figure 5. Sample APA Reference List Entry. Adapted from “1. Journal article.”

Full citation includes both in-text and reference list entries, and promotes ethical writing by:

1. Ensuring the reader knows when an idea comes from another writer and given credit for it, which corresponds to *attribution* as an ethical objective.

2. Providing a means by which the reader can find the original source, which corresponds to *transparency* as an ethical objective. While the specific rules associated with citation will vary across stan-

dards, all citations accomplish these same two objectives.

### E= Explain

In clear language, this step asks you to tell your readers specifically what the source material accomplishes. To see how this step could be accomplished, let’s first examine the words of Iowa State University student Madeline Gillette in their reflection on their piece “4 Reasons RoboCop Should Be Considered A Feminist Icon” as published in the *JUMP+*:

> In Tracy Chapman’s “New Beginnings,” a song from her fantastic 1995 album New Beginnings, she laments that the “whole world’s broke and it ain’t worth fixing.” To that end, she sings that we need to begin society anew and “make new symbols, make new signs”---a sentiment that I often find myself agreeing with. A question arises, however, when I ponder this task: What do we do with the symbols and signs that were once useful, and will likely remain important, to the pre-reformation society? This question reveals that I---and, likely, others---have a sometimes embarrassing attachment to media that better critics may have left to rot in the putrid abyss of the past.

Gillette quotes from a “fantastic 1995 album,” which then led their “ponder[ing]” an idea. This question-and-answer method does not only quote the singer Tracy Chapman, it also explains why now thirty-year old lyrics matter to an intriguing contemporary question.

As another example, student Shota Maeda of Soka University of America published in *RhetTech Undergraduate Journal* that:

> Western Sydney University (2024) defines digital literacy as “having the skills you need to live, learn, and work in a society where communication and access to information is increasingly through digital technologies like internet platforms, social media, and mobile devices.” According to this definition, digital literacy has two aspects: communication and information. (44).

Maeda uses “according” to flag two “aspects” of the original definition, which never uses “aspects” as a word choice. Rather their text builds from the definition as published and inserting a new word pivots the text into a new direction. Other elements of the definition, such as “technologies” or “access,” simply do not continue forward as the explanation does not invoke them. In this sample of Madea’s text, the term “aspects” serves as a lens that guides the reader’s attention.

# Conclusion

As a college writer, you can quickly and easily access lots of information. There’s no shortage of noteworthy connections awaiting your attention. As you write new ideas, you bear the responsibility to share your thoughts ethically and eloquently. The issue becomes simply not whether to offer new ideas synthesized from existing sources, but how to do so effectively. Hopefully this piece offers sound advice for your journey as a college writer and beyond.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

Ideally, this topic would land in a workflow somewhere between finding sources for an expository writing assignment and undertaking deep revision.[^tr-1] It follows a recognizable sequence of first approaching the term in concept before moving into practice. Whenever possible, each stage benefits from experiential and collaborative practice and a focus on organization. Below, I offer guidance aligned with each stage (theoretical to productive) and encourage reflection throughout.

# Questions

1. How in prior writing assignments have you forged connections between sources? What do you feel went well? What feedback did you receive?

2. When you’ve worked with sources before, where do you feel you encounter the biggest challenges? Is it for instance interpreting the original source for possible contributions to a new argument (entering the matrix)? Perhaps you find the actual language you construct does not adequately guide your reader (ICE-ing sources)?

3. Have you found yourself “overquoting?” What does that term mean to you? What problems does it create in your writing?

4. When introducing an idea from another source, what are your favorite verbs? What do you like about them?

5. When seeking connections between sources, do you encounter any implicit or explicit biases that potentially influence your writing? What’s the nature of that influence? What steps can you take to manage or minimize biases in your synthesis writing?

## Develop a Matrix

Before a class session, provide students at least two sources as homework assignments. Direct them to annotate, summarize, and/or take notes on the readings before they arrive in class. Then, during class time, have each student complete a matrix with at least three rows. Depending on the level of the class, you may wish to provide the left-hand titles thereby requiring the students to respond to a specific prompt. Alternatively, especially in more advanced writing courses, students may self-select and define the left-hand titles for their matrix. In either case, ensure more than one source addresses the left-hand label. Then, place students into groups of 2-4. Direct them to read each other’s work and identify strengths only. Close with a reflective writing exercise, that in at least 100 words, discusses what they learned from the experience. You may then wish to share your observations with the class to reinforce what went well.

## Peer Review

Direct the students to bring two (2) physical copies of a work in progress. Ask them to read their own work, and flag where they synthesized sources. On a separate document, prompt them to free-write a self-assessment. Did they adequately introduce, cite, and explain their sources? Were their choices to paraphrase, quote, and paraphrase accurate and appropriate? Then ask peer reviewers to read works and independently identify sites of synthesis. Where this occurred, what did they like about how the author ICE’d their sources? Then have the students compare their notes and discuss their ideas in groups. Close by asking the students to reflect on where they agreed, where they disagreed, and what they learned from the experience.

## Understand Academic Honesty

Direct students to read “Did I Plagiarize? The Types and Severity of Plagiarism Violations” by Curtis Newbold (https://thevisualcommunicationguy.com/2014/09/16/did-i-plagiarize-the-types-and-severity-of-plagiarism-violations/). Ask them to note any information that surprises or is new to them. Place them in teams of 3-5 to compare notes and discuss: what two things did they learn about academic honesty from the infographic? Then lead a class discussion on the lessons learned by each group. This activity would work well as an introduction to discussing the expectations for full citation (MLA, APA, etc.) and reference list construction.
