# Introduction

As two college writing instructors, we ask our students, “What do you know about ChatGPT?”, initiating what will end up being an ongoing semester-long conversation about generative AI (GenAI) tools and writing. Before you read on, take a brief moment to inventory and think about what you know about ChatGPT. Recall your instructors’ policies about generative AI usage on their syllabi. What are they? Does each class have a policy? What are your general attitudes toward AI?

Whether we like it or not, we’ve all been thrown into this new and evolving generative AI landscape, and it isn’t going away anytime soon. IBM says GenAI will continue “to reshape the way we interact with technology and access information, making [it] a pivotal part of the modern digital landscape,” so it only makes sense that we take some time to understand what that landscape looks like and what interacting with it means for our own voices as writers and how they show up in our future personal and professional relationships and workforces. In this chapter, we will pull back the curtain on how GenAI writing tools like ChatGPT actually work. As we learn and become informed users, we can better evaluate our GenAI use, benefits, and what may be at stake when we use it for certain types of writing situations over others.

To start, Peter Elbow, a well-established Writing Studies scholar, offers an easy way of thinking about voice and writing. He says, “With practice, people can learn to write prose that ‘has a voice’ and ‘sounds like a person,’ and interestingly, when they do, their words are more effective at *carrying* *meaning*” (176). This is important to think about, especially when GenAI is thrown into the frame. We will establish later how human connection and understanding goes hand in hand with our writerly voice, and how ChatGPT may be good at generating language, but it has some glaring limitations when it comes to its own “voice” because of its inability to understand language like we as humans do.

In both the classroom and beyond, we are asking for you to engage in the process of writing and consider how your personal voice can lead you along unexpected, delightful paths. In “Large Language Models Write Answers,” professor and Writing Studies scholar, Annette Vee, reminds us that in composition, “We ask students to generate ideas, form questions, examine their own experiences, and ‘make productive use of uncertainty’” (177). Rather than seeking definitive answers in the early stages of a writing process, we are asking for that questioning, that uncertainty. S. Scott Graham, professor of rhetoric and writing, points out, too, “This is part of the reason [author] Ann Lamott’s “Shitty First Drafts” is such a popular assigned reading in writing education: It’s a colorful way to encourage students to get literally any text on paper so that they can actually start practicing the more robust and recursive revision/drafting practices that lead to high quality writing” (166--67). It is in the process that we discover ourselves, and it is in the process that we begin to uncover our unique voices and ways of approaching writing.

Before we jump in, we should lay a couple things out there:

1. We aren’t trying to dissuade you from using GenAI altogether. Rather, we wish to pull back the curtain a little and provide some insight on how the technology works. We seek to remind ourselves that GenAI isn’t a human, and therefore, cannot think like humans do, so we’ll consider what this means in our writing.

2. We will consider, too, the stakes associated with GenAI usage, such as its benefits and affordances, but also its disadvantages and things that may be lost. We ultimately argue for a writing approach that centers the agency and writerly voice that we bring to the process as humans.

# So What Is an LLM?

Before we move on, let’s take a look at how GenAI tools like ChatGPT work so we can better understand their capabilities and get a sense of how they produce language. When you enter a prompt into ChatGPT, it uses an algorithm to *predict* the word sequencing of the text that it will produce in response to the user input. It predicts these words using a large language model: an LLM. LLMs like ChatGPT generate text based on the data they are trained on. For example, Writing Studies professors and researchers, S. Scott Graham and Antonio Byrd, break it down to help us understand:

> The model uses complex algorithms and calculations to determine the likelihood of each possible continuation, based on the probabilities it has learned from the data. This process is often referred to as autoregression, because the model generates each new word or phrase based on the words that came before it in the text (Graham). ChatGPT, along with other LLMs, train on public content extracted from the Internet (called datasets or corpus texts) to learn the nuances and patterns of human languages (some can also train on datasets that contain millions of images to generate multimodal content and analyze multimodal inputs to generate text as output in response). As chatbots, LLMs seem to be an effective knowledge system and natural language processor to human users; however, they have really created mathematical formulas to predict the next token in a string of words from analyzing patterns of human language. They learn a form of language, but do not understand the implicit meaning behind it (Byrd).

Essentially, LLMs like ChatGPT, trained on massive amounts of data (more on this later), use mathematical formulas as a part of their text generation to predict which words will come next. But the relationship between machine and text does not include evaluation or understanding of such text. While we as humans can “make productive use of uncertainty” and engage in critical inquiry by asking and answering questions informed by our own knowledge and lived experiences, this is something an LLM can’t do (Vee, “Large Language Models Write Answers” 177; Jennings, Alex).” It doesn’t actually *understand* or *comprehend* ideas, concepts, relationships, and connections like we as humans do. It is important to understand the difference we are emphasizing between *production* and *understanding*. While the writing these LLMs produce may give the illusion that it is sentient and opinionated, it is not. It is only producing text using a prediction algorithm by piecing together existing information from the data that it has been trained on. And further, as Emily M. Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major, and Shmargaret Shmitchell remind us, “The Internet is a large and diverse virtual space, and accordingly, it is easy to imagine that very large datasets, such as Common Crawl (“petabytes of data collected over 8 years of web crawling,” a filtered version of which is included in the GPT-3 training data) must therefore be broadly representative of the ways in which different people view the world” (613). So, while we might be inclined to think that more data is better and may lead to more representation of all sorts of voices, it’s important to remember why this isn’t the case---the internet isn’t a space free from racism and underrepresentation, and neither are LLMs.

# What Does My Voice Have to Do with AI?

Perhaps one of the most important things to remember as we continue to think about how voice and GenAI are related to our writerly voices is that we have to think about writing as more than just a means to an end. Rather, it’s a tool to help sharpen our abilities to read and think critically, and to understand ourselves and the world around us. We know that “writing helps writers develop knowledge about themselves, their interests, their goals, and how they fit into the world around them, [and that] writing contributes to personal agency” (Glenn Lester, Taylor Lucas, Sydney Doyle, and Alison Overcash 4; see Writing Spaces Volume 5 for their full chapter), so when we think about the relationship between voice, writing, and GenAI, we might start by examining a seemingly simple question: What does it mean to have a voice, and why is this important within our writing?

To tackle this question in our classrooms, we start with three framing texts: Jacqueline Jones Royster’s “When the First Voice You Hear Is Not Your Own,” Amy Tan’s “Mother Tongue,” and Annette Vee’s “Why ‘Just Right’ Is Wrong,” each of which grapple with the stakes involved when our voices are diminished. For Royster and Tan, the question of voice often starts with not being heard for a number of reasons, including outward societal perceptions that are influenced by racial and gender-related biases.

In “Mother Tongue,” Tan, a Chinese-American writer, reflects on her mother’s (who immigrated from China to the US) relationship to language, both how she perceives her mother and how society perceives her mother’s use of language. She does this through the use of examples from her life, such as her mother clashing with an American stockbroker and the embarrassment she felt as a child when she had to translate for her mother. “Like others,” Tan writes, “I have described [my mother’s language] as ‘broken’ or ‘fractured’ English. But I wince when I say that. It has always bothered me that I can think of no way to describe it other than ‘broken,’ as if it were damaged and needed to be fixed, as if it lacked a certain wholeness and soundness” (2). Over time, Tan understood her mother’s use of language as more than just what she and others heard. It was her mother’s personal way of speaking, her *voice.*

Similarly, Royster, a Black scholar, echoes Tan’s experiences about how non-dominant voices and languages are perceived as lesser or incorrect by the white-dominant standard of English. In her essay, she shares three scenes from her life that express different ways of being heard in such environments. In the first scene, Royster is spoken for; she is not heard at all. In scene two, when she is heard, she is not *listened to.* The third scene carries particular weight, as it joins together the themes of the first. In the third scene, Royster has presented at a speaking event, and after, an audience member approached her to congratulate her on the talk:

> [The woman said], “How wonderful it was that you were willing to share with us your ‘authentic’ voice!” I said, “My ‘authentic’ voice?” She said, “Oh yes! I’ve never heard you talk like that, you know, so relaxed. I mean, you’re usually great, but this was really great. You weren’t so formal. You didn’t have to speak in an appropriated academic language. You sounded ‘natural.’ It was nice to hear you be yourself.” I said, “Oh, I see. Yes, I do have a range of voices, and I take quite a bit of pleasure actually in being able to use any of them at will.” Not understanding the point that I was trying to make gently, she said, “But this time, it was really you. Thank you.” (36-37)

The other woman had formed an opinion about how she believed Royster *should* sound as a Black woman, and when Royster’s speech in this particular setting matched the woman’s stereotyped perception, she praised Royster for it. In reality, as Royster points out, she has “a range of voices” and she values being able to shift between them based on her purpose and audience. Rather than “appropriat[ing] academic language,” as the audience member believes, Royster is utilizing her range of voices, and her essay calls to mind what it means for us to be heard and really listened to when we speak in a multitude of ways that feel comfortable and authentic to us based on where and to whom we speak.

*Take a moment to pause and consider your own voice. When do you feel like* *your voice is most heard whether that be in conversation or in your writing?*

When posed to our students, they shared that they feel most heard at times when people give them verbal and nonverbal listening cues, offering things like head nods and eye contact. Others mentioned they felt most heard in one-on-one or small group environments, suggesting connection was important to feel they were being taken seriously. These types of rhetorical transactions are important to keep in mind as we consider what we value within our own voices.

Other times, however, we might feel as though our voices are restricted in ways that are similar to those that Tan and Royster experience. Royster’s essay illustrates how her own voice is heard in academic spaces as a Black woman, further speaking to the fact that marginalized people, particularly people of color, are often expected to negotiate their voices and reduce themselves to a predetermined standardization of whiteness (Kristen DeMint Bailey, An Ha, and AJ Outlar; see Writing Spaces Volume 5 for their full Chapter). Bailey et al. add that “[m]any people don’t notice a voice in academic writing because the voice that is there has been normalized and disembodied,” meaning that voices often become flattened into such a standard in academic writing (64). Consequently, adhering to this standard erases more than an author’s style; it erases one’s voice (Bailey et al. 64).

We might be inclined to think that because an LLM is trained on vast amounts of data that include a number of voices, it must be able to avoid defaulting to one white-centric, standard academic voice, right? Well, more data isn’t always better. In fact, Bender et al. tell us, “In all cases, the voices of people most likely to hew to a hegemonic viewpoint are also more likely to be retained [in LLM training]. In the case of US and UK English, this means that white supremacist and misogynistic, ageist, etc. views are overrepresented in the training data, not only exceeding their prevalence in the general population but also setting up models trained on these datasets to further amplify biases and harms”(613). When we do use LLMs, we should think about whose voices are missing from the training data as we evaluate the responses it outputs. Even when marginalized peoples contribute toward digitized spaces, their voices and contributions may not be included in the body of texts most studied in Westernized, white-centric spaces “because they lack enough incoming and outgoing links to show up in [these] corpus texts” (Byrd 136). Examining GPT-4’s responses, Byrd notes the way that it adheres to “standard American English,” and such outputs reinforce conceptions of a “correct” English, rather than one that is rich in voice and reflecting the multitude of Englishes that persist throughout the world.

The last piece we discuss with our students, Annette Vee’s “Why ‘Just Right’ Is Wrong,” helps to join together the scholarship here by pointing to Google’s ad for their LLM, Gemini. During the 2024 Summer Olympics, the tech giant aired a short video where a father described his daughter’s wish to write a fan letter to her favorite athlete, Sydney McLaughlin-Levrone. Throughout the video, the father speaks for his daughter; never once do we hear the young girl speak, and when it comes time to write the fan letter, the father sees Gemini as the perfect tool to convey his daughter’s love for Sydney. In her essay, Vee asks, “What’s the point of using AI to write here [in the context of a little girl’s fan letter]?”

> Let’s take a moment to imagine ourselves as a famous athlete in Sydney’s position. We’ve received fan mail from a young girl, and when we open it, we find a typed letter. How might we feel about such a letter? Does it feel like it has the voice of a child? Does it include all the misspellings and creative syntax that we might expect? What about drawings or crossed out words? In the example of a fan letter, we would hope to see the human and all its messiness and genuine enthusiasm.

It is important to use our voices to create connections and ignite meaningful change. We should recognize that LLMs are limited in such a capacity, and we should be aware of such limitations as we consider our writing intentions and the places/situations where we would especially want our voices to be heard.

# My Favorite Artist Is . . .

So far, we’ve looked at how LLMs work and how our voices appear in writing, and now we’d like to experiment. As you read along, we invite you to take a few minutes (we gave our students ten) to respond to the following prompt about your favorite musician to get a sense of what is distinct about your own voice and how certain characteristics may be different than that of an LLM like ChatGPT. After you respond to the prompt and continue to read on, we’ll invite you, like we did our own students, to input the same prompt into a LLM of your choice (we used ChatGPT) and ask it to tell you why your favorite artist is the best. We’ll make observations about your and the LLM’s responses and continue to think about writerly voice.

To begin, please respond the prompt below:

> *Tell me why your favorite artist is such an amazing musician. Give* *examples. You can approach the prompt however you wish, but make* *it personal and highlight the elements of your chosen artist that make* *them the most special to you, whether that lies in the realm of their* *talent, their performances on stage, interviews, etc. We’re essentially asking you to advocate for your artist as if you were recommending them.*

After responding to the prompt, take note and think about your tone, style, the formality of the language you used, the memories and personal experiences you chose to weave in.

Our students pointed out distinct elements related to their audience, tone, and structure. One student said they always try to incorporate humor or satire into their writing, no matter what it is they’re writing about, always using witty and snappy prose. Others pointed out that they were naturally writing informally and that the tone of their responses felt casual and conversational. Another common element across a number of responses was the way they wrote about their favorite artists. Many of the students included memories and explained the feelings the artist’s music elicited and recalled how the music influenced parts of their lives, making for a variety of chronological approaches and structures.

For example, in my response (Alex), I wrote about Chappel Roan and the first time I heard “Pink Pony Club.” I was in my garage and literally just parked my tractor after cutting the grass on a really hot July day. The song elicited so much queer joy (and pain) and was just such a good time. I opened my response with “My favorite artist is your favorite artist’s favorite artist,” (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L94zTNhLnXo&t=229s) which famously circulated on TikTok after Jimmy Fallon asked her about the statement which she explained was a play on Sasha Colby saying “I’m your favorite drag queen’s favorite drag queen,” further adding a layer of queer whimsy. Many of these characteristics closely aligned with things the students valued when it came to identifying when they felt their writerly voices were most apparent and heard.

Now we invite you to interact with a LLM. Open a new browsing tab and navigate to your chosen LLM. Input our initial prompt, and ask it to tell you why the artist you chose to write about is its favorite. Once you’ve done this, read its response and compare it to yours. Take note of the things we identified in our own written responses like tone, audience, chronology, structure, and style. What did it do well? What didn’t it do well? Did anything feel odd about it? Ask it about another artist or two and take a look at what it says about them.

Perhaps unsurprisingly, our own students noticed that it did a number of things well. It completed the task and responded to the prompt, it gave a coherent amount of information in an order that made sense to the reader, and it offered clear, concise summaries. The students said it was grammatically sound and presented well-constructed sentences. (These sentences, of course, mostly adhere to Standard American English, unless prompted otherwise.) However, there were some glaring issues.

Several students said it responded with hits, singles, and lyrics that were made up and didn’t exist. They pointed out that the tone was flat, boring, and felt odd and forced. One student said when he prompted it further to “respond as if you were a college student” the tone got worse in some ways because it was trying to use words that were “cool” and he commented that nobody really talks like that or uses the words in that way. The responses were also void of emotion and personal connection--a piece that the students said was important and the main reason they were drawn to their favorite musician in the first place.

Students also noticed that ChatGPT’s responses were standardized, always followed the same structure, and used the same repetitive adjectives, concluding that there was a major lack of emotion, uniqueness, and personal connection across the text that GPT generated. In my (Megan) example that I modeled for my students, I examined these moments of repetition and made-up songs. When a LLM produces information that is inaccurate or misleading, this is known as a “hallucination.” This happens because the LLM does not know what is true; remember, it is only producing predicted text based on the data it has been trained on. Writing about the lead singer of the band Motionless in White, Chris Motionless, I shared my personally written response with my students, and followed with a preview of what GPT wrote about Motionless. In the figure below, I highlighted themes that continuously appeared no matter how many questions I prompted GPT with, and I noticed that the responses often contained hallucinations, like the incorrect song and album pairing below, as well as other instances where GPT produced themes for music videos that didn’t quite match.

In examining the differences between their writing and that of ChatGPT’s generated content, students asked questions that were informed both by the activity and the Royster, Tan, and Vee readings, like:

- How does identity affect one’s writing?
- How do we negotiate the privilege of interpretation?
- How do societal pressures impact the authenticity of one’s voice?
- How can AI be an ethical tool to help people through the barrier of entry into academic spaces without stifling individual voices?
- Who controls language and meaning and what does it mean when a certain voice has privilege over others?

As you reflect on this activity, you may notice, like our students, that there were plenty of instances where ChatGPT could certainly be useful. We determined it could be helpful for organizing our ideas, brainstorming, and assisting with things like planning and scheduling--all of which are writing related tasks.

However, when we dig deeper into what we value about writing, we are left with an intriguing knot of questions, like those from our students. While GenAI can certainly function as a multiuse tool for things in and around the writing process, we hope this conversation illuminates the importance of our role as humans in the process. Understanding how GenAI tools like ChatGPT work, even at the most basic level, begins to accomplish this illumination, but it also invites us to raise serious questions about what it is we value as writers and as writing instructors. If ChatGPT and other language models can easily mimic emotional and believable stories about human experiences, to what extent is replacing such narratives beneficial? Does outsourcing certain inquiry-based pieces of the writing process actually make us more efficient writers or thinkers? We imagined this to be a jumping off point to generate questions and conversations about the objectives we hold as we engage in writing related tasks amidst the changing AI landscape. We hope, too, that this chapter has given you additional insight into your own writerly voice and how voice is entwined with our complex, human experiences and the meanings that we make out of it in light of generative AI. Our writing, in general, is heightened by this subjectivity, by our *voice.*

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

This chapter was largely informed by our curiosity about the ongoing conversations about generative AI’s place in Composition Classrooms.[^tr-1] We were influenced by the Text Gen Ed (https://wac.colostate.edu/news/textgened-collection-released/) series, a collection of lessons, materials, and activities that interact with digital tools and writing technologies. While we’ve been thinking about how to incorporate discussion about AI ethics and use into our classrooms for awhile now (and even had some approaches that were flops), we wanted to co-create an activity that would help ease some of the anxiety and confusion that we have heard students talking about in relation to GenAI. It was important to us that we pull the curtain back for our students on how GenAI operates, because most of our students didn’t understand how it works. We felt that voice would be a good vehicle for that because it highlights what we value about writing--our humanness, relationships, creativity, and so much more.

We would also like to add that we believe it is important to weigh both the risks and benefits associated with GenAI tools, and we imagined this activity as a way to encourage ourselves and our students to think about AI use in larger contexts through writing. We see this as an activity that can be easily modified to fit the theme of most writing classes, and as a tool to facilitate ongoing course conversations about Voice & Power, and as a way for students to become more informed and aim to interact with new writing related technologies in a sensible way.

# Discussion Questions

The following questions work best if students have first read Annette Vee’s “Why ‘Just Right’ Is Wrong” and after completing the activity described in the “My Favorite Artist Is…” section and further detailed below in the “Activities” resource. These are meant to stimulate a conversation about the student’s feelings toward both AI and their own writing.

1. In “Why ‘Just Right’ Is Wrong,” Annette Vee notes that the little girl, who is supposed to be the author of the fan letter to her favorite athlete, has no voice in Google’s Olympic Gemini ad. Her father speaks for her, and then, Gemini speaks for the father. After completing the voice and AI activity above, ask yourself how ChatGPT’s description of your favorite artist parallels this issue. What parts of your “voice” are erased in its description?

2. Vee also asks, “What’s the point of using AI to write here [in the context of a little girl’s fan letter]?” What would be the point of using AI to write about your favorite artist? Think about the tension between purpose and audience here.

3. Vee notes, “Crucially, Gemini and other commercial LLMs are tuned to produce what’s sometimes called ‘Standard English,’ or even ‘Standard American English,’ since the leading AI companies are based in the US. This form of English dominates formal education and is often perceived to be ‘right.’” Consider how your paragraph might deviate from “Standard American English.” What makes these deviations so compelling? What is lost when writing strictly adheres to ChatGPT’s English?

4. Lastly, Vee writes, “[I worry t]hat AI will flatten the diversity of writing expression.” How does AI flatten your writing expression in this exercise? The next set of questions are ones developed by students during the

ChatGPT activity:

1. How does identity affect one’s writing?

2. How do we negotiate the privilege of interpretation?

# Activities

The following section includes the materials we used to help students learn about writerly voice and ChatGPT. We both delivered this lesson in Seminar in Composition First Year Writing classes over the course of one week (Megan across three 50-minute MWF sections and Alex across two 75-minute MW sections), and here, we’ve included a breakdown of what this can look like.

Our activity was a two-part activity that involved responding to a prompt about our favorite musicians in different ways that would build from the conversations about voice from Amy Tan’s “Mother Tongue”, Jacqueline Jones Royster’s “When the First Voice you Hear is Not Your Own” and Annette Vee’s “Why ‘Just Right’ Is Wrong,” that we discussed in the chapter. Part 1 involved the students’ hand-written response to a prompt about their favorite artist followed by a large group discussion so we could practice identifying unique attributes related to their writerly voices and consider how we and others show up in our writing through relationship and connection. Part 2 involved asking the students what they knew about generative AI tools as we transitioned into a lesson about how LLMs (specifically ChatGPT) work. This lesson was intended to pull the curtain back so the students could have a basic understanding of how ChatGPT functions and they could be better equipped to make informed decisions when they choose to interact with it. We asked the students to have ChatGPT generate a response and tell them why the artist or musician they chose was the best. After toying with ChatGPT, we arranged them into small groups of 3-4 and asked them to begin to analyze its language generation ability. We made a list of the things they noticed it did well and not so well on the dry-erase board to further discuss.

## The Prompt

Tell me why your favorite artist is such an amazing musician. Give examples. You can approach the prompt however you wish, but make it personal and highlight the elements of your chosen artist that make them the most special to you, whether that lies in the realm of their talent, their performances on stage, interviews, etc. We’re essentially asking you to advocate for your artist as if you were recommending them.

## In Sum

This activity was intended to create a connection to the students’ own experiences related to voice and the larger discussion and analysis, highlighting the inequities we discussed in the readings, and those that exist across digital spaces.
