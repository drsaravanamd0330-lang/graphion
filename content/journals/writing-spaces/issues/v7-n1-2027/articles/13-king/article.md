So many years later, I remember sitting in my professor’s office, absolutely flummoxed with her feedback on my essay. I was writing about how the Dixie Chicks’ song, *Goodbye Earl*, brought attention to the issue of domestic violence. Unfortunately, my professor had just told me that my paper was not, in fact, persuasive about the role of the song in sparking attention. I needed to hugely revise my approach:

> “You’ve got to make me care about these facts. Aren’t you angry or upset that 1 in 4 women experience domestic violence in the US? Don’t just give me a bunch of facts about domestic violence.

> Maybe, use the story of Wanda and Marianne and why they felt they had to kill Earl to bring some life to these statistics.”

Reader, I had no idea what to do with that feedback. Of course, I was “angry and upset” about how often women experience domestic violence---that’s one of the reasons I chose the paper topic! But why did it matter what I *felt* about it? Didn’t the facts speak for themselves? Isn’t that why we use statistics---to show the impact and how widespread an issue is?

My experience is not uncommon. In fact, you might be agreeing because “Well, yeah, I was always told to ignore how I felt about information and focus upon what the information says.” Or you might think: “But isn’t that how you avoid being biased?” But it’s not quite that simple. In general, we’ve been taught to think of our feelings and emotions as separate from cognition (which is how we process information, how we think). But emotions and cognition are never really separate. The research on how to write tends to emphasize cognitive processes and tries to offer discrete steps that most writers take. However, these steps always include feeling and emotional elements. For instance, if you feel a lot of anxiety about a writing project, research has shown that you’re more likely to experience writer’s block or have trouble making writing decisions about your project.

Unfortunately, we often don’t think of recognizing our emotions and feelings as important to the process of finding and reading sources. After all, have you ever seen a multiple-choice question on a reading quiz that said something like, “pick the emotion you had when you read [whatever] . . .”? Honestly, that’d be a weird question because we don’t all have the same responses to information, so how could you pick the right one? But this doesn’t mean we should just ignore our feelings and emotions when we read. Ignoring these parts of our experiences inhibits us when we need to explore why we feel that “something matters” or we need to wrestle with becoming angry (or otherwise emotional) as we learn about an issue. Ignoring our feelings and emotions can create blocks in the reading-writing process where we can’t effectively harness our own positions and experiences.

# How Feelings and Emotions Create “Sticky Connections”

Researchers have been exploring dimensions of how feelings or emotions impact us for decades. I’m going to focus on two terms that I’ll use as I discuss reading: *feeling* and *emotion*. When we read texts, we might describe how we feel about them, or even the emotions we experience as we read. For example, we might describe how re-reading a favorite novel feels comforting; or maybe, how reading a news article describing political unrest makes you angry about the injustice in the world that the article brings to your awareness. Emotional experiences can be intense like when we experience anger, fear, or joy, but our feelings can also be more subtle for things like agreement, recognition, or a sense that something is useful.

Essentially, what is sometimes referred to by researchers as the affective (or the non-cognitive) domain is made up of lots of different kinds of experiences. Laura Micciche, a writing scholar who researches how emotion impacts writing and the teaching of writing, suggests that we think of feelings as our individual bodily experiences, while emotions are experienced “… *in relation*---between people within a particular context” (Micciche, 11). Essentially, emotions have deeper, communal aspects to how we understand them. While feelings and emotions are often connected in how we talk about them, we tend to recognize a feeling in our body whereas emotion has the potential “to perform meanings and to stand as a marker for meanings as they get performed” (14). The emotion is “more than just” a feeling of it; it’s more meaningful. In my examples above, you can see a version of this when rereading evokes *a feeling of* personal comfort. As we read a text, we have feelings that correspond to how we’re receiving information and engaging it. However, sometimes our response to a text is more than just a feeling---it forms “in relation” with other beliefs and people to perform more complex social meaning.

Basically, I want you to start thinking of how feelings and emotions come with what theorist Sara Ahmed evocatively describes as “sticky-ness” (45). Ahmed believes that emotions create “sticky” connective fibers between us and different objects or texts. This stickiness is not a property of the text or object (or even of us) but accumulates through time and contexts; it is the circulation of the object, persons, and the feelings/emotions associated individually and culturally that makes “stickiness.” Our job is to unpack and critically engage with that stickiness and how it can shape relationships and actions. When I’m asking you to recognize the emotions or feelings of your reading experience, I’m doing so because we want to pay attention to the *stickiness* between us and different texts. Recognizing feelings and emotions will allow us to explore *why* we have these responses and learn to use them in our own writing. As we go through the research process, we need to recognize when ideas or examples “stick” around as we think more about our topics.

When we refuse to admit how certain sources or facts were particularly “sticky” for us, we remove these felt connections from the process of writing papers. Sometimes, this stickiness is a feeling that makes meaning individually important; other times, this stickiness stems from those emotional resonances related to greater “relational” contexts. My Dixie Chicks example illustrates this: when I was a freshman writing that paper, I had just learned about issues of domestic violence in my family history; this was probably why I had such a strong *feeling* about “Goodbye Earl.” If I had recognized how my feelings of anger and disbelief created a “sticky connection” between myself and the song, I may have been better able to create a successful paper that didn’t just list facts but “brought some life to them,” to paraphrase my instructor.

Our role as readers is not merely to recognize feelings or emotional cues so that we can resist them. Most students have only experienced discussing emotion elicited by a text as a minor part of a rhetorical analysis exercise---perhaps you’ve been asked to examine how a text uses *pathos*, for example. Such exercises tend to encourage us to see emotion and feelings as forms of manipulation (ruining our logical reasoning). But we need to stop being so suspicious of emotion and break its opposition to rational thought in our collective mind. If we recognize emotional cues as the sticky connections that occur throughout our research process because they clue us in to the connections we form between our experiences, our perspective, and our reading of our sources, we can more productively harness the energy that these connections showcase.

# How to Monitor “Sticky” Reading Experiences

Many reading scholars and theorists have focused on trying to explain how readers create meaning as they peruse texts (Carillo; Sullivan; Mc-Cormick). In general, these scholars agree that reading is a dynamic, transactional activity (Rosenblatt, xiv). The reader is the active shaper of the meaning they are creating when they read a text---a text is not pouring information into the reader. Rather, the reader’s understanding of the text occurs because of a mix of what the text says, the reader’s own prior experiences, and contextual knowledge of the topic which influence how the reader understands and remembers what the text says. Figure 1 shows this interactive process for reading.

Figure 1. Three overlapping circles illustrate how reading comprehension occurs at the overlap of reader, context, and text. Figure created by Carolyne M. King.

Sometimes, the meaning may be impacted by one of these factors more than another. For example, if you’re reading the manual of a car to learn what a dashboard symbol means, the text probably influences your understanding the most. In contrast, if you’re reading a self-help book, your own experiences and feelings are probably most influential in how you are understanding the information. Although text, context, and the reader all influence the reading, these factors may not always influence the meaning being made *equally*. Tracking our emotions and feelings as we read can help us to better understand “why” we understand a text the way we do.

Here’s a description of how I approach reading a text and keep track of how I’m feeling as I engage the text during my reading experience. Generally, I use annotations and even emojis (☺) or things like “!” or “?” to mark what I feel at certain places.

# An Example of Tracing Your Feelings During Reading

For this example, I’m reading “How to Read like a Writer” by Michael Bunn---another *Writing Spaces* chapter.

1. I begin with a check in: *How am I feeling?* Today, I’m feeling tired as I’ve been working for about 2.5 hours already. I remind myself to try to give it a good effort for 30 minutes, and then I can get a Diet Coke as a reward for being focused.

2. Then, I conduct a brief survey of the text: *What does it look like* *overall?* 17 pages! As I scroll down the PDF, I notice that the headings are logical---first explaining the approach, then “why learn to read like a writer” (75). *Okay, it’s going to persuade me to* *do this thing, I think.* My reading begins with the title: “How to Read Like a Writer” by

Mike Bunn. *Sounds like some directions.* My interest is piqued by the first sentence: “In 1997, I was a recent college graduate living in London for six months and working at the Palace Theatre…” (71). I pause, and re-read. *1997!? ---that’s kind of long ago. And London---must be nice! I wonder how* *he ended up there.*

I’m interested in how Bunn starts his chapter with personal details. But, as Figure 2 shows, you might even see my comments as slightly hostile. After all, I’m still tired and a bit frustrated that I *still* have more work to complete for class.

![A page from Mike Bunn’s essay How to Read Like a Writer with three passages highlighted in yellow and a short typed comment in the left margin beside each; the passages and comments are given in full below.](assets/figure-p007-1.png)\
Figure 2. Image of annotated page showing 3 highlighted parts and 3 brief marginal comments. Image created by Carolyne M. King.

There’s nothing overly special in the annotations shown in Figure 2 above. My comments show that I’m mildly curious about the details that Bunn relates---the time and setting are certainly unusual. Of course, feeling interested in such details means it’s easier to connect with the text and stay focused upon it. This certainly benefits any reader who is feeling tired (like I was) as they get started. My biggest point of personal feelings is seen in the comment “I’d hate this” about using the flashlight to read.

As you read, I am asking you to actively focus on recording your natural responses to the text---pushing yourself to document the feelings and emotions you have (no matter how seemingly inconsequential). I’m asking you to particularly recognize *feelings* like increased interest, sympathy for a boring job experience, confusion, uncertainty, disbelief, discounting an example, etc. and to make notes that will help you recognize and remember that part of the experience. Recognizing the feelings and emotions that are already part of your reading experience provides a roadmap for you to revisit your sources. It can help you trace the “sticky connection” you might have to a particular example or statistic or even argument. When writing with sources, it’s important to have a way to sort through all the different facts and information you’ve learned. Being able to find places that engage you (and then, figure out *why*) is what “Reading with your Feelings” helps you to do.

# How Feelings Can Impact Source Selection

Feelings and emotions don’t only occur during the reading experience. Learning to recognize our feelings and emotions as you search for and encounter texts, helps you be more careful in selecting sources that work for your purposes. Recognizing the initial feeling you have about a possible source can help you think more critically about your assumptions about the quality, credibility, or even, if it contributes helpful information on the topic. Much of the way students are often taught to evaluate sources focuses upon specific (logical) categories---things like the credibility of an author, or how timely the information is. These are good qualities to pay attention to, but you also need to develop flexible ways to think about what a source might bring to you and why: why might you have felt a spark of interest for a story, for example, even if it’s “not credible” by traditional metrics?

Let me give you an example, drawing upon Sarah Singer’s insightful argument for better attending to what she calls “wild card sources,” or sources that focus upon “emerg[ing], contested, or under-explored topics” (154). “Wild card” sources are primarily websites that are broadly compelling and attract a lot of users. Singer uses Lyme disease as a relevant and easily understood example for her readers of how these sources work. First, she shows how the CDC website is a good, credible source of information that would be helpful in defining and understanding the disease. However, this website falls short of patients’ needs for understanding treatment, especially for a chronic experience of Lyme disease. Chronic patients might be better off reading Dr. Joseph Jemsek’s website, which is an example of a wild-card source. Although a commercial site that advertises his practice, it also serves as a patient-focused hub of information and community. Importantly, patients can connect with other patients and read their stories and testimonials which might be the kinds of information that are most valuable to a patient seeking explanation for their symptoms. As Singer argues, wild card sources like Jemsek’s website illustrate the kinds of sites and information-uses that require students to develop new ways to understand source credibility and its relationship to source-use (169-170). In this case, users must evaluate how and why they are connecting to the information offered---essentially, how they feel about it, or the emotions they experience from engaging with it. In this example, a feeling of recognition for a shared experience is a key element that makes this a useful and credible source.

Students need to recognize the emotions and feelings that are part of their engagements with sources. Don’t see your feelings and emotions as bad, irrational, or something that needs to be risen above. Rather, learn to reflect on “what” you are experiencing and “why” you are experiencing it. If you can understand your feelings about the source, you can then consider how these feelings impact your plan for how you’ll use the source in your papers.

# Example: A Need to Engage Feelings to Write with Sources

I worked with a student, Evan,[^a1] whose situation shows why it’s so important to be aware of your feelings and emotions about the sources you’ve read. Evan’s paper explored artificial intelligence and whether popular culture’s “fear of A.I.” was scientifically supported. (Evan was writing in 2017---so this is before the introduction of AI into our everyday life!) His enthusiasm for the topic is reflected in the 13 sources he cited, ranging from data about self-driving cars and car crash statistics to a computer science textbook. As he writes in his conclusion, “What were once the musings of modern mathematicians and science fiction writers, has become a reality to every person on the globe in the 21st century.” Overall, Evan wrote a successful research paper.

Despite his enthusiasm, Evan’s paper had some issues---especially when he worked with the source that he described as his “most informative” source in his interview. For example, his first body paragraph is *2.5-pages* *long (795 words!)* in which he heavily quotes and summarizes from a single source: a computer science textbook called *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern* *Approach.* Evan’s instincts---that his reader needed to understand what AI is and how it is defined---were correct. However, his attempt to provide that information to his reader was boring because he failed to engage his reader in learning this important definition.

When we write research papers, our use of sources often reflects the emotional engagement we had when reading that source---bigger feelings or emotions can be mirrored by a larger use of the source. Indeed, Evan described the textbook as his most informational source, and he certainly writes “the most” with it! Unfortunately, he didn’t use his feelings well and instead just blasted his readers with an overload of facts. If Evan had carefully reflected on “why” he had such a strongly positive feeling toward the source, he might have been able to recognize that reading this source early in his research process helped him to solidify his understanding of a key element of his topic and to feel more confident in his understanding. Once you recognize the role of feelings and emotions in shaping your perspective on a source, you can think more critically about how to use them as part of writing with the source.

If we recognize this connection, we can start to identify “how we are responding” to a source more intentionally. Rather than our feelings toward the source remaining private and only part of our reading experience, we need to more pointedly engage these feelings, be able to describe them, and to consider how they influence our intentions with using our sources.

# How to Create Reader-Based Source Integrations Using your Feelings

We need to better interrogate our feelings and emotions when reading our sources and we need to carefully consider how our own responses can be sites of connection and engagement. But how can we pass along our feelings or emotions about the source, as part of how we use it in our research papers? First, we need to see these experiences when reading as the “sticky connections” between us, our source, and the reader of our research paper. Our source integration can showcase our feelings and emotions from our experience reading and thinking about the source; it can encourage the reader to share that same feeling or emotion.

An orange arrow points to an orange ring around the overlap from a box that reads: Our source integration needs to showcase the feelings and emotions entangled in reading.](assets/figure-p011-1.png)\
Figure 3. Three overlapping circles to showcase the connection between the reader, the source, and our feelings while reading the source. Figure created by Carolyne M. King.

Writing with feeling about your sources, however, is not merely using emotionally charged language. You don’t want to introduce sources with descriptors like:

> I read this very informative source, [TITLE], which is a textbook that reviewed everything you need to know about [TOPIC].

Or

> This horrible article provides gory details about the disgusting impact of . . .

Both of these example statements use emotional language that shows a “sticky connection” that the author experienced. However, neither really converts that emotional experience into something that can persuade the reader. The mere presence of emotional language about the source is not what you’re going for here.

Your instructor might have introduced you to the idea of “reader-based prose” (compared to writer-based prose). Reader-based prose creates a shared context between the reader and the author; texts that use reader-based prose are usually easy to read and comprehend. In contrast, texts that use writer-based prose prioritize the author’s thought process. When I talk to my own students about reader-based prose vs writer-based prose, I explain it as transitioning from early drafting, where you are using writer-based prose and working out your own thoughts and ideas, to getting close to reader-based prose which is the final product---where you know what you want to say, and you’re now focused upon how your reader will understand you.

When we integrate sources, we might start with writer-based prose which prioritizes our feelings about the source. Early drafts, using writer-based prose, might intentionally record our emotional engagement for how it influenced our choice to use the source:

> “I really liked [TITLE] because it helped me to understand [TOPIC] especially in the clear example it provides about [X]”

As we transition into using reader-based prose, we want to keep the notion of the clearly explained connection between us, the text, and why we chose it. However, we must write this in a way that will help our reader appreciate the credibility and usefulness of the source. Remember, your reader won’t have read the source; they’re learning about that experience second-hand from you. Thus, you need to subtly create emotional cues:

> [TITLE] helpfully reviews [TOPIC]. In particular, the detailed explanation of…

Or

> By providing disgustingly gory descriptions of [TOPIC], [TITLE] encourages us to . . .

In the first example, the adverb “helpfully” subtly suggests the evaluation of the text as well as some of the reasons for the excitement over that emotional response to ‘finally learning the needed information’ for the paper. In the second example, emphasizing *why* the details were provided to cue a particular experience for the reader (that the author had), allows the author to both introduce their feeling of disgust as well as the efficacy of this approach to shaping their understanding of the topic.

Probably, when you’ve learned source integration previously, you’ve been told to use a signal phrase to ensure that your reader knows that the material you’re referencing comes from another author. You may have even received a handout listing common “signal verbs” to encourage you to use more precise verbs than merely ones like “talks about” or “discusses” that are rather vague in the relationship they signal to the reader. When you’re selecting your signal verbs, think about how the verb might help establish the ‘sticky connection’ that you experienced with the source. You might also find that you can be a bit more specific about what drew you to the source, and why/how you are drawing your reader’s attention to it---as the examples above show. Certainly, you can do more than merely introduce the source’s author and provide a neutral sentence reviewing information.

Drawing upon feelings and emotions creates “human connections” in writing---and isn’t that the point of persuading your reader? We are not automatons, regurgitating information and moving it from one source-bucket to our own papers. Integrating sources in ways that show how you felt about the source, or which emphasize emotions experienced while reading or thinking with the source, will help you to more persuasively engage your reader.

# Using Your Feelings as You Write: Some Suggestions

When you write with sources, think about your job as encouraging your reader to appreciate the work and intelligence you’re bringing to the subject. Your emotions and feelings about the text have already influenced the essay you’re writing! By providing emotional cues for your reader, you bring them into sharing in your perspective. It might take some time to get used to this emphasis on tracking your feelings and emotions, but working to include more emotional cues in your source integration will help you to write more engagingly with your sources.

Think of this as a three-step process:

1. Track your emotions via annotation as you read. Just like I showed in my example with Bunn, reading and actively noting down how you are responding to a text is important. It’s not enough to merely highlight sentences that caught your eye because you won’t remember “why” later on! Instead, get into the habit of consistently writing out your response.

2. Write reflective notes about your sources and how you feel about them. Taking just a few moments to capture your feelings about a reading will make it easier to sort them out as you try to figure out which sources are most important for you to use. When you write these brief reflective notes, you can start to tease out what your feelings are about engaging with the text, and your feelings about how the text supports your project. Think: *what most stood out to* *me from the source, and why? What’s “sticky” about the source?*

3. Draft using emotional language about your sources. You don’t have to gush about how much you *loved* or *hated* a source, but you reader shouldn’t question that you have a perspective on it! You might even re-read your brief notes (see #2 above) and try to weave in some of the feelings about the source from your descriptions. Subtle displays of feelings and emotions can help your reader appreciate the engagement you had with the source. * Not every source requires you to think deeply about your feelings or

emotional connection to it; but certainly, spending the time to integrate your favorite source in your paper more intentionally (to share that experience with your reader) will improve your writing. And in the meantime, you can practice monitoring how you respond to the use (or lack thereof) of feeling descriptions around sources. In fact, if you review my use of sources in this chapter, you might find that some are more important and interesting to me, than others---and you can tell by how descriptively I integrate them.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategy

This chapter can be a helpful bridge that connects reading with integrating sources into an essay. Instructors might assign this chapter when students have found sources but need additional guidance and attention to how they are reading these sources.[^tr-1] Students need to more critically reflect on their process of reading and using sources---and especially, learn to monitor their feelings and emotions as they read so as to effectively use them in their writing. As the example of Evan in the chapter shows, students may struggle to effectively use sources in their research papers even when they have 1) selected a credible, useful source, 2) read it carefully and critically, and 3) have a sense of purpose for integrating the source into their essay. This chapter offers additional guidance to these important processes: it encourages students to focus upon creating reader-based prose where they guide their reader in appreciating the source in a way that reflects their emotional resonances when reading and engaging with it.

Too often, instructors’ response to students’ issues with source integration has been to focus upon integration and citational strategies that emphasize only “how to write with sources” and which largely ignore the reading experience. To combat this, this chapter encourages students to understand a connection between their reading and writing practices (e.g. that tracking their feelings and emotions while reading can help them when they write with the source). It promotes student recognition of how feelings are always part of their reading experience and thus influences their source selection. Once students recognize how this might work, it then encourages them to gain more nuanced attention to source integration, especially looking for emotional cues that might reflect the read-

ing experience and the attitude of the author. Language used to integrate sources does not need to be stripped of the emotional residue of the reading experience merely to appear academic in an essay. In fact, more carefully integrating emotional language can help students more precisely analyze the reading-writing connection as they work to create reader-based prose that reflects their experiences with the text.

This chapter would pair well with other *Writing Spaces* texts like Bunn’s, Carillo and Horning’s chapter on evaluating credible online sources, or DeRise’s interrogation of the role of bias; it might also be useful to think of in relationship to Kyle Stedman’s chapter on source integration. Certainly, instructors could encourage students to explore “how does emotion connect to their desire to integrate a source, and why?” in relationship to any of these texts. This chapter can connect to several different units or themes that are common in composition curriculums as well. For example, if students are investigating the impacts of personal beliefs on their identity and/or literacy practices, they might find that considering their attitudes toward emotions and academic writing warrants attention. Of course, this chapter is useful in relationship to engaging students in building up the practices of a critical reader---especially in examining the role of emotion in selecting sources, and how a process of active, marginal annotation can help mark and track such engagement.

# Activity 1: Reading with Feeling by Practicing Emoji Annotation

## Directions

As you read the assigned text, practice paying attention to the emotions you experience. You can use any emojis you’d like (you determine what they mean), but you should annotate at least 4 places in the text where you had some sort of response.

Remember, like we’ve been talking about, your feelings may not be “huge responses” but might just indicate an evaluation of something in the text---annoyance, frustration, interest, agreement, excitement… and more.

## How we’ll use this Annotation in Class:

You’ll be asked to partner or group up and then discuss the places in the text you annotated and *why*. The goal of this discussion is to learn to both articulate your feelings or emotional cues, as well as to learn about what parts of your experience of the text are shared or similar with your peers and where you differ.

# Activity 2: Comparing Source Integration Examples

## Directions:

For each of the examples below---all taken from *Writing Spaces* chapters like those we’ve been reading---can you figure out the relationship between the author and their source? With what emotion, do they want you as the reader, to view that source?

1. Start by considering---do they “like” or “dislike” the source? Then consider more specific affective elements of the use: is the source useful? Inspiring? Horrifying?

2. After we’ve discussed each of the examples, I’ll ask you to reflect on which ones you like “more than others” and why.

3. Lastly, would any of these act as good examples that you can imitate in your own essays? Why or why not? Even if you don’t feel like a writer, it is imperative to understand that, like me, you will benefit from writing, firstly by developing personal agency. Educational psychologists Barry Zimmerman and Timothy Cleary define personal agency as your “capability to originate and direct actions for given purposes” (45). To me, “originating” and “directing actions” for a purpose sounds a lot like answering the question *So what*? (Lester et al. 4). Don’t believe my metaphor? That’s okay! Instead, take wisdom from writers David Bayles and Ted Orland, who remark that what is needed to write well is “a broad sense of what you are looking for, some strategy for how to find it, and an overriding willingness to embrace mistakes and surprises along the way” (21). (Lester et al. 6). Work and Rework. While I’m sure you’ll be taught lots of technical advice on what makes revision work well, from a resilience perspective, I like what Elbow presents when he advises us to “[eat] like an owl: take in everything and trust your innards to digest what’s useful and discard what’s not” (Elbow 264). Make sure to give yourself time to avert your eyes and mind from your first draft for at least a day before you come back to revise. (Lester et al. 7). In *Biased*, a 2019 book about racial bias, author and psychology professor Jennifer Eberhardt likens bias to a categorization effort by the human brain. Eberhardt also acknowledges that this trait is universal: it’s our version of a short cut for processing vast amounts of information, “bringing coherence to a chaotic world” (24). However, these very same shortcuts have significant limitations, as they “impede our efforts to embrace and understand people who are deemed not like us” (24). This idea is at the heart of Eberhardt’s research about implicit racial bias and its life-or-death consequences. Tragically, in the years since Eberhardt’s book was published, acts of violence have continued against Black individuals by law enforcement. (DeRise 140) Chances are you’ve heard the word genre describing a TV show or movie, with labels like sci-fi and comedy guiding your expectations. Such labels are useful for any genre of the written word, too, for which Schick and Miller provide the following definition: “a typical, commonly recognized form of communication used to achieve a recurring (that is, repeating) purpose” (21). The more we read, then, the more we discover patterns in these categories that shape our expectations for the content. (DeRise 142) News bias is actually quite problematic and is likely to go undetected, because we have to be knowledgeable about a topic to even spot instances of bias. Check out the Writing Spaces chapter “Effectively and Efficiently Reading the Credibility of Online Sources” by composition scholars Ellen Carillo and Alice Horning for some excellent source evaluation strategies. Among other tips, they remind us that it’s a writer’s job to “negotiate bias” shown in sources, remembering that we “can’t somehow remove bias from these sources” (42), but that rather, it matters how we present these slants to our readers. (DeRise 144) Media literacy specialists, including AllSides marketing director Julie Mastrine, acknowledge that bias is everywhere and unavoidable. Like Eberhardt, Mastrine accepts that human beings are biased by nature. Specifically related to media, though, Mastrine argues that bias only becomes a problem when not disclosed, which may result in readers “being manipulated into (a biased outlet’s) point of view and not able to evaluate it critically and objectively” (qtd in Sheridan). Experts like Mastrine also note that media outlets improve their credibility with the public by labeling content as news or opinion. (DeRise 144)
