Think back to choosing your first-ever cell phone. What did you want and how did you know? Did you want the same phone as all your friends? Did you need the fanciest camera? Did you choose a particular case to make the phone feel like you? If so, you made a rhetorically-influenced choice, the same kind of choice customers have been making for several decades. Unlike those customers, however, you probably weren’t wondering if the phone has navigation capabilities, could connect you to your friends through text messages and social media apps, or play music. For many people in the early 1990s, a phone being truly *mobile* at all would shock and awe. For most people in the early 2000s, a phone you could carry around was old news, but a phone that put *the internet in your* *pocket,* gave you directions, and played your favorite songs was something exciting. What kind of phone would shock and awe you, given who you are and what you know? These questions, which seem technological, are actually also *rhetorical*.

Although rhetoric can be conceptualized in many ways (Duffy), we focus on rhetoric as a method for analyzing the choices people (or rhetors) make when writing, speaking, or composing multimedia texts that seek to engage and/or persuade particular audiences. Many cultures have developed unique theories of rhetoric, but one common rhetorical framework from Ancient Greece---the rhetorical appeals of ethos, pathos, or logos---has persisted as a useful, if limited, tool. You may have already learned about the appeals, and perhaps written a rhetorical analysis essay that allotted one paragraph each to identifying how a commercial or speech establishes credibility (ethos), evokes emotion (pathos), and employs logical arguments and evidence (logos).

This kind of rhetorical analysis can be useful, but it’s important to remember that ethos, pathos, and logos aren’t like prizes in a claw machine, with you snatching up one ethos dinosaur and one pathos unicorn, while finding all the logos pandas missing. Instead, you can think of the appeals as lenses that reveal different elements of a text’s rhetorical strategies; a single example from a text can simultaneously appeal to ethos, pathos, and logos. Also, it’s important to remember that the appeals may be most useful as tools for thinking rather than fancy terms to sprinkle into your writing. You can write an effective analysis of the persuasive appeals of a text without using the words ethos, pathos, and logos explicitly. After all, simply identifying that a speaker uses an appeal doesn’t provide as much insight as describing that appeal more precisely. For example, saying that an animal rescue ad full of sad puppies and mournful music has a lot of pathos is less precise than saying that the rescue ad evokes compassion and grief to encourage the audience to donate.

To analyze a text’s rhetorical appeals, it’s essential to consider audience and context. We need to understand both who the rhetor is trying to talk to and which concepts, ideas, and historical events are floating around in the heads of the rhetor and their audience. What would the original audience already know or understand about the argument in this time period? How might other audiences respond and why? Remember that you are only one audience for the text; instead of trying to write as if your unique reaction is universal, it’s better to disclose how your positionality (your identities, your values, your location in time and place) influences your analysis. To do this, you will sometimes need to use “I” in your writing (Maddalena).

You may have heard from your teachers in the past that using “I” weakens your argument, or just been forbidden from using it altogether. And we admit that there are some situations where this advice is on point. For example, using “I” to qualify your argument typically isn’t helpful (saying “I think this advertisement appeals to its intended audience” versus “This advertisement appeals to its intended audience”). You are the author, and we understand the essay reflects your thoughts! However, sometimes you need to intentionally bring in your own lived experiences and knowledge, which you can’t do without naming yourself explicitly. For example, when analyzing a cell phone ad, you might name yourself to acknowledge the differences between your perspective and the commercial’s original audience. This could look something like: “while audiences like me anticipate a cell phone having GPS and internet access, those features would be new and exciting for the original viewer.”

It’s also important to recognize that the appeals are not “universal,” but instead come from an Ancient Greek rhetorical tradition that reflects biases operating within that culture (Powell). If we choose to use the appeals as a lens, we need to ask critical questions that Aristotle never considered: What identities (race, gender, class, sexuality, disability) are privileged and excluded in this text? How might the rhetorical choices of this text reinforce and/or subvert structures of power within its context?

When we think of rhetorical analysis as identifying Ancient Greek appeals like prizes in a claw machine, we miss a great deal of complexity. Instead, we should think of rhetorical appeals as lenses that expose new but limited insights every time we put them on. To model this for you, we will redefine ethos, pathos, and logos, starting with a simplistic “prize ” rhetorical analysis question and then offering a “lens” rhetorical analysis question. After that, we model our lens approach, using it to analyze three cell phone ads from different time periods.

# Ethos

## Prize: Where does the rhetor establish credibility?

Traditional rhetorical analysis might ask what makes the rhetor credible, like their professional expertise, education, or celebrity status. This is a great start; ethos *is* about how rhetors establish themselves as legitimate and worthy of time and attention. However, this question is laden with unstated assumptions and doesn’t help us see the depth and complexity of the ways rhetors establish ethos.

### Lens: Which audiences might find the speaker credible or relatable?

A more nuanced approach to ethos asks *which audiences* might find the speaker *credible* or *relatable*. While the first question implies credibility and relatability are static and asks you to assume your initial response is universal, this question reminds us that we are always relating to specific persons or groups. Leaving these assumptions unchecked can cause biased interpretations or even a total misinterpretation. Take the expectations we have about language choice. A traditional analysis of ethos may assume that “white mainstream English” (Baker-Bell 2020), particularly the kind of jargon-y language you see in academic writing, is universally “credible” and always proves the rhetor is an expert. For this to be true, you have to assume the intended audience for everything is people who know and value that kind of English. Depending on your audience and your intended rhetorical effect, another English (say, Black American English), a blend of Englishes, or a blend of English and other languages might signal knowledge and expertise better (Alvarez, Wan, & Lee). And you shouldn’t assume that just because someone is a professor, they only value white mainstream English; for example, we are two English professors who love it when our students and colleagues draw on all their language resources to write engagingly and persuasively.

# Pathos

## Prize: What emotions are invoked by this text and how?

Traditional pathos interpretations may identify one strong, easily identifiable emotion (such as fear in a political attack ad or excitement in a toy ad)---pointing to ways the text creates an emotional effect.

## Lens: What cultural narratives would the audience need to believe to feel those emotions?

While we tend to associate pathos with texts that provoke strong emotions like joy, fear, and anger, there are subtle, overlapping emotional appeals in all texts. For example, a social media platform with long, legalistic terms and conditions in small font attempts to provoke boredom and tiredness, encouraging you to skip reading and simply agree. Of course, to feel that terms and conditions are boring and skippable, the audience needs to already believe that legal writing is dull. They must also either believe that social media companies are trustworthy or that they are untrustworthy but unchangeable (so not worth fighting). If we imagine an expert in consumer privacy rights as an audience for terms and conditions, they might both find the policy fascinating and feel hopeful about creating fairer terms.

# Logos

## Prize: What forms of evidence are presented?

A limited approach to logos asks us about “evidence” in a rigid way: facts and statistics. This perspective obscures all the subtle ways rhetors establish support for their claims, including images, narrative examples, citations of secondary sources, personal stories, use of common sayings, and even the unstated assumptions upon which arguments are based.

## Lens: What unstated values or beliefs would the audience need to hold to be persuaded?

At the deepest level, logos refers to all the unstated beliefs or values that an audience would need to hold to be persuaded by the argument. Imagine a hurricane evacuation warning. For the warning to be effective, the audience must believe in the value of the National Weather Service, recognize that it is necessary to evacuate before the full storm path is known, and understand that the benefits of evacuating the area are worth the potential costs.

# Putting our Lenses On: Three Cell Phone Ads

Now we’re going to do some real rhetorical analysis! We will analyze three cell phone advertisements from different historical moments, using the appeals as lenses, not prizes. We will start by explicitly naming the appeals, then use the appeals as thinking tools to guide our analysis.

Because phone technologies evolve quickly, cell phone ads are a great way to consider how audience and context influence the effectiveness of rhetorical appeals. Cell phone advertisers must convince audiences that their products are revolutionary, but what counts as “progress” changes over time. The rhetoric of progress in cell phone ads is probably best exemplified by the slogan for the iPhone 4, “This Changes Everything. Again.” ---a slogan that came just three years after the first gen iPhone “changed the world.”

## 1990: When the Phone Travels

![Still from a 1980s cellular phone commercial. A man in glasses and a suit sits at the wheel of a red sedan, holding a brick-sized phone to his ear; a door sign reads Metro Realty 555-3500, and a SOLD sign stands in front of the car.](assets/figure-p006-1.jpg)\
Figure 1. A white man, wearing a suit in a red car with a “Metro Realty” sticker, talks on a large phone in front of a “SOLD” sign. The narrator says, “Finding a phone in a car isn’t that unusual anymore, except when it leaves the car” (00:01). Screenshot captured by the authors, from https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=694TX2lQ7Uo

In a 1990 RadioShack ad, we open on a suited realtor chatting on his phone in his car, a sight the narrator tells us is “not so unusual anymore.” What is truly unusual, he notes, is when the phone “*leaves the car.”* As the phone leaves the car, we follow: on a golf course, in a boat, at a restaurant, between father and son on a bench. When it rings, the realtor, befuddled, hands the receiver to his son. Bespectacled and dapper, the little businessman demands one hundred shares. With comical alarm, the wide-eyed father takes the phone back, presumably before funds are inappropriately dispersed. This advertisement draws on white, upper-middle class, masculine values and lifestyles to depict the mobile phone as both a vehicle for and a symbol of affluence and success.

![Still from the same 1980s commercial. A man in a bucket hat, glasses and a purple polo shirt talks into a corded mobile handset at the helm of a motorboat while a boy in an orange life jacket sits behind him; on-screen text reads Cellular Service Available in Most Major Cities.](assets/figure-p007-1.jpg)\
Figure 2. A white man in a purple polo shirt talks on a large mobile phone on a boat while his son sits on the bow, wearing a life jacket and a swimsuit. Text on screen reads, “Cellular service available in most cities,” while the voice-over narrator enthusiastically says, “on the high seas”---with a touch of irony as the boat is actually in calm water near shore. Screenshot (00:08) captured by the authors, from https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=694TX2lQ7Uo

Before we can analyze the appeals of this ad, we should consider what television audiences in 1990 might already know or believe about telephones. The early 1990s audience likely assumed that phones were mostly static (wired) and used only for voice calls; moreover, the audience would assume that the car phone was an expensive luxury used mostly for business. Realizing this context, we note this ad features a newspaper and an early laptop alongside the phone, because a 1990s audience would never have imagined that a phone could be used to access news or type a document.

Looking at the ad with a logos lens, we note that it is thin on evidence ---it claims the phone is affordable but doesn’t say the cost; it says service is available in “most major cities” but doesn’t offer details about coverage; however, it does represent the RadioShack phone moving across multiple locations, providing a kind of visual evidence of its ability to leave the car. Moreover, by showing the phone’s use by a wealthy white male realtor for business purposes, it suggests that buying the phone may be a key to financial success. To be persuaded by this evidence, an audience would need to believe that making money is a virtue, that a white upper middle-class lifestyle is desirable, and that the world of business is (and perhaps should be) predominantly led by white men. Of course, even in 1990 (as J can attest), there were some people who resisted these values and would not have been persuaded by this ad.

Turning to ethos, we can consider the people portrayed in the ad and which audiences might feel included or excluded by those choices of representation. Both the father and the son in this ad are dressed in clothing commonly associated with wealthy white stockbrokers in the early 1990s---we might say that the khakis, golf shirts, and suspenders evoked “finance bro” then in the way a Patagonia vest might in the 2020s. Moreover, this advertisement only features white men and boys using the phone ---women appear only briefly in the background in service roles. In many ways, this ad reinforces sexist stereotypes that men are the early adopters of new digital technology---a stereotype that ignores the long history of women’s contributions to computing (Blair). We can surmise that women and nonbinary people (yes, we existed back then!) would be less likely to relate to or identify with the speaker here.

![Still from the same 1980s commercial. On a park bench, a boy in large glasses, a blue sweater and suspenders talks into a corded mobile phone while the man beside him, in matching glasses and a red plaid shirt with a newspaper on his lap, stares at him in surprise.](assets/figure-p008-1.jpg)\
Figure 3. A man sits on a park bench with a newspaper on his lap and his son beside him. The father and son wear matching glasses, and the son sports a blue collared shirt and suspenders. The son talks on a large mobile phone, acknowledging he “heard about the merger” and requesting the listener “buy a hundred shares.” The man looks on with comical alarm as the son cheekily grins. Screenshot (00:22) captured by the authors, from https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=694TX2lQ7Uo

And finally, we turn to the question of pathos. One of the prominent emotional appeals is humor; the audience is supposed to laugh when the child takes the phone and tries to buy stock in a recently merged corporation. For this to be funny, the audience would need to think cellular telephones are primarily used for business by adults, so the child using the telephone is silly. To believe children using phones is lighthearted, not frightening, you need to feel it’s positive for a young boy to follow in his businessman father’s footsteps, and that it’s not realistic to worry that a child would have their own phone.

Of course, when we watch this ad now, it’s funny for all kinds of reasons the producers never could have imagined. The narrator’s excitement at the portability of a phone slightly smaller than a briefcase is hilarious to viewers accustomed to pocket-sized phones. The clothes that signaled white upper-middle-class masculinity then might today be great ironic finds at a thrift store. And the evocation of a world in which only white men use mobile phones seems quaint and dated, given the more diverse market for these devices today. Juxtaposing audiences for this advertisement, the original and modern viewers, reveals how dramatically context transforms rhetorical efficacy. What was once cool becomes embarrassing, what was once exciting becomes boring, and what was once progress becomes a relic of a time before.

## 2007: When the Phone Does it All

![Still from a commercial for an early iPhone. Against a black background, a hand holds the phone sideways while a thumb flicks through album covers, a red Yeah Yeah Yeahs cover in the centre of the screen.](assets/figure-p010-1.jpg)\
Figure 4. A white hand holding an iPhone navigates to a music album cover by the Yeah Yeahs Yeahs. The voice over says, “It’s amazing what fits in your pocket these days. Your favorite music.” Screenshot (3:05) captured by the authors, from https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gw5ZtW_GLbg

In 2007, the iPhone changes everything again. Where the RadioShack ad opens with the surprise and delight of a *mobile* phone, the Apple ad wants us to almost forget we’re looking at a phone at all. The narrator muses, “it’s amazing what fits in a pocket these days,” as a disembodied hand navigates through the touchscreen. We are taken along a user-manual style account of the features, directing us through music, access to the internet, videos, and more. Once we have been dazzled by all that fits in our pockets, the device rings, and the narrator sagely reminds us that “of course,” it’s also “a phone.” This advertisement responds to the emergence of a potentially complex, confusing new technology by representing the interface as easily navigable, useful, and fun within and beyond professional life.

While the RadioShack ad features fully developed characters, the iPhone shows only a pale hand holding and using an iPhone in front of a black background. The medium-sized hand does not have any adornments (rings, manicured nails, etc.) so it might be said to evoke masculinity, though we as a white cisgender woman (Elizabeth) and a white nonbinary person (J) both see the hand as potentially representing us, too. The hand on a black background (rather than a person in a specific place) invites all viewers to imagine settings in which they might use the phone. The black background also calls to mind a particular high-end minimalist fashion and interior design aesthetic that evokes understated wealth. The phone also appears to be floating in outer space, suggesting that Apple has boldly gone to a new galaxy with its new phone. While the ad may appear to be designed for everyone, we can still see an ethos that appeals particularly to white people who love minimalist design as well to people who agree with the unstated assumption (logos) that new technological developments signal progress.

![Still from the same iPhone commercial. A hand holds the phone against a black background; the screen shows an incoming call from David Taylor over a picture of the Earth, with a red Decline button and a green Answer button.](assets/figure-p011-1.jpg)\
Figure 5. A pale hand holds an iPhone with an incoming call from “David Taylor” highlighted on screen. The voiceover comments “And, of course… a phone,” broken by the ringtone (3:20). Screenshot captured by the authors, from https:// www.youtube.com/watch?v=gw5ZtW_GLbg

The first iPhone popularized the technology of the touchscreen for the average person, who would have been more familiar with the discrete buttons of a flip phone or the swirling wheel of an iPod. Further, the idea of high-tech devices being user-friendly was itself an innovation, which is why the commercial spends so much time showing how easy the phone is to navigate. Only at the conclusion, after we’ve seen the new elements, does the narrator remind us that it is “of course, a phone.” There is nothing “of course” about this phone, and the choice to highlight a voice call in the final seconds deemphasizes its call feature.

J remembers seeing this ad and being excited at the prospect of using a phone to access digital music, video, and news. In the ad, the hand guides us through a music library featuring the Yeah Yeah Yeah’s, an indie band J loves, and YouTube, then a new platform primarily used by younger people. It also shows the hand accessing email (the only nod to work) and the *New York Times* (a news source that J still reads every day). In contrast to the RadioShack ad, this ad seems to appeal to middle-class millennials and early Gen X “hipsters” who enjoy music, social media, and liberal-leaning political news but also need email for their jobs. This ad suggests that the iPhone is a way to consume media that reflects your interests. J was willing to spend more money than they had at the time for an iPhone, because the phone’s evocation of indie music and the *New York Times* made it feel like a technology that reflected who they wanted to be. Notably, J chose to disclose their personal connection to this ad because ethical rhetorical analysis requires reflection on how bias can impact our interpretations.

## 2024: When the Phone Does Less

![Still from a commercial for the HMD Barbie phone. A bright pink phone with a light blue back marked Barbie tumbles through the frame against a pink background, among floating pastel beads, charms and a swirled pink, blue and yellow phone case.](assets/figure-p012-1.jpg)\
Figure 6. A pink, shiny phone spins against a gradient background, with beads, sprinkles, and phone cases floating in the frame. Upbeat music plays in the background (0:04). Screenshot captured by the authors, from https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpKA6OFK0Ac

We now turn to a 2024 ad for a Barbie phone that contrasts sharply with the iPhone. Immediately, the screen turns pink. Rhythmic drumming beckons as the iconic Barbie font spins out, revealing a pink box and even pinker phone. The “HMD Barbie” floats up to us alongside a string of beads and a sprinkle of confetti: hearts, palm trees, ice cream cones. When we zoom out onto a pastel pink beach the phone unfolds, revealing the surprise of buttons in place of the omnipresent touch screen. We scroll through pictures of happy multicultural women, then watch a spiral of Barbie phones customized with stickers. The final frame urges us to “live our dream life,” presumably with the HMD Barbie in tow. This advertisement reflects how values around technology and progress have changed as phones become key features of modern life by embracing the concept of the “dumb” phone and celebrating a feminine, playful aesthetic.

Who does this 2024 explosion of pink girlishness welcome? Certainly, Elizabeth, whose very first phone in the 2000s was a bright pink flip model not unlike the HMD Barbie. Elizabeth can remember the rigid abbreviations we used in our infrequent texts (thx) that transitioned into the full sentences enabled by her first iPhone. Elizabeth can remember the satisfying snap of a flip phone closing after a terrible conversation. Most importantly, Elizabeth can remember barely looking at her phone throughout the day. For audiences like Elizabeth, the HMD Barbie reflects nostalgia for a simpler youth, where we spent whole days neither thinking about nor using our cell phones.

This contemporary ad persuades by stripping cell phones of all of the functionality the prior advertisements we’ve examined eagerly highlighted. Where the cellular telephone and the smartphone ads dazzled by highlighting new features, the HMD Barbie paradoxically represents progress by making itself “dumber.” A news article describing its features notes “it includes only technologies that were available in the 1990s and 2000s era of the early mobile phone: a camera, calls and texts” (Griffin).

The appeal of the “dumb” phone relies on the cultural and technological shift facilitated by blending texting and calling with internet access in one device. The cellular telephone and the smartphone promised their audiences more mobility, more access, more information, and more connection; the contemporary audience has lived through the world those phones promised and found it lacking. When the ad asks us to give our smartphones “a vacay,” it conflates the smartphone with a host of stressors and anxieties, from facilitating bullying to cultivating negative body image to breaking down our work-life balance. The girlishness, the vibrance, the string of beads, and playful icons hearken back to an imagined “before” time in childhood, resplendent with play and friendship, free of work emails and online harassment. It supports its claims through a clever inversion, by emphasizing what the phone *does not have.*

![Still from the HMD Barbie phone commercial. A pink flip phone stands open on a pale, stylised beach lined with palm trees, its screen showing app icons; pink text at the left reads Pick up and give your smartphone a vacay.](assets/figure-p014-1.jpg)\
Figure 7. A pink flip phone unfolds in front of a soothing beach background of swaying palm trees, sand, and lapping waves. Bright pink text implores us to “Pick up and give your smartphone a vacay” (0:09). Screenshot captured by the authors, from https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpKA6OFK0Ac

The ad’s negotiation of age functions for two audiences: adults who remember the flip phones it references and young adults who lack the referent but may be persuaded by the style and customizability, as well as its association with the 2023 Barbie film. The HMD Barbie, as depicted, is a phone someone of any age would want, and any parent would want for their child. It establishes credibility in multiple registers by arguing it can free us from the pain of modern life, protect vulnerable children from experiencing the ravages of the internet, and help all of us connect to each other in fun, safe ways.

Though the advertisement explicitly hails femininity, highlighting a multiracial group of adult women in the images on the phone, blending the music with feminine “ahs” of amazement (and, of course, all the pink), we never see a particular person holding or using the phone. Where the RadioShack ad relied on a rigid, unquestioned white masculinity, the HMD Barbie is both lavishly feminine and, perhaps, also for everyone. The inyour-face girlishness evokes youth and playfulness. Instead of presenting an ‘aspirational’ white man doing important business, the HMD Barbie welcomes viewers into being one of the girls. (Both Elizabeth and J feel included in the feminine club this ad imagines, though we realize many other viewers may not). Nevertheless, there is a sexist contrast between the masculine/neutral “smart-phone” advertisements, which emphasize the workplace, current events, and power, and the feminine “dumb-phone” we should take on vacation.

Figure 8. A pink phone on a pink-teal gradient background spins in our direction to display the screen. It showcases a picture of two women of color and one white woman lying on a blue gingham picnic blanket. All three wear sunglasses and smile up at the camera (0:18). Screenshot captured by the authors, from https:// www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpKA6OFK0Ac

In these ways, the HMD ad represents a transformation in public perception of technology, with attendant shifts in values around childhood, gender, and progress. No longer the providence of older white men, nor valued for its complexity and novelty, the cell phone is an omnipresent feature of modern life. A child using a phone is commonplace, and the ad intervenes in a robust and critical conversation on the *dangers* of what can fit in your pocket. By reading media through the values that underlie its persuasiveness, we can trace changes in fundamental concepts that govern our relationships to both technology and each other.

# What Now?

Ultimately, rhetorical analysis is something that you do all the time. You wonder about the effectiveness of arguments, both tiny (which phone to buy) and huge (how can I be an ethical consumer in an unethical world?). All your decisions involve thinking about what’s important and evaluating the persuasiveness of arguments around you.

One of the key features of this chapter is that the simplistic prize approach to the appeals doesn’t just create weaker essays, it can also create *misinterpretations.* You can get things wrong when you don’t challenge your assumptions or think critically about audience, context, and positionalities. Assuming your perspective is universal makes your world smaller and means you miss out on so much. The more nuanced lens approach to the appeals we offer is not just a strategy for writing great papers, it’s a way of staying both curious and kind, learning from the world around you. Now that you have your rhetorical appeals lenses, you can take them with you everywhere and see all the colors you may have missed.

# Teacher Resources

In this essay, we provide a re-introduction to the rhetorical appeals of ethos, pathos, and logos that can help students understand and apply the appeals in more complex, nuanced ways---while also considering some of the limitations of viewing rhetoric through these lenses.[^tr-1] While we designed this essay to be accessible to students who had no prior instruction in rhetoric, we also think it could be a helpful reframe for students who may have learned about ethos, pathos, and logos in the past and applied them in somewhat reductive ways.

Readers familiar with rhetorical theory may notice that our discussions of ethos draw implicitly on Burkean theories of identification and feminist theories of positionality, our discussion of pathos has resonance with feminist theories of affect, and our discussions of logos reflect a synthesis of Aristotle’s articulation of the enthymeme with more contemporary ideological criticism. While we did not explicitly cite these theories in our article to make it more accessible, we include here some activities that teachers can use to discuss Aristotelian, Burkean, and feminist rhetorical theories more with students.

While we provide model rhetorical analysis of three commercials in this essay, we very much hope that teachers and students will treat our models as open to criticism and revision. We weren’t able to unpack all the strategies operating in each of the ads in the limited space available; moreover, we are clear in the essay about how our positionalities have influenced our analysis. Students and teachers with different positionalities from ours will likely notice different strategies and make different observations about them. And this is a good thing!

# Teacher-Led Activities

1. Present students with a list of general/nonspecific “observations” about the appeals. For example: “The iPhone ad effectively uses logos.” Instruct students to draft new versions of the statements that are more specific, detailed, and arguable.

2. Present students with different historical examples of persuasive texts in groups. Clearly designate the year/location/audience of the text assigned. Instruct students to research the rhetorical context, identifying two to three important historical facts that help them better interpret the persuasive strategies. Have the groups share what they learned and how the context informs their analysis.

3. Review an advertisement together as a large group, and work backwards from the appeals to who the imagined or intended audience is. Instruct students in pairs to redesign the advertisement for a broader/more inclusive or entirely different audience. How might the language, images, sounds, and more change based on who is being hailed? Have the pairs present their updated advertisements, then have the other students guess the new/expanded audience.

4. Instruct students in groups to suggest an issue relevant to themselves and their peers that would benefit from greater public awareness. Examples could include the availability of mental health services on campus, study strategies, underutilized resources, etc. Have the groups develop a public awareness campaign for that issue, identifying at least a slogan, a visual, a location, and a genre. Have groups explain their campaign to the larger class using the language of the appeals, describing each choice with at least two appeals (i.e. “we chose to use welcoming, non-judgemental language when describing STI support services to reduce the emotions of stigma and shame, as well as to establish our credibility about these services”).

5. To engage students in the impact of feminist positionality and affect on rhetoric, you can ask them to read excerpts from Audre Lorde’s “The Uses of Anger: Women Responding to Racism.” We particularly recommend instructing students in pairs to highlight sections of the piece where Lorde names herself and her emotions (for example: “I speak out of a direct and particular anger…”). What impact do these choices have on her argument? After analyzing Lorde’s expert use of her positionality, ask students to develop a definition of anger grounded in her work. What is anger for Lorde? What makes her definition distinct and what does it demonstrate about affect and emotion in rhetoric?

6. To engage students in reflecting on the cultural biases of Aristotelian rhetoric, you can ask them to read excerpts from Book 2, part 1 of Aristotle’s *Rhetoric* about pathos. We might particularly recommend chapters 10 - 14 (in which Aristotle outlines the emotional appeals of envy and emulation and then considers the different characters of young, middle-aged and old men and how they respond differently to emotional appeals). You can then ask students what biases they see in Aristotle’s assumptions about envy, emulation, and male audiences of different ages---reflecting about how and why they might need to update pathos analysis for contemporary contexts.

7. To engage students in reflecting on Burkean identification, you might teach an excerpt of Burke’s discussion of identification in *Rhetoric of Motives* (p. 55--57). In particular, you might ask students to reflect on what hidden assumptions about gender Burke makes in the examples he uses to introduce the concept. You also could open up a dialogue with students about how Burke’s concept of identification is both similar to and different from contemporary discourses of “relatability.” We also would recommend opening up a critical conversation about the limits of identification as a form of persuasion in a world where communicating ethically across difference is vital.

# Student Discussion Questions / Informal Writing Prompts

1. How do this essay’s conceptions of ethos, pathos, logos, audience, context, and/or positionality differ from your prior understandings of these concepts?

2. Find a short video advertisement that you found persuasive (now or when you were younger). Consider one rhetorical choice this advertisement makes and how you might analyze that choice as reflecting at least two distinct appeals.

3. Review the same advertisement twice, once with the “prize” questions and once with the “lens” questions. Compare and contrast the insights developed.

4. Some rhetorical scholars have suggested that the field should turn away entirely from Aristotelian rhetoric because of the ways it reflects masculine, elitist Ancient Greek biases. This article makes the case that rhetorical appeals (most famously codified by Aristotle) retain some value as long as we update them with contemporary questions of social justice. What do you think? What might be gained and lost if we abandoned ethos, pathos, and logos entirely as ways of analyzing rhetoric? What theories and concepts (from your education or from your home communities) might replace them?

5. Watch one of the commercials discussed in this essay at least three times. Try to identify at least three rhetorical choices that the commercial makes that the authors of the essay do not discuss. Also, re-reread the model analysis of the commercial and consider one claim the authors make that you think is limiting, lacking in complexity, or possibly inaccurate. In addition to explaining how your analysis of the commercial differs from the authors, also reflect on how your positionalities (identities, values, location in time and place) may have influenced your analysis.
