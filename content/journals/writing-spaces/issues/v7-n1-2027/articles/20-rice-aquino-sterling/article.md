# Introduction: What Happens When Different Worlds Meet?

Imagine a group of four college students---Lena, Jake, Aiko, and Chidi---assigned to work together on a persuasive writing project. Their task? To write a proposal addressing a local community issue. Lena, who grew up in a typical US high school, wants to dive right into making a clear, strong thesis statement with three straightforward points. Jake, also

American, thinks the group should first gather lots of statistics to back up their claims. Aiko, from Japan, prefers to share a personal story that captures feelings behind the issue before making any direct claims. And Chidi, from Nigeria, feels it’s important to consider cultural and ethical values that shape the problem and suggests beginning with a proverb to set the tone.

At their first meeting, things seemed okay. By the second, though, confusion creeps in. Jake feels frustrated that Aiko and Chidi aren’t “getting to the point.” Lena wishes the group would just agree on a structured outline. Aiko feels rushed and worried that her way of explaining is being ignored. Chidi senses that the deeper ethical-moral questions he cares about are being overlooked. And everyone disagrees about whether and how AI can be used to generate ideas or write parts.

Think back to your own collaborative writing projects. Does such confusion look familiar? So, what’s really going on here?

It’s more than just a clash of personalities. It’s a clash of communication styles shaped by cultural values---different ways people around the world understand how to argue, persuade, and express ideas. Lena and Jake expect clear logic and structure. Aiko and Chidi value storytelling, relationships, and shared meaning. This reveals something important when trying to communicate in today’s workplaces: the dominant Western model, focused on linear logic and a clear thesis, isn’t always enough for communication with diverse stakeholders. For example, Kaplan’s well-known model of cultural thought patterns illustrates how both British and American English often privileges linear progression, while Semitic, Asian, and Romance languages may emphasize circular, digressive, or parallel structures to develop meaning (see Figure 1). It’s not critical to understand every distinction so much as it is to know that people present ideas differently because they value different approaches. This awareness can move us from the Denial (missing differences), Polarization (judging differences), and Minimization (de-emphasizing differences) of varied culturally relevant ways of communicating, through written discourse to Acceptance (deeply comprehending differences), and ultimately Adaptation (bridging across differences). Only then can we make a shift from a “monocultural mindset” to an “intercultural mindset.”

The current essay, itself, uses some storytelling as well as argumentative, evidence-based reasoning to illustrate important dynamics. In so doing, as co-authors, we grappled with our own positionalities and culturally-situated communication styles. Rich is a scholar-researcher and professor of English language rhetoric who grew up in the US, and American English is his native language. On the other hand, Cristian, a bi-/multilingual education scholar-researcher who grew-up in the Dominican Republic and migrated to the US as a teenager, learned American English as a Second Language (ESL) while maintaining his mother tongue, Spanish. Particularly, as co-authors, we’ve acknowledged that even though Cristian is fluent in American English, when it comes to the writing process, he still thinks in Spanish---a language that tends to be more elaborate and indirect, often providing background and context before reaching the main point (Background → Context → Main Idea). The approach tends to use longer introductions, subordinate clauses, and rhetorical flourishes, relying more on shared cultural understanding than explicit statements.

On the other hand, the linear model---found in both American and British English, for example---tends to reason that if “X is true and Y is desired,” then “Z” *should* be used to change X into Y. Within such logic, it is important to include evidence to support such a claim. And include evidence to support this logic. An example: “If climate change is accelerating (X) and a stable environment is desired (Y), then reducing carbon emissions (Z) *should* be prioritized as a means of altering the current trajectory---especially given the growing body of scientific data supporting this course of action.” In this model of writing, clarity, structure, logical sequence, and coherence are paramount (Claim → Evidence → Conclusion). With this type of writing, you’re often taught to use active voice, plain language, short sentences, one main idea per paragraph, and to avoid logical fallacies. In addition to emphasizing clarity and brevity with a direct style that gets to main points quickly, the English language, across its many varieties, is low-context, meaning ideas tend to be stated explicitly rather than relying on shared context.

> English: Claim → Evidence → Conclusion

> Spanish: Background → Context → Main Idea

How can writers who come from different culturally-situated writing/ thinking styles collaborate effectively while drawing on the strengths of their approaches? First, differences should not be perceived as deficits but as assets to leverage. Articulating complex problems often requires more than one approach to resonate with different readers. Culturally appropriate narratives might not fit neatly into linear argumentation structures (Campbell and Filimon). Second, in addition to purpose, always think carefully about the intended audience. This is where intercultural communication competence (ICC) comes in.

# What Is Intercultural Communication Competence (ICC)?

Put simply, intercultural communication happens anytime people from different cultural backgrounds interact. And levels of experience within an organization could be likened to different cultures; consider, for instance, how someone in your major communicates differently than someone outside your major. But ICC helps us go further (Deardorff). It’s the ability to communicate effectively and respectfully across cultures by being aware of cultural differences, by appreciating them, and by adjusting your own communication style accordingly. Consider these *three* areas in the context of collaborative writing/thinking you’ve done in the past and might do in the future:

- *Knowledge and Awareness (Cognitive):* Understanding that people from different cultures may value different ways of making arguments. For instance, some may view storytelling or metaphor as central to persuasion, while others prioritize logic or credibility appeals (Patterson and Matias).
- *Feelings and Attitudes (Affective):* Being open, curious, and willing to sit with discomfort when faced with unfamiliar styles of communication. This includes empathy and intercultural sensitivity, which are traits that go far in today’s diverse writing situations. Look up these terms within the context of your own future workplace (Plantin).
- *Skills and Actions (Behavioral):* Practicing new ways of communicating---listening closely, adapting tone, structuring your argument differently---to better reach your audience. Just because an approach is different doesn’t mean it’s wrong or limited. Think asset rather than deficit. People too often make false connections between different rhetorical styles or language use and correctness (Rakrak).

Ultimately, developing ICC in writing means realizing that persuasive communication isn’t just about having the right facts; it’s about deepening knowledge and awareness, acknowledging and understanding feelings and attitudes, and applying skills and actions. Although that’s a must, of course. Otherwise, we’re in danger of spreading misinformation. ICC also recognizes that communication strategies are not universal, and that cultures may prioritize different rhetorical elements---such as logic, emotion, context, or narrative---when constructing arguments. It also requires you to adapt based on cultural context, openness to difference, and stylistic differences.

Consider the following two culturally-situated approaches attempting to articulate the same idea:

> English: Social media damages self-esteem. Studies show that heavy use correlates with depression and anxiety.

> Spanish: In today’s world, where technology invades every corner of our lives, it’s no surprise that young people find themselves affected emotionally. Social media, with all its promises, can actually damage self-esteem.

At first sight, and for the speaker-writer educated within a Spanish language cultural logics, the English presentation might seem disjointed, choppy, avoiding “knitting the ideas” (or “hilar las ideas”). For those working from an English language cultural logics, the Spanish presentation might seem non-economical and fraught with unnecessary detours that obfuscate the main idea. Developing an intercultural orientation and disposition toward these various forms of writing means letting go of “either/ or” assumptions that place a higher value on a particular approach and dismisses the value of the other.

# Two Related Terms to Learn and Apply

- *Ethnocentrism:* The belief that your own culture’s way of doing things is “normal” or “best.” Your way of relating or understanding information may be less appropriate or accurate, even when working with an audience that has different ways of making sense. It’s like wearing glasses that only let you see your own values clearly while blurring others. Ethnocentrism in writing often appears when students dismiss alternative rhetorical styles or perspectives as “confusing” or “incorrect” (Bennett and Bennett).
- *Intercultural Sensitivity:* The ability to notice and value cultural differences without rushing to judge them. It opens the door to applying empathy and improving audience awareness, which is especially important in persuasive writing in a world valuing global distribution of thinking (Peirce; Usó-Juan and Martínez-Flor).

Whether you’re writing a group paper, an email to your professor, a policy memo, or a social media campaign, your audience is likely to be diversified, culturally and/or experientially. Think lived experience. If you’ve only learned to write in one rhetorical mode---such as the thesis-driven, evidence-first approach---you may struggle to reach others effectively. Further, if you’re reading material that values approaches different than what you’re used to, it does not mean the ideas aren’t sound or useful. ICC doesn’t replace logic or clarity; it expands your toolkit. Consider these applications:

- In *global work environments*, colleagues may prioritize relationship-building or consensus rather than confrontation or individual assertion. This is why understanding cultural and organizational value similarities and differences is so important (Hofstede, Hofstede, and Minkov).
- In *multilingual communities*, storytelling and emotion may carry more weight than just data or facts. This is why using plain language and relating directly and clearly between facts and examples descriptively is critical (Olga; Vaughn et al.).
- In *academic writing*, demonstrating you understand your audience’s values and background---what they know, believe, and care about---can increase your credibility. This is why verifying that your information is factual is imperative (Graff and Birkenstein). AI hallucinations can really hurt your credibility.

Another way of putting it? Effective writing today depends on flexibility and very careful thinking. ICC helps you move from “This is how I write” to “How can I write in a way that connects with my readers?” How can we grow as writers/thinkers?

# The Intercultural Development Continuum (IDC)

The Developmental Model of Intercultural Sensitivity (DMIS) and its updated version, the Intercultural Development Continuum (IDC), provide a roadmap for how people develop intercultural communication competence (Bennett and Bennett; Hammer, Bennett, and Wiseman). Feel free to Google them. There are five stages to work through:

1. Denial: People at this stage may not notice cultural differences at all. They assume everyone thinks and communicates like they do. Well, they don’t.

2. Polarization: Differences are seen, but they’re judged as inferior or problematic. One’s own way of arguing is considered “better.” This isn’t always true, of course.

3. Minimization: People recognize differences but still assume that deep down, everyone is pretty much the same. Differences can coincide productively.

4. Acceptance: There’s a growing appreciation for different values and ways of thinking. Communicators begin to adapt. Greater understanding of purpose and audience becomes more evident. Adaptation: Communicators shift their style depending on context

and audience. They can navigate across cultural expectations with fluency. Communication is more effective. Yes, the first stage is denial. You know, we often move in and out of these stages, but what’s important is to understand them and to move forward along the continuum *toward* adaptation. Those with experience adapting to cultural differences can more fluidly integrate multiple cultural perspectives into their own systems of values and beliefs, which is often the case of many bicultural or multicultural individuals. Western linear thinking can be complemented with sensitive, culturally appropriate communication approaches along the continuum (Deardorff; Irwansyah; Usó-Juan and Martínez-Flor).

Let’s revisit our student group from our introduction and apply these stages. Lena (Denial) asked, “Why can’t we just write a thesis and list our points?” Jake (Polarization) thought, “Their methods are slowing us down.” Aiko (Minimization) reasoned, “We all want the same thing---I’ll just tell my story, and they’ll understand.” And Chidi (Acceptance/Adaptation) noted that “Different styles work in different ways. Maybe we can blend a proverb and data to reach more people.” Are these stages or perspectives you’ve seen when working with others?

Now that you know more about intercultural communication competence, what are strategies for this team or for your own group projects to create more effective communication? Let’s work to understand differences and similarities further so that we can put some of these ideas together. As we do, apply them to your own writing spaces.

# Cultural Logics and Global Argumentation Styles

*Cultural logics* refers to the often-unspoken rules that shape how people from different cultures build and understand arguments. As you know, in the West, argumentation often follows a deductive model: present a thesis, support it with evidence, and end with a conclusion. The model prioritizes individual clarity, logical progression, and empirical data. But in many other cultures, something different may be happening, and these approaches are just as effective in the right contexts. These are stereotypes (Google “cultural heuristics” for more info), but important to be aware of and to embrace, as we have in this article:

- *East Asian rhetorical styles* may emphasize circular reasoning---building arguments through multiple perspectives, emotional appeal, or relational balance. Instead of “proving” a point, the goal might be to foster mutual understanding (Baral; Jensen). How have we used circular reasoning or repeated content in different ways in this essay?
- *African traditions* often rely on proverbs or oral storytelling. These stories convey values, wisdom, and community norms in a way that builds collective understanding (Rakrak). Are there words or wisdom we’ve shared to convey meaning effectively?
- *Indigenous rhetorical traditions* value holistic, cyclical narratives over linear argument. Stories often carry moral or spiritual messages, and knowledge is embedded in relationships, nature, and lived experience (Yunkaporta). What examples of lived experience do you recall from this essay?
- In *Middle Eastern* or *Latin American* cultures, honor and relational harmony are deeply tied to how arguments are framed. Maintaining respect can be as important as being “right” (Rama). We’ve discussed misinformation, ethics, and credibility as important for carrying respect and believability in your writing.

These differences in rhetorical moves don’t mean some ways are better or worse than others. They reflect different cultural priorities: individualism vs. collectivism, logic vs. ethics, data vs. experience. What’s most optimal? It depends. And it changes, constantly. And it’s usually a combination of several approaches. That’s why writing is easy, but writing effectively is not. It’s critical to know what strategies you have available to you, of course.

For instance, in East Asian rhetoric, circular reasoning is often preferred over linear progression (Baral; Jensen; Olga). Rather than focusing on cause and effect, East Asian argumentation emphasizes balance, harmony, and interdependence, drawing from philosophies like Confucianism, Taoism, and Buddhism that prioritize interconnectedness and the fluidity of human existence. Here, arguments aim to provide a holistic understanding of the issue, not just to persuade with empirical evidence and logic. The goal is to foster harmony and mutual understanding, not to win through deductive reasoning. The approach presents multiple perspectives and seeks common ground between conflicting viewpoints, prioritizing a deeper understanding over merely proving a point (Galtung).

Without understanding that such differences exist, we risk reinforcing the “rules of the culture of power” described by Lisa Delpit in *Other* *People’s Children: Cultural Conflict in the Classroom,* which is a great book that really helps you better understand the impact of cultural logics on day-to-day activities. For instance, Delpit argues that schools often fail to bridge gaps between teachers and students from diverse racial, cultural, and socio-economic backgrounds. When the dominant culture’s way of speaking, writing, and thinking is equated with success, students from marginalized communities who communicate differently are unfairly labeled as “deficient.” You may have experienced or have seen something like this. It’s a form of *silencing*. Silencing occurs when voices, perspectives, or communication styles that differ from the dominant norm are dismissed, devalued, or excluded, leaving individuals without a real means to participate. Have you felt silenced and/or alienated in some situation due to the way you communicate?

But when a student from a Native American background, say, uses a story instead of a thesis or claim, it’s not because they “don’t know how to write an argument.” It’s because storytelling is an argument. It’s stepping into a conversation using inductive instead of deductive reasoning (Graff and Birkenstein). It builds trust, conveys values, and honors tradition. When someone dismisses such an approach, without using empathy and discerning through their intercultural communication competence what’s going on, without understanding the cultural logics, they risk silencing and/or alienating others’ voices.

Storytelling has long been a primary tool for conveying persuasive messages, often serving to impart moral and spiritual teachings. Stories emphasize a holistic view and broader context over linear progression, reflecting the values and beliefs of the community. For instance, Native American cultures use allegories, myths, and parables to convey deep philosophical truths and societal norms (Jones). The logic of these arguments is circular or holistic, focusing on interconnectedness and the fluidity of human experience, rather than a direct “X leads to Y” structure. Instead of logical progression, their persuasive power lies in evoking emotion, providing moral guidance, and instilling a shared sense of identity. Similar storytelling traditions in parts of Africa and Asia convey a deep sense of interconnectedness between individuals, nature, and society, presenting a more complex worldview.

Consider the following regarding something you need to write: who is your audience? What moves them? A statistic might convince one reader. A parable or emotional narrative might reach another. Blending storytelling with logic, circular reasoning with direct statements, or personal reflection with citation-rich analysis can make your writing more inclusive as well as more effective. Of course, it may confuse readers. It all depends on what the audience knows and needs to know.

What counts as valid evidence in argumentation can vary between linear and other forms, and your credibility may depend on a broader purpose. In Western models, logic is emphasized, with emotional appeals used sparingly, typically as anecdotes or case studies. In contrast, other approaches may rely more on emotion, appeals to family values, social harmony, or collective well-being. One model may prioritize individual authority supported by independent evidence, while another values collective wisdom and respect for elders.

# Navigating Communication Conflicts

When you bump into different expectations in writing groups or classrooms, or experience some sort of “friction,” ICC gives you tools or strategies to move forward:

- *Pause and reflect:* Ask yourself, “Could this be a cultural difference?”
- *Seek understanding:* Ask your peer, “Can you tell me why you started with that story?”
- *Blend approaches:* Try opening with a proverb or piece of wisdom, then pivot to statistics. Use a narrative to frame your analysis.
- *Experiment with styles:* Reframe a paragraph using a different rhetorical tradition. Perhaps even play with AI to compare different ways of presenting information, or even to use some of the questions in this essay as AI prompts to intensify perspectives on what you’ve already written.
- *Collaborate mindfully:* When working in groups, discuss expectations explicitly. Invite different voices. Value multiple forms of evidence.

And there are a variety of ways to promote ICC to develop and deepen your communicative effectiveness and, therefore, your “intercultural mindset” (Hammer):

- Include evidence from non-Western traditions, such as storytelling or parables, appeals to tradition, communal experiences, or proverbial or indirect reasoning (Baral; Vaughn et al.).
- Encourage others to share persuasive techniques from their own cultures.
- Introduce frameworks like “they say/I say” while also critiquing their limitations (Graff and Birkenstein; Lancaster).
- (Re)consider the *rhetorical triangle* as dynamic and situated, not fixed. That’s the ever-flowing and changing relationship between reader, writer, and text.
- Value translanguaging and honoring others’ full linguistic resources.

Primary skills essential for better communication, which may not result from linear argumentation only, include understanding differing cultural norms of argumentation, active listening, respect, persuasion, relationship-building, and conflict resolution (AAC&U; Galtung; St.Amant). As Graff and Birkenstein argue in *They Say/I Say: The Moves That Matter in* *Academic Writing*, identified above to promote ICC, good argumentation is not just about presenting one’s own views. It involves engaging in an ongoing conversation with others, acknowledging their points, and responding to them. “They say” includes others’ views, arguments, and positions, often shaped by cultural and organizational values. “I Say” represents your contribution, presented after others’ perspectives are considered. This more inductive rhetorical model emphasizes engaging with ideas in balance, framing arguments in relation to existing perspectives, summarizing them fairly, and acknowledging multiple viewpoints to establish credibility.

Intercultural communication competence allows you to recognize differences (“they say”) and adapt others’ argumentation styles accordingly and perhaps more effectively (“I say”). In intercultural settings, people might have varying ways of listening or responding in a conversation. For example, translanguaging---where bi-/multilingual individuals use their full linguistic repertoire fluidly---challenges the notion of fixed language categories (García; Kachru). Respecting language fluidity as a strategic choice, rather than viewing it as a lack of proficiency, promotes deeper understanding as it requires you to focus on meaning rather than just form, interacting with empathy and adaptability (Irwansyah).

# Final Thoughts on What this Means for You

In many non-Western cultures, ethical considerations in argumentative writing extend beyond content to include the communicator’s credibility, fairness, and respect for the audience. The idea that “the text must stand for itself” doesn’t always apply. In some cultures, maintaining the dignity and respect of all parties is as important as the logical merit of the argument. For example, in many Middle Eastern cultures, the concept of “honor” shapes how arguments are framed and delivered. While the argument must be logically sound, it should also uphold dignity, avoid direct confrontation, and respect opposing viewpoints. In this context, persuasive effectiveness is determined by both content and delivery, emphasizing that the medium and message are inseparable---think globally, write locally (Rice and St.Amant; St.Amant).

Being able to adapt your communication across cultural lines is not just a nice-to-have skill. It’s essential, especially for engaging in effective and culturally and linguistically relevant scholarly collaborations across national contexts (Aquino-Sterling, Megale, and Pérez Cañado). Whether you’re preparing to work in education, public health, policy, law, design, tech, or another field, your ability to understand and be understood by people from different backgrounds is key. Writing is about more than clarity. It’s about connection. By learning ICC, you’re learning how to move through the world with more insight, flexibility, and respect. You’re learning to listen, not just argue. To connect, not just convince. Remember to develop your empathy, celebrate translanguaging, avoid ethnocentricism, practice intercultural sensitivity, move along the intercultural development continuum toward adaptation, and think globally with your writing/thinking projects.

# Teacher Resources

# Discussion Questions

1. What are *cultural logics*?[^tr-1] How can intercultural communication competencies help navigate them?

2. Why is *intercultural communication competence* important for effective argumentative writing in today’s diverse academic and professional environments?

3. If collaborating with people who may have facility with multiple languages, may have significant experiences with other cultural values, or come from organizations with specific and unique purposes, how can a writer go about determining the best ways to determine *what audiences know and need to know* to compose effective arguments?

4. What are the strengths and limitations of *linear argumentation* in a globalized, multicultural context?

5. How do the *stages of intercultural sensitivity* in the IDC improve argumentative writing, and why is it important to move from ethnocentric to ethnorelative perspectives?

6. How can understanding *different norms for argumentation* (e.g. directness vs. indirectness, storytelling vs. logical reasoning) improve one’s ability to argue persuasively across cultures?

7. How can *ethical considerations* in argumentation differ across cultures?

8. How does understanding *principles of intercultural communication competence* improve effective communication and conflict resolution?
