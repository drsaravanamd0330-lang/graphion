# Introduction

Dear students:

This is an apology.

We are a pair of university instructors in Western Canada (well, actually Keith Harms is a writing instructor, and Victoria Surtees is an inter-

nationalization specialist and applied linguist--but close enough). We have noticed that some students struggle with writing that involves making personal connections to course concepts or readings, what is often called *reflective writing*. In these assignments, students are expected to share their own ideas, experiences, opinions, goals, or emotions.

Usually, reflective assignments start with questions like:

- What about the reading resonated with you and why?
- Describe your writing process. Which parts felt challenging, which parts felt good?

Hot tip for students: For reflective assignments, you are not supposed to repeat or copy what you read (except to help the prof understand how your opinion connects to the course materials). And you are definitely not supposed to ask generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools like ChatGPT to come up with an opinion for you (profs really hate that). That defeats the whole purpose of the assignment, which is to actively use your brain to make connections between what you already know and what you’re learning.

Reflective assignments are a standard assignment for writing and arts classes in North America. In fact, your instructor is probably making you read this because they plan to assign a reflection or two. Recently, we have been chatting with undergraduate international students like Vanshika, our co-author, about their writing experiences in higher education. Through our conversations, we’ve realized that even though we are trying to empower and connect with you by giving reflection assignments, in some cases, we are actually doing the opposite.

We want to make amends. So, in this essay, we share what we learned from Vanshika, a multilingual international student from India, who helped us see that our reasons for assigning reflections don’t match the realities, priorities, and past experiences of students like her. After you read it, we hope you will *reflect on* the ways culture and colonialism might be shaping your and your instructors’ approaches to reflective writing.

In solidarity,

Victoria Surtees and Keith Harms

# Instructor and Student Perspectives on Reflections

In general, profs believe that like vegetables, reflections are good for you. Research on learning shows that reflecting explicitly about something you’ve learned is a way to actively use the new knowledge. That, in turn, helps you retain it and reuse it in future. Kathleen Blake Yancey, a writing studies scholar who has studied the benefits of reflective writing, explains:

> First, for learning to take hold, we must “do,” engaging in experience … but we must also think, or reflect, on that learning for it to make sense, and when we do, our performance improves. Second, such reflecting contributes to self-efficacy precisely because it helps us understand that we have learned (even if not always successfully); how we have learned; and how we might continue to learn. (8)

In other words, the process of reflecting makes things explicit that might otherwise remain hidden in our subconscious. And in making these processes explicit, it then becomes more likely that we will apply that learning to relevant situations later. In education research, this is called *transfer*, and findings from transfer research are very consistent: transfer is not automatic, and in fact, can be quite rare. But this same research also says that reflecting on what and how we learned makes transfer much more likely to happen.

We (Keith and Victoria) always take time to explain to students why reflections are like vegetables. And Vanshika said that her teachers did, too. But to her, reflections didn’t seem practical or like a good use of her time, even though her instructors told her why reflections are good for learning. As we thought about it, we realized that both Vanshika and we, as instructors, had other understandings of reflection assignments that usually go unspoken. We share those with you here. But let us be clear: our goal isn’t to convince you that we’re right or that instructors always know better. We are sharing our rationales so that we can examine where cultural expectations come into the picture. After each rationale, we present Vanshika’s perspective as a counter-rationale. You’ll notice that Vanshika’s reflections are in quotes. That’s because Vanshika prefers to engage in spoken reflection and we wished to honour her voice and process. As you read through our written reflections, and her spoken reflections, see if you can spot the cultural mismatches between the instructors’ ideas and Vanshika’s desires and experiences.

# Rationale #1: Process Matters

# Keith’s Perspective

One thing that you may not be aware of is that the best writing teachers see writing more as a verb than a noun, a thing you do, not a finished product. From this perspective, if we base your grade entirely on finished papers, as was the standard practice for decades in writing classes, we miss all the decisions made along the way that ended in that polished piece. In other words, focusing on a finished, polished essay hides the learning process. Further, empowering you to make decisions during the writing process is better for transfer than giving you a detailed list of expectations that you follow diligently on the first attempt. When you just follow a list of expectations, the decision-making process is taken from you, and so you’re not actually learning to improve your writing. Many writing instructors even believe that grading a final product at all (as opposed to the process, or your reflections *on* the process) is counterproductive because it encourages students to focus more on scores than learning.

As a new teacher, reflective essays were a revelation. I felt that I suddenly had a window into my students’ thinking that essays and research papers alone didn’t provide. When I first read students’ descriptions of their learning processes, not only did I feel like I was working with more information to assess their learning, but I also felt (and still feel) that it is unjust to base the assessment of an entire semester’s learning on polished writing, like essays or research papers. And I’m not being dramatic when I use the word “unjust.” I truly believe that.

# Vanshika’s Response

When we shared our reasoning for assigning reflection with Vanshika, she generously shared a few reasons that the importance of reflection assignments was just not on her radar when she first arrived in Canada.

> “In the English class, you’re given questions, you have to do reading, you come across a lot of words that you don’t understand. Even sometimes, I do not understand. I’m like, what is this? What does this line means? And I’m like searching on Google or something like that. … Even when you have like words that are known to your understanding, but still when they’re put into a sentence, you do not get what, what’s it, right? And even, like, I’m talking about *resonating* and *metaphor*. If I go back like two to three years back, I was not able to understand like, what do these words mean? So, it’s, like, confusing. And eventually, you do not have like one subject to focus on, you have many. [...] You know, we have hectic schedules, we face many issues like homesickness. Even when I came here, like, I didn’t know how to cook. I was learning how to cook. And that’s overwhelming when you just come, when you spend, like, nineteen years of your life in a country where you didn’t do anything without your parents and you’re coming here, you have to find a job, you have to have a work life balance.”

> “So, I think if you consider the one who just landed here, for her, it would be like nothing. It would be, like, what the hell is going on? I do not like this stuff. I do not even appreciate it. Why the professor is giving it?”

In other words, Vanshika was just learning to navigate an overwhelming number of courses and assignments and didn’t yet see the significance of reflective assignments. Those abstract reflection prompts just didn’t make sense to her or seem important. On top of all that, she was learning to live far away from the support of family members. Without using these words, what Vanshika is describing is the cognitive overload many newcomers experience when adjusting to a new way of life.

# Rationale #2: It’s Culturally Responsive

# Keith and Victoria’s Perspective

For instructors like us, we want you to feel like your culture and experience has a place in our classroom. In our institution, and we suspect in many others, there is a growing feeling that reflections create space for students to express their cultural perspectives in ways that other assignments, like exams or research papers, don’t. Indigenous Canadian scholar Jo Chrona of the Kitsumkalum First Nation explains the importance of reflective learning from an Indigenous perspective:

> It allows us to connect our learning to who we are and what we bring to our learning. … It is connected to being patient with our learning and how we communicate with each other. It is a part of the process of being thoughtful about how what we are learning connects to ourselves and our communities, and to our past, present, and future. (130)

We also see reflections as a way to challenge traditional assessments, like multiple-choice exams and timed essay writing, that have excluded students based on cultural and linguistic identities. You may or may not already be aware of the debates surrounding standardized testing. Some universities have even stopped accepting standardized test scores because the tests contain the cultural biases of the people who write them. Further, more economically advantaged students can pay for classes that prepare them for these standardized tests that disadvantaged students can’t. We feel these kinds of inequalities are especially problematic in multicultural, immigrant nations with histories of colonial domination like Canada or the United States. One common response to this problem, especially in writing classrooms, has been to use reflective writing prompts in which students describe their own learning and previous experiences in school rather than forcing them to take tests that might exclude their own cultural experiences. We definitely have this in mind when we award a significant portion of your grade for reflection. After all, the thinking goes, everyone knows how to talk about their own experiences.

# Vanshika’s Response

In speaking back to us about the issue of culture, Vanshika highlights how reflection assignments were a totally new genre for her and not really part of her “culture” at all.

> “The Indian system usually works on theory-based. It’s not practical. It’s not computer-based.

> … So Indian students, specifically Indian students, I would say, have never done this. Even me, when I just came here, and I was like, what is this? I have never done this. I have never written like that. And I was, like, I had zero knowledge about, like, citation. … It’s totally different versus when you are asked to write on computer, because you have to give your thoughts, and it’s all about your thoughts.”

> “I do believe that was the first time … is when I was giving my IELTS exam[^2] … There’s an essay which comes, like, opinion-based, so you give opinions there but if I think, like, going to

> high school or the school which I attended like the junior school, it was the English stuff, we used to give opinions. But it was more of like … you’re given a situation where … you have this opinion and you have to write about it, so it’s quite different. So yeah, I think when I was doing IELTS, that was one thing when I gave my own opinions writing.”

> “Also, there are, like, some cases because our cultures are totally different, right, and when there is some sort of reading, if the reading is based on, like, Western culture and you have never heard about some phenomena, so how can you have an opinion, right? For, like, for me, when I just came here I didn’t know why do you celebrate Halloween. What’s Halloween? I don’t even know. How can I have opinion on that thing, right? When you don’t understand something, you cannot have an opinion, so you use AI at some point of time.”

From her response, we came to understand that Vanshika’s previous schooling had not valued this kind of writing, and she had little experience with it. What’s more, she felt like profs often asked her to share opinions on materials she didn’t have the cultural context to understand, and that it made sense--and was maybe even justifiable--why some students might turn to AI to fill the gap.

# Rationale #3: Connection and Trust-building

# Victoria’s Perspective

If I’m honest, the reflections that get the highest scores from me are the ones in which students write about personal revelations or growth: the more emotional and personal, the better. Reflections make me feel connected to students, and like I am making a difference. Reflections also help me see where I could improve my teaching. When a student admits they didn’t understand something, that isn’t a “bad” reflection. It’s helpful. It’s a sign of trust and I value it. I think that sometimes, students think that I have a correct answer or opinion in mind, and they get stressed trying to figure out what I want. But truthfully, all I want is to know what they noticed, liked, hated or what worked for them. I get disappointed when students just summarize whatever they read. For me, that’s not the point of reflections. I already know the reading. I assigned it!

Tell me something I don’t know. Something personal, so I know it’s real. When students started using AI to write reflections, it robbed assignments of their authenticity. It stole my opportunity for connection. I was resentful, especially because I viewed reflections as easy grades for students. I mean, it doesn’t even involve extra work or research. Is it so much to ask students to just write something from the heart? Telling my students to loosen up and write more informally never seems to help.

# Vanshika’s Response

When we asked Vanshika what we could do differently to support students in their reflective work, she was not shy about giving direct advice: more talking, less writing.

> “I do believe, like, you can, maybe, you guys can improve that by more of talking instead of, like, I think, giving written assignments. … because talking and giving your thoughts out, I think it’s more easier than to write that.”

> “I have seen, like, people do have like their hobbies to, like, jot down notes and all that stuff, like diary writing. I was never into it. … I am more of, like, talking to people. Although I am an introvert, I just talk to few people whom I just know I have, like, a very close circle. I just have two, three people whom I just talk and I just tell them that if I’m talking to you, you should feel like you are blessed by the God that I’m talking to you.”

> “So I think talking is more, it would be more appreciated by students than giving written assignments … because when you write you need proper words, but when you talk, you can be, like, a bit humble.”

Vanshika’s reference to humility took us both by surprise--you might even say she humbled us and gave us a lot to consider about how we assign reflections.

# Did You Spot The Cultural Bias?

As you read, you probably noticed a few differences between Vanshika’s values, practices and experiences and ours. After reflecting on our reflections, we identified four assumptions that lead to biased outcomes for multilingual and international students (and probably others, too).

# Assumption #1: Reflective Writing Is Natural And Easy

Just because you dance bhangra does not mean you can dance ballet or hip hop.

If you’ve never written a reflection before, it’s bound to be challenging, just like it was for Vanshika, who explained in Rationale #2 that the only time she had ever shared her opinion through academic writing was on the English language test she took to be admitted to university. We both thought that informal writing would naturally be easy for students, as we highlighted in Rationale #2 when we said that “everyone knows how to talk about their own experiences.” But literacies researchers like Brian Street tell us writing different types of texts is a learned social practice. No type of writing is easy. We are both used to writing academic articles with at least fifty citations. For this piece, we were supposed to write something research-informed but only use a few sources and speak directly to students. It was hard. So, it’s all about what you’re used to.

Even though we teach the structure of essays, reports, and memos, most instructors we know (including us) don’t share sample reflections, even though there are some very conventional phrases and structures for reflection. What’s more, we didn’t even consider that Vanshika had never done her written schoolwork on a computer before arriving in Canada. If we really think about it, there are a lot of skills involved in that, too, and we don’t teach those, either.

Oops. Our bad.

# Assumption #2: The Reflections Are The Problem

Expectations about what should be learned in the classroom differed vastly between us and Vanshika. In his Rationale #1, Keith explained that he’s teaching habits, strategies, and ways of thinking. He sees learning processes as more important than learning products (e.g., a polished essay) and so he gives higher grade value to process. This way of teaching contrasts sharply with Vanshika’s experience in India, where teachers are responsible for delivering knowledge and textbooks serve as study aids for exams. For her, the goal of university education is accumulating knowledge--in her words in Rationale #2, “it’s theory-based.” From this perspective, it’s only logical that assessment should be an opportunity to demonstrate what you know, not writing about how you feel or what you don’t know. That’s probably why in Rationale #1, Vanshika expresses her frustration and explains that at the beginning, she didn’t appreciate reflection or know what the hell it was for and was frankly too overwhelmed by everything else going on to care.

Valuing process and skills development is typical of the “flipped classroom” model, and it is the dominant model of instruction in North American classrooms. In the flipped classroom, students read or learn on their own first, then come to class to discuss what they learned. The theory is that if students read first, then class time can be used to clarify and apply concepts, allowing for deeper engagement. The system in India is what Paolo Freire, an educational philosopher from Brazil, has referred to as the “banking model” of education (72). It sees the role of education as depositing information into students’ brain banks for later withdrawal. Underlying these two models are different values and understandings of what education is for.

We should briefly explore where the banking model came from. Exam-focused systems that prioritize rote learning come from colonial occupation. For example, in India, a document called “Macaulay’s Minute” from 1835 explains that the goal of Indian education is to create a class of people who were, “Indian by blood and colour, but English by tastes, opinions, morals, and intellect (19.18. To sum up).” The aim was to ensure that Indian subjects served British interests, rather than the students’ own communities and values. For many instructors like us, centering reflective learning is an attempt to counteract the colonial goals expressed by Macaulay. However, as Vanshika has pointed out, just expecting you to know how to reflect in ways that we expect is just another way of unfairly imposing our values on students.

Perspectives like Keith’s that value process over product challenged Vanshika’s understanding of education and asked her to make a substantial values shift at a time when her whole world had been turned upside down: she was literally learning the basics of survival--like cooking meals-- in a new country without the support of parents. So, it’s not surprising that Vanshika viewed these assignments as less important. Shifting values is hard. If an instructor told you suddenly that swearing in class was important for learning, would you believe them right away? Would you be able to just start swearing at your teacher and peers? I bet you wouldn’t. And that’s why students can’t just suddenly open up in their reflections just because we say it’s good for them.

# Cultural Assumption #3: Reflective Assignments Should Be Written

Why do we have to demonstrate our learning in writing? Vanshika invited us to reflect on this question, explaining that humility--or a “humble attitude” as she calls it in Rationale #3--was something better demonstrated in conversation with others. We found that Vanshika was indeed more open, vulnerable, and authentic in her spoken interaction with us--and that’s one reason we included her thoughts in their “spoken” form in this paper.

By lamenting opportunities for oral reflection, Vanshika, perhaps unknowingly, drew our attention to a bias that has existed in the colonial education system for a long time: that for knowledge to be legitimate, it has to be written. In the documentary *People Are Knowledge*, which Keith has assigned in many of his writing courses, Geetha Narayanan, director of Srishti School of Art and Design in Bangalore asks, “coming from a country like India … where so little is written down, do we then say we know nothing?” Of course, we would answer no. But this was not always the case. The privileging of written information is so deeply ingrained in our academic system that Vanshika’s suggestion to allow student reflections to be orally delivered was a total revelation to both of us, despite our career-long commitments to decolonizing our teaching.

# Cultural Assumption #4: We Are Doing You A Favor By Allowing Your Cultural Stories

Finally, in Rationale #2, we assumed that you’d like these assignments because we were asking you to write about how your cultural experiences are connected to course concepts. We believed that by setting you up as experts, you would feel confident and included and that, just maybe, we were cooler than other profs that didn’t give you that opportunity.

But after talking with Vanshika, we realized there are lots of reasons why this “cultural sharing” might not work for you. How weird must it feel to tell your prof your ideas and opinions when the prof is supposed to be the expert? And maybe the experiences that might be relevant to share didn’t happen in English and wouldn’t make sense to an outsider. Would your teacher even get it? And if they did get it, would they really think that it was appropriate? Or worse, what if the instructor or other students assumed that everyone that looks and sounds like you has the same experiences or goals? What would others in your community say if you shared those experiences with an outsider--and for marks no less! In most cultures we know of, you would never *require* someone to share these types of stories, and the experiences certainly wouldn’t be graded.

Of course, many students have told us that sharing their experiences is extremely affirming and making personal connections to course content *is* an essential learning strategy recommended by research in culturally responsive teaching (Hammond, 122). The problem arises when we ask students to do this work before relationships and trust have been established. North America often positions reflections as individual work, but students like Vanshika teach us that reflections are gifted to another only when trust has been built. It is a relational practice, not an individual practice.

# What Did We Learn?

Our conversation with Vanshika opened many doors for us. Here are some of the ways our priorities and values have shifted because of our conversation.

Vanshika: “When I just came here, when I just landed here, when I used to get these assignments, I was, like, really frustrated … but now when I just think now, I understood that, like, it helped me to improve my thinking skills. I am more opinionated now. I have more opinions. I can judge, like, which thing is right, and which is wrong. So, I think all these, like, reflective essays help you to, like, generate opinions and when you, like, read from other people, you improve your opinions. It doesn’t mean that if you have an opinion, it’s always right. So, when you read other people, you evolve them. You make changes. You make them better. … Doing these reflective things doesn’t just improve your like skills in academics. I think it has, a bit, helped me in the social area as well because I have more opinions because I can now judge things, like, would this be better or this be better because you have that thinking level. So, I think yeah, it has helped me and now I don’t feel frustrated.”

Keith: If I, as an instructor, really want to use reflective writing to understand my students, I need to think more holistically about reflective writing prompts. Further, although I have been one of the few voices in my own English department to advocate for more audio, video, or mixed media assignments, I failed to think beyond written response for my reflective assignments. Integrating more oral-based reflections, whether through video or in class discussion, is something I can make more use of. Victoria: I really took advantage of my position as instructor to ask for personal narratives that not all students were comfortable sharing. As I think back, I can see that the ones I thought demonstrated “good reflective capability” were coming from students that looked and sounded like me, a White, middle-class English-speaker. They probably felt safe opening up to me because they felt reasonably confident that I would understand and align with their experiences. Going forward, I’ll think more carefully about when and how to do reflections in my courses, and how I actively build trust with students before engaging in that work.

To conclude, we just want to thank Vanshika for what she has taught us. It was illuminating. We hope that you can have a similar discussion with your own peers and instructors. And then, maybe you can write a reflection about it.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

We wrote this essay in the spirit of teachers and students learning from each other. Collaborating with Vanshika helped us both to see reflective writing in new ways.[^tr-1] Although it only expresses one student’s experience, we hope that it will function similarly for you. We also wanted to be careful not to create a piece that asks students to reject their previous schooling outright. After all, aren’t reflective assignments about integration of the past with the present and the future? Isn’t that exactly the thing that makes them culturally sustaining? But our own experiences as instructors and as specialists who provide instructor training has told us that many of us are doing exactly that without realizing it.

If we return to our initial comments, the very first bias we need to confront is the discourse that international or multilingual students struggle with reflection. Vanshika’s comments in this article demonstrate a high level of reflective capacity. She is not struggling with the act of reflection. Instead, what Vanshika struggled with was:

1. Understanding written reflections as a legitimate academic practice;

2. The language and cultural context of the readings she was supposed to reflect on; and

3. The meanings of reflective prompts. We realized that we don’t very often teach ways to structure reflec-

tions in helpful language; we just expect that students will inherently know it. The current North American obsession with individual achievement, signified by the many online influencers selling “personal growth” and “wellness” strategies, and the emphasis on reflective learning in K-12 education in North America means that many students who have grown up in this context will already be familiar with ways of using language to “do self-reflection,” and so they likely will have an advantage in this kind of writing. People who grew up in other communities may be more familiar with other ways to do reflection, such as through prayer. And we should probably do away with the phrase “resonate with.” Though this is a common phrasing in reflective assignments, it is one that can trip up students who use English as an additional language, even experienced ones, such as Vanshika.

As we previously explained, one reason we assign reflections is because we feel they level the playing field for students, unlike exams. However, because the flipped classroom model usually requires students to do the reflective assignment *before* discussing the reading in class, reflections can sometimes exacerbate inequities. As Vanshika points out, how can students reflect on something if they don’t understand it in the first place? By giving students reading without previewing the technical terms, the reason for assigning it, or background or context, we advantage students that have prior knowledge of the language and context in the reading.

Ideally, this essay would be assigned early in the semester, especially if your class makes frequent use of reflection, and especially if your institution has a high population of international, multilingual or multicultural students who might not be used to, or even aware of, our teaching methods. For example, Keith imagines including this in an introductory unit that introduces students to what to expect from their first-year writing course. This unit might include other *Writing Spaces* chapters such as Taczak’s essay on transfer, DePiero and Dippre’s piece that enacts reflection and process in the essay itself, and/or any of the many culturally sustaining pieces such as Alvarez et al. or pieces in this volume by Li, Jimenez and Salonga, or Sayed. It would also work well with McIntosh’s piece about the importance of low-stakes writing.

# Discussion Questions

1. Vanshika said that the first time she ever did reflective writing was when she took her IELTS exam, an exam that measures English language proficiency. What has your experience been with reflective writing? Is this your first time encountering it? If so, how do you feel about it? If it is not your first time, what advice would you give to those students for whom it is their first time?

2. What parts of Vanshika’s experience sound similar to your own experience as a student? What parts are different?

3. Thinking about the ways your current writing instructor talks about reflective assignments, which of the three rationales discussed in this essay sound closest to the kind of reflections your instructor is asking you to do? Be sure to look ahead in your syllabus to future assignments. If you are unsure, it’s okay to guess or even ask your instructor.

4. Having read these rationales for assigning reflective writing, how do you think you can use that knowledge to approach future reflective assignments that you are given?

5. The authors identified four biased assumptions in their thinking about reflective writing. Can you identify any additional biased assumptions about your learning that you wish your instructors knew about?
