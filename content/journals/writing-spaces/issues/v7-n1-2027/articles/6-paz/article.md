# Introduction

I’m going to let you in on a secret about me: finding and reading sources is my least favorite part of my writing process. That’s right! --- I, a college professor of writing, find sources difficult. If I’m procrastinating, it’s probably because I need to read more and just don’t want to do it. It takes a lot of time to find research and scholarship, to determine what’s relevant and useful, and then to read it. I get anxious, worrying that I won’t find *the* article or book---the one that I must cite if I’m going to be taken seriously. It wasn’t always this way. As a young student, I thought sources were easy. A couple of google searches, some library database queries---I could find my required number of sources, dig out some cool-sounding quotes, and be done. But as I learned more about writing, I also learned how important sources are. I learned how important it is to read many essays and research articles about a topic. And I learned how this practice is essential to making new knowledge that builds up academic and professional communities. In other words, if I want to meaningfully contribute to industries, organizations, or academic fields that I care about, I need to read sources and use them thoughtfully in writing, difficult as that process may be.

I want to share with you what I’ve learned. First, it’s important to understand why we use sources, especially in academic writing. Then we’ll talk about how to integrate sources in your writing, not just by citing them but by rhetorically deploying summary, paraphrase and quotation. These three tools each have strengths and weaknesses that require you to consciously choose between them. But this essay doesn’t just discuss how to use sources; it is an example of how to use sources too! To help you see source use in action, I’ve highlighted the different kinds of source use in my essay. Summaries will be bolded. Paraphrases are underlined. And quotes (with signal phrases or other integration) are in *italics*. As you read, try to keep track of how often I use each kind of integration, when I use it, and how it connects to other instances of source use around it.

# The Why of Source Use

As a student, I had many inaccurate beliefs about source use. These misconceptions kept me from understanding why I should invest time in research and reading before writing. Why go through the process of finding, reading, and using sources? What strength does it provide to your writing process and your essays? I didn’t have good answers to these questions because I didn’t understand source use correctly. Science teachers and researchers Julia Gooding and Bill Metz describe the danger of misconceptions in exactly this way: we form wrong ideas based on our opinions or personal experiences and those wrong ideas interfere with our later learning or activities. To improve our learning and our ability to apply that learning, we have to correct those misconceptions. Just like I did, writers often have many misconceptions about why we use sources. Here are some common ones:

- You use sources to “prove” that your argument is right.
- You use sources only in school or for academic writing.
- You use sources only for writing in English or writing classes.

None of these are accurate, and this essay could present many different reasons why each is inaccurate. Instead, let’s focus on the disciplinary and rhetorical purpose source use serves.

Research and source use are necessary because they build academic communities and connect your writing to those communities. Academic fields are simply communities of researchers who work together to solve problems of common interest. They pose difficult questions to one another and answer those questions with their research and critical thought. Political scientist Michael Oakeshott referred to this as *“the conversation* *of mankind”* (199)---an unending exchange of ideas between people learning about life and the world. *To be successful participants in this conversation, however, we must learn “to recognize the voices, to distinguish the* *proper occasions of utterance, and…acquire the intellectual and moral habits* *appropriate to conversation”* (199). Our writing, in other words, is part of a conversation taking place in a particular community. Writing scholar Dan Melzer explains that these are discourse communities, groups of people organized around a common interest and shared means of communication and language. To participate meaningfully in our communities--to say something that moves their conversation forward and that the community will actually listen to--we first must learn to listen: listen to who is part of this conversation (recognize the voices), when they speak (the proper occasions of utterance), and how they share their ideas (the habits appropriate to conversation). In academic communities, research and reading teach us these things. By listening to the conversation, you become knowledgeable about where the conversation has been, what topics people are interested in, what kinds of evidence and arguments are valid to them, and how to make your ideas interesting to them.

Source use is the application of that knowledge. Used appropriately, sources can inform not only what you say but also how you say it, demonstrating your understanding of and respect for that conversation. Perhaps more importantly, the source you use identifies which community and conversation your writing responds to and situates you within that community as a peer to those voices. Rhetorical theorist Kenneth Burke refers to this strategy as identification. He suggests that persuasion is best achieved when the speaker and audience identify with one another--that is, they agree that they share something in common that aligns their interests (20). *Burke states, “You persuade a man only insofar as you can talk his* *language by speech, gesture, tonality, order, image, attitude, idea, identifying* *your way with his. [...] And you give the ‘signs’...by deference to an audience’s* *‘opinion’” (55).* The audience sees you are one of them because you know their ideas, speak their language, and address their concerns. As you use sources, you identify yourself with the community and connect your ideas to the issues they care about.

With all this in mind, effective source use establishes your ethos as a writer. You may be familiar with *ethos* as a word that means “credibility,” but *writing researcher Kathleen J. Ryan offers a different definition: “the* *strategic positioning of the rhetor in relationship to the audience and/or community” (129).* She tells us that we construct our ethos to tell the audience who we are---to name our identity and tell the reader who we are in relation to them. When the Greek philosopher Aristotle wrote about *ethos* in his book on rhetoric, he described why the audience could believe in you, including (1) your expert knowledge and experience, (2) your morality and virtue, and (3) your sincere desire to help the audience. Source use illustrates all three kinds of ethos. Not because a published writer is saying the same thing you are, but because you have absorbed the conversation of the community and can write intelligently about it (knowledge), because you have respected the community’s conversations by reading them and referencing them (virtue), and you care about the issues they are discussing and want to help solve them (desire to help). Using sources helps you construct your ethos and identity as someone the audience can identify with and trust.

From this perspective, we can revisit our earlier misconceptions to see how those beliefs might change. First, rather than using sources to “prove” your argument is right, research and reading help you to understand what conversations you can join and contribute to. Instead of choosing a topic first and finding sources to support your perspective, you should use research before determining your topic or argument to discover what information the community already has and what opinions have been supported or rejected and why. Second, when reframing source use as references in a conversation, you may begin to see this practice everywhere, beyond academic writing and English classes. Your professional, civic, and hobby communities are constantly engaged in conversations that build community and solve problems, and they too are using sources, though the “voices” they cite might not be academic articles. They might be government reports, local news stories, top comments on internet threads, or the local expert at the hobby shop. Here too, you need to learn how to identify yourself with the community you want to be a part of. Without research, reading, and listening, it may feel impossible to make a real, meaningful contribution to their conversations.

Our discussion so far points to the rhetorical purpose behind source use. Why go through the process of finding, reading, and using sources? What strength does it provide to your writing process and your essays? Source use helps readers to identify your writing as belonging to their community and to accept it as an appropriate contribution to their conversation. Your source use demonstrates that you and your writing are trustworthy because you are knowledgeable, respectful, and well-intentioned. You have taken the previous discussions of the community seriously and used them to create a new response that offers your new ideas. Accordingly, the audience will take you seriously and engage with your ideas. However, to best leverage these rhetorical advantages, you also need to understand how to integrate those sources in your writing most effectively.

# The How of Source Use

Now that you know why we use sources, we turn to how we use sources. There are two big misconceptions about how to use sources that might get in the way, however, so we need to talk about those first. Those misconceptions are:

1. All that matters is that my citation is correct.

2. Quotes are the best way to put sources in my paper. These two inaccurate beliefs mislead writers into lazy source use. They

suggest that using sources is a mechanical exercise with three easy steps: find quote, copy quote, cite quote. Sandra Jamieson and Rebecca Moore Howard, writing researchers whose Citation Project studies how students use sources, examined the essays of 174 students, totaling 1911 instances of source use across those essays. *They report that “94 percent* *of the 1,911 citations were written from isolated sentences in the source text”* (123). In other words, 94% of the time, those citations only referred to a single sentence in the source, ignoring or failing to represent the bigger ideas and arguments that sentence was a part of. Not only that, but 77 percent of citations only used a quote from the first 3 pages of the source. These two patterns of behavior tell us that students aren’t reading the sources they cite in their essays nor are they trying to understand the concepts in those essays. Instead, they prefer the practice of *“sentence-mining”*---skimming sources until they find a “money quote” to copy into their own essays. This formula of find-copy-cite isn’t actually an act of composing; it’s just, well, copying, and it can’t leverage all that source use can do to identify with your readers, situate your contribution, and establish your ethos. To take full advantage of the rhetorical power of sources--that is, the power that source use has to engage and persuade your reader--you should make an intentional choice between different methods for integrating sources: summary, paraphrase, and quotation. To be sure, quotation is a powerful and useful tool, but it is only one possibility for how to integrate a source.

When you choose to use a source, you should think through your rhetorical purpose in using that source. What information does this source offer? How does that information complement the ideas you’re developing in your essay? And, especially, how can the audience see that connection best? How you answer these questions should determine if you use a quote, paraphrase, or summary. To better understand the rhetorical strengths and weaknesses---because they all do have weaknesses---let’s look at each of these integration strategies more closely.

## Quotation

You already know what a quote is. After all, it is the most popular form of source integration. Jamieson and Howard’s Citation Project found that 46% of the time, students used a quote when they cited a source in their essays (122). And as we read earlier, their findings imply that, most of the time, students read a few pages of a source and then copy the first quote that matches what they were already looking for. These kinds of quotes may deliver a punchy soundbite, but this process won’t help you to learn the conversation or community. When we are impacted by something we read, it’s easy to imagine that another reader will have the same experience. Quotes seem like an easy way to transport that experience from another’s text to our own. Unfortunately, it doesn’t always work that way. Without the context of the original essay, your quotes might not be as effective as they might sound. It’s essential to read the entire text to understand how those powerful quotes connect to the writer’s overall message and to the concerns and interests of the community. Even then, you need to consider if a quote is the best way to deliver that idea to your readers. Quotes are just one method for source use; it should not be the default.

Quotes are powerful because they capture the beauty, strength, and authority of someone else’s language. Many writers have a distinct style to their writing, and a quote is an excellent way of allowing readers to engage that writer’s unique voice. You might find that particular words or sentence structures capture an idea with precision, and you don’t want to mess with it. This is also a great use of a quote. Finally, when a claim or concept feels controversial or hard to believe, a quote allows you to borrow the authority of another writer to deliver that claim. For example, did you know that after natural disasters, many people donate used clothes for victims? *However, according to disaster researcher Bendeguz Papp, “This often* *leads to accumulations of huge quantities of useless garments that victims cannot or will not wear”* (254). Depending on your audience, this claim might not seem to make sense, so you might use a quote to demonstrate that this claim was made by other researchers and you have not misconstrued or misinterpreted it.

With that said, quotes ought to be used sparingly. In fact, quotes can backfire and actually take away from your credibility. If you don’t fully understand a quote or the argument it originally developed, quotation can become a crutch. Quoting ideas you don’t understand often leads to confusion for you and your reader. If you use them too often, quotes can overwhelm your own voice and perspective as a writer. Readers come to your text to hear your thoughts, but if quotes interrupt your voice too often, it can be difficult for readers to hear you at all. You risk your essay becoming a list of quotes, offering no contributions of your own.

To make the most of your quotes, it’s important to integrate them thoughtfully. Each time you use a source, whether it’s a quote or otherwise, the reader will encounter a different voice that originates in a different text and is distinct from your writerly voice. Without warning the reader, the sudden switch between two voices will be jarring. Writing scholar Kyle Stedman explains that a source without introduction feels like *“armadillo* *roadkill”*: when an animal--an armadillo, in this case--suddenly jumps in front of your car while driving. You hit the animal and wonder where it came from. *He summarizes this experience with the statement: “It just came* *out of nowhere”* (244). Source use should not feel so sudden or unexpected. Use signal phrases that introduce the text or the writer so the reader knows whose voice this is and why it’s worth listening to. Further, you should consider whether your quote needs the entire sentence, just a phrase, or just a word from your source. Quoting smaller portions of the text can help integrate the two voices more smoothly and create more emphasis on what is important in the quote.

Take a minute now to go back and look at all the quotes I’ve used in this essay, which are *italicized*. Pay close attention to the signal phrasing. You’ll notice that I usually name the writer and his credentials. How often are the quotes just a few words? Full sentences? My goal as a writer was to use quotes in specific moments of emphasis---to deliver important ideas in a dynamic way.

## Summary

A summary is a condensing of a text or part of a text. Summaries are simple and brief overviews, allowing readers to have a big-picture understanding of an idea or text quickly. They can be as short as a sentence or as long as a paragraph, depending on the level of detail you want to provide. You can summarize a whole book or just a specific chapter, just as you can summarize an entire movie or just one specific scene. Summary is an essential skill for research and leveraging its rhetorical power in writing. Summary is the tool that demonstrates that you, as a writer and reader, have engaged and understood a whole text (or a significant part of it). Jamieson and Howard use the metaphor of *“digestion”* to explain the strength of summary (127). When you digest food, your body consumes it, breaks it down, and absorbs its nutrients. Applied to source use, summary suggests a healthy digestive process for sources: you’ve read the source (consuming), you’ve understood it (breaking it down), and have learned from the material (absorbing). And because you’ve done that successfully, your understanding is strong, and you can explain it to others.

And that is part of its rhetorical strength--demonstrating that you understood a text as whole. A good summary---one that accurately represents the text’s ideas---shows the reader that you have read the literature thoroughly. You show that you’re a responsible, informed member of this community, using summary for the rhetorical purpose of making information from a source accessible to your audience. For readers who are unfamiliar with the summarized text, you are providing context: information that helps them to understand the ideas from the text and the arguments you will make based on those ideas. For readers who are familiar with text, a good summary proves your arguments are based on accurate understanding of that text’s arguments. As your summaries draw attention and connections to specific concepts from a source, you may also illuminate those ideas for these readers, helping them to see those texts from new perspectives they might not have previously recognized. Used in these ways, summaries build trust and goodwill with your reader.

It is important to remember that summary is a flexible tool to help readers see what you have seen in a source. You can use summary to zoom in on certain key ideas in a text that are relevant to your discussion, while omitting less relevant portions. Your audience does not often need extensive detail to understand your source use, so summaries provide the necessary context for that information and allow you to simplify the source’s argument and distill the most important parts for your essay so your reader can follow along. For this reason, summary is an ideal way to introduce an audience to your source before you quote (or paraphrase). Introducing your source with a summary helps readers prepare for the change in voice and understand the source text and its purpose.

Summary does have its weakness, however. On their own, summaries do not advance arguments; they are informative but not persuasive. Therefore, summary should be combined with paraphrase or quotation to develop your essays, or you should immediately build on an idea introduced through a summary. Used as an introduction to a source, summary can establish who your source is and why its ideas are useful or important. Then, a paraphrase or quote can introduce specific ideas from that source to further your discussion.

Once again, I invite you to review this essay and look for the bolded summaries. Usually, you’ll find summaries living side-by-side with a quote or paraphrase. How would you describe the relationship between the summary and the quotes or paraphrase around it? You’ll also find moments where summaries introduce a key concept from a source that I then use to make a new point. What information do those summaries provide? As I described, I tried to use summaries regularly to give you, the reader, access to the bigger ideas or goals of the source before narrowing in on a more specific idea. Did you find that helpful?

## Paraphrase

Finally we come to paraphrase. A paraphrase restates an idea in language different from the original source. While summaries condense larger portions of a text like a section or whole book, paraphrases deal with smaller, more specific units of text, often one or a few sentences. Like summary, however, paraphrases demonstrate your ability to digest a specific idea and present it in a new way to your reader.

Paraphrase’s greatest strength is in its ability to recontextualize a source’s ideas or arguments. While a quote retains the original language, paraphrase allows you to take a text’s idea and reshape it to your tone, style, and emphasis. That doesn’t mean you change what the text is saying, but that you adapt its information to make it fit naturally with your voice and the discussion you’re having. Whereas quotes replace your voice, paraphrase allows you to deploy sources within your own voice. This ability to restructure information also enables you to simplify complicated ideas to make them easier to understand.

Let’s look at an example to see paraphrase in action. Earlier, I quoted from Bendeguz Papp’s research on disaster misconceptions. *Here’s another quote that we’ll attempt to paraphrase: “Despite the fact that Japanese* *public hazard education is very thorough and in-depth, the potential failures of* *the nuclear power plant had not been considered” (255)*. In this quote and in his essay, Papp focuses on one specific event, the Fukushima nuclear power plant failure. However, if my essay were more interested in the hazard education Japan provides, I might paraphrase this sentence by saying: Papp acknowledges that Japan provides thorough hazard education but notes that it did not account for potential nuclear power plant failures in 2011 (255). This paraphrase might be helpful in an essay comparing emergency hazard responses in different countries or in an informative essay about the history of disasters in Japan. Paraphrase allows me to adapt Papp’s information in a way that complements the argument I am developing, while also appropriately representing Papp’s ideas.

That balance between recontextualization and representation can make paraphrase tricky to write effectively. You may have been told that paraphrase is about using “your own words,” but that’s not so easy. Before her work on the Citation Project, Rebecca Moore Howard discussed the difficulty of paraphrase in her essay, “A Plagiarism Pentimento.” She described how many instances of student “plagiarism” aren’t plagiarism at all! Students don’t intend to cheat; they just write bad paraphrases that look like plagiarism because the language is too similar to the original. Howard calls this “patchwriting”---stitching together pieces of a source’s original language like patchwork. If you are relying too heavily on the original language or if you are just swapping out words for synonyms from the thesaurus, your paraphrase will likely result in patchwriting. To avoid patchwriting, challenge yourself to use not only different words but also different sentence structures when writing a paraphrase. Returning to the above example from Papp, you can compare the sentence structure of the original and the paraphrase. Ignoring the signal phrase (“Papp acknowledges that”), you’ll notice that the subject (the person or thing doing the action) is different, for example. In the original, “failures of the power plant” is the subject and focus of the sentence. In the paraphrase, Japan is the subject. Further, the main action has changed from considering power plant failure to providing education. Combining different words with new sentence structures is not a perfect strategy but can be helpful in writing stronger paraphrases.

It is difficult to evaluate the success of paraphrases without seeing the original text, but it may still be helpful for you to look back on the underlined paraphrases in this essay, especially to observe how those paraphrases connect to summaries and quotes around them. You might also notice how often I am able to use sources without it feeling like the voice changed. Paraphrase allowed me to apply knowledge from other sources frequently while still maintaining my presence as a writer.

# Conclusion

In this essay, you’ll find 23 instances of source use: quotes, summaries, and paraphrases. There are 7 quotes, 8 summaries, 8 paraphrases, though there are a few instances of smaller quotes within summaries or paraphrases I didn’t count. I draw attention to the numbers to illustrate how this essay used sources beyond quotes. I used these sources for a variety of purposes: to introduce important ideas, to provide examples, to elaborate key concepts. In each instance, I asked what form of source integration would meet that goal and applied the appropriate strategy. Also take note of the time I took after I cited each source to continue discussing its ideas and explaining their importance to my overall message---a move often referred to as “unpacking” the source.

Even before I began writing, however, these sources helped to shape my thinking. The authors I cited are voices I have been listening to for many years, who helped me understand more about how writing and source use work. My contribution in this essay was to bring together many of their ideas to help you see their ongoing conversation and how you can join it and others like it by listening, learning, and writing.

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

This essay combines insights from two strands of research to inform its purpose: research on student misconceptions and research on student source use practices.[^tr-1] Misconceptions research suggests that much of the resistance or failure we encounter with student learning is the result of misconceptions, inaccurate beliefs or ideas that learners use to explain or interpret their experiences in and outside of education. Misconceptions interfere with student learning, such as when a student is disengaged with a writing class because they think that writing is a talent you are born with rather than a skill to develop. To engage students most effectively and improve their learning, we must identify and address misconceptions and transform them into accurate understandings. On the other hand, research on student source use practices, like Rebecca Moore Howard and Sandra Jamieson’s Citation Project, suggests that students don’t work deeply with their sources. They “sentence-mine” the first few results on Google or a library database (if they’re putting in some effort) until they reach the source use quota. Applying misconceptions insights to student source use, I suggest that students source use practices might result from misconceptions that students hold regarding sources.

Following the recommendations of misconceptions research, I use this essay to name some of those misconceptions, begin a conversation about them, and introduce alternative, accurate conceptions of source use. Misconception research recommends first identifying misconceptions that students might hold and then addressing them through discussions, activities, and other exercises. In this case, I specifically am interested in disrupting the belief that source use is an arbitrary, mechanical exercise in pleasing teachers and that quotation is the best form of source integration. By reframing source use as a rhetorical act of identification and ethos with and within their target academic and professional communities, I aim to motivate students toward a process of research whose purpose is deeper than meeting an assignment requirement. Then, by examining the rhetorical advantages and weaknesses of quotation, summary, and paraphrase, I hope to disrupt the default preference toward quotation and challenge students to think more critically about their source integration choices.

I highly recommend that, before or after assigning this reading, instructors engage students in an open discussion about their beliefs and idea about source use---why we use sources, when we use sources, how they were taught to use sources, what makes a good quote, when is a paraphrase better, etc. (see the discussion questions below). After reading, further activity might include examining source use in other academic and, especially, non-academic texts. This essay had limited space to offer and analyze examples of source use, so instructors might find it needful to do more hands-on analysis of source use in classes.

With that said, the essay does attempt to be its own example of source use in action. Quotation, summary, and paraphrase are visually highlighted in the text to help students recognize the different modes of integration when they appear and to see the connections between them. While the essay invites students to analyze the source use as they read, this might be an activity instructors conduct together with students in class. Additionally, instructors and students might similarly highlight other texts in class to see how other writers use sources and to see if similar patterns of source use emerge. This method might also be applied for peer review, to give students a way to comment upon one another’s source use.

As the conclusion suggests, it may also be especially helpful to observe the unpacking that happens following many instances of source use. Students are often tempted to move quickly past their sources, dedicating one or two sentences to their quotations or paraphrases. I hope my example might illustrate the need to dwell in those ideas and unpack them. I made sure to introduce the term “unpacking” so that students and instructors might share that vocabulary for both discussion and eventual feedback on classroom assignments.

Finally, this essay has much affinity with many other *Writing Spaces* chapters. I believe this essay would be most effective in combination when assigned alongside those chapters, including Kyle Stedman’s “Annoying Ways People Use Sources,” Cynthia R. Haller’s “Walk, Talk, Cook, Eat,” Kathleen J. Ryan’s “Constructing Scholarly Ethos,” Karen Rosenberg’s “Reading Games,” and Dan Melzer’s “Understanding Discourse Communities,” as I extend their discussions of why and how writers position themselves with their audiences, read academic writing, and integrate that information into their writing. Placing our essays in dialogue will produce a rich starting point for discussion about misconceptions of source use and writing more generally, as well as build a deep vocabulary for classes to discuss writing issues.

# Discussion Questions

1. This essay discusses how misconceptions about writing---like why we use sources---can keep you from writing effectively. We learn many things about writing through our life that aren’t always true, resulting in misconceptions. In writing especially, misconceptions often form when we think that a contextual rule is a constant rule (like rules that say you can never use “I” in your writing). What other ideas or beliefs do you have about writing that you think might not be accurate?

2. The need to listen and learn the conversation would imply that you need to read sources before you start writing. What has been your usual process for finding, reading, and using sources? When in your writing process does that happen---before you start writing, after you’ve decided on your topic? How do you think that has impacted your source use in the past?

3. This essay invited you to review the author’s source use and asked if he used sources successfully to engage you and enhance your understanding. Where did his source use succeed? Where was it not as effective? How might he have integrated his sources differently to be more effective?

4. Find a writing assignment that used sources that you wrote previously for this or another class. Examine your own source use. How many times did you use quotes? How many summaries or paraphrases are in your essay? Can you remember why you integrated those sources in those ways? After reading this essay, are there instances of source use in your essay that might change?

5. Different discourse communities have different ways they communicate and different ways they cite each other’s words. You belong to many discourse communities, including the academic field of your major, student organizations, a religious group, or hobby groups. How did you learn to be part of those communities---to know what they care about, how they talk to each other about those things, and how to communicate with other members? Consider who gets “cited” when members try to solve problems in the group? Do they quote or paraphrase other members? What books or texts do they respect? How do members introduce ideas from outside of the group so that others will listen?
