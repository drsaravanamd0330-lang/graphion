# Introduction: One Quiet Student’s Perspective

Whenever I encountered participation grades in course syllabi as a college student, I immediately felt defeated. I am an observant, quiet person, someone who struggles to engage in conversations with people who aren’t my close friends. In college, that meant participating in discussions in most of my classes seemed beyond my capabilities. I was (mostly) prepared each day, having done the required reading.

I would listen and take notes in class. Yet, I rarely offered my own ideas and interpretations. When professors called on me, I found it impossible to think of anything to say. So, if participation was 10% of my final grade in a class, I would simply hope and accept I could earn a B+, or maybe an A- with a generous instructor, by relying on my writing skills alone. What my professors didn’t know was that after class, when I’d had time to think about and process what we’d covered in class, I could easily talk about my ideas with classmates. What kept me from speaking was the fast-paced nature of class, the limited time frame for processing complex concepts, and the pressure to speak in profound ways in front of an authority figure.

At this point, you may be wondering what participation grades and experiences of quiet students have to do with writing. Well, first-year writing (FYW) courses value collaborative activities like group writing activities, peer workshops groups, and full class discussion. In other words, FYW expects you to participate, and you may be graded on that participation in one way or another. What we instructors aren’t always good at explaining is how and why these talk-based activities are important for your writing process. In this chapter, I offer you more context for these participatory expectations, but I also challenge you to find ways to recognize and support silence and listening as legitimate forms of engagement in the FYW classroom.

I started college in 2007, but it wasn’t until I began studying silence for my doctoral dissertation in 2018 that I was able to make some sense of my experiences as a quiet student. My professors never explained how or why they expected oral participation, and I only had one professor who asked me why I was quiet and how he could include me in class. Instead, I was often cold-called, made to feel inadequate for not speaking, and graded as less-qualified than my peers simply because, in my mind, I was better at listening than speaking. As I progressed through graduate-level education, I felt as if I did not belong in academia. Of course, I’m now a college professor. I persevered, but not without much frustration and significant strain on my self-esteem. As I began to study silence, I came to realize that no one, including myself, had considered what my silences might be *doing*. I strive now to listen to silences---my own and my students’---as part of the conversation. That is, I try to listen to all silences as *rhetorical*.

I began this chapter with my own experiences as a quiet student because I know I’m not unique. I’m guessing that at least a few of you identify with my experiences; or you may be a talkative student who is confused by your quiet peers’ seeming lack of participation. Others of you may have different and equally valid reasons for choosing silence. Quiet or not-soquiet, you may feel uncomfortable in silence. As you move through this chapter, I urge you to think more critically and consciously about your relationship to silence, particularly in classroom spaces. Take some time now to think through these questions: How do you feel during silence? What do you observe about others’ responses to silence? When and why might you choose to use silence in your FYW class? Hold onto your answers; we’ll revisit these questions later. First, let us explore the unspoken rules around speaking and silence and how they come to shape the FYW classroom.

# Western Cultural Expectations about Silence and Speaking

A rhetorician named Cheryl Glenn wrote a whole book about silence, part of which explores the ways Western communities tend to privilege speaking over silence. Glenn notes that Westerners “learn early on to fill up social space and compress silence, to use words for…small talk, and idle conversation” (6). Think about a moment in one of your classes when the professor asked a question, and no one responded. What did your professor do? Did they rephrase the question? Did they call on someone? Did they sit and wait? How did you feel during the silence? What did you do? What did your classmates do? Did someone---maybe even you---talk just to break the silence?

We try to fill the space silence leaves because of a “cultural belief in the importance of a streamed conversation” (Glenn 6). Those of us who grew up in the US strive for fluid discussions, and as a result, quiet people are often perceived as “inappropriate” when they interrupt that conversation with silence (Glenn 6). In the classroom, an uncomfortable student might choose to say something, even if it’s off-topic or the idea is ill-formed. Or, out of discomfort, your professor (yes, they are probably uncomfortable too) might respond with a sigh or a question like, “Did anyone even do the homework?” These responses to silence expose how Western cultural expectations around speaking and silence play out in the classroom.

Those of you who did not grow up in a US-based learning environment may feel confused, curious, or even frustrated by these expectations. Richie Hao, a communications scholar, describes the jarring experience of moving from the Philippines, where silence was expected and valued by his teachers, to the US, where the urge to “speak up” made him feel “like a failure” (267). While working on my doctoral dissertation, I had the opportunity to interview graduate students about their past experiences with silence in classrooms. One student, Sarish, found silence in the US classroom to be *less* fraught than his American peers because of his past experiences with education in Nepal, where students were expected to be silent. Experiences of silence can be widely varied, and the Western ideal of a streamed conversation risks leaving out many other ways of being, knowing, and learning. While you may have little control over how your professors interpret your own and your peers’ silences, I will later suggest ways you, as a student, can still play a role in creating a more inclusive learning environment for diverse experiences and uses of silence and listening.

# Pedagogical Beliefs about Writing and Participation

Cultural expectations can shape all discussion-oriented classrooms to value verbal participation, but why is participation part of FYW, where *writing* is the content and primary task of the course? To answer this question, we need to look more closely at the collaborative activities that make up FYW and why FYW relies on them. In FYW, you’re likely to encounter activities such as full-class or small group discussion about sample essays; peer review sessions; group writing activities; oral brainstorming sessions; and conferences with your instructor. All these activities require some level of oral communication.

Your FYW instructor likely believes in a long-held theory that writing is a *social process*. While writing seems like a solitary activity, composition scholars argue that much of the work of writing happens *in conversation*. Kenneth Bruffee, a scholar who traces the history and value of collaborative learning, argues that writing is deeply linked with the ability to converse. Essentially, he believed that in order to improve your writing, you need to talk more. (To learn more, check out the *Writing Spaces* chapter, “Navigating Your Collaborative Project” which applies Bruffee’s argument to group writing assignments.)

While these theories have been developed and complicated since Bruffee was writing in the 1980s, the social activities that support writing as a collaborative process persist because they *are* often invaluable to your writing process. For example, in another *Writing Spaces* chapter called “How Writing Happens,” Zach DePiero and Ryan Dippre model a messy writing process that includes social activities like giving and receiving feedback. Their argument is that collaborative tasks play a critical role in developing your writing for an audience that isn’t your teacher. In yet another *Writing* *Spaces* chapter, “We Write Because We Care,” the authors ask you to see yourself as a writer with your own rhetorical purpose. Without collaborating and talking with others, it can be more difficult to make that cognitive leap from being a student writing for a grade to being a writer who has a purpose and an audience beyond the classroom.

Such beliefs about the sociality of writing directly impact instructors’ decisions to include participation as part of their grading policies. Genevieve Critel, who studied FYW instructors’ participation policies, explains that “[g]iving students grades for participation…gives teachers another way to signal they value the social elements of writing processes and practices.” Participation grades often intend to assign academic weight to the work you do in the FYW classroom, but as my own experiences in the introduction to this chapter show, that’s not always what it might feel like to you---even if you are someone who finds it easier to speak in class.

# Rhetorical Silence Outside the Classroom

Let’s take a break from the classroom and think about silence itself. In Glenn’s book about silence, mentioned in a previous section, she makes a case for silence as a powerful *rhetoric*, saying, “Silence can be something one does, something that is done to someone, or something one experiences” (9). What she means is that silence can have purpose, and that it can have an effect on an audience, just like speech and writing can.

I urge you to take time to watch X Gonzalez’s speech at the 2018 March for Our Lives, which can easily be found on YouTube. A surviving student from the 2018 mass shooting at Marjory Stoneman Douglas High School in Florida, Gonzalez speaks of the shooting and its victims, and then, without warning, holds silence for four and a half minutes. Gonzalez’s silence breaks with the convention of what we expect of a public speech. Because the silence is unexpected, the live audience clearly is uncertain; you can hear them periodically erupt into chants. Watching through a screen, it’s hard to know what to do during the silence, but I, at least, find it impossible to look away. At the end of their silence, Gonzalez explains, “Since the time I came out here, it has been six minutes and twenty seconds. The shooter has ceased shooting and will soon abandon his rifle, blend in with the students as they escape, and walk free for an hour before arrest. Fight for your lives, before it’s someone else’s job.” Gonzalez’s deliberate silence and subsequent call to action serve as a powerful example of silence working rhetorically on an audience, helping Gonzalez make a case for stronger gun control.

I often tell students that it seems like silence can work as a rhetorical strategy *because* it often makes people so uncomfortable, particularly in our Western cultural context. The tricky thing about using silence is that because it’s uncomfortable, it can also easily be misinterpreted. Speech and writing can run into this problem too, though. We’ve all been in situations where someone heard something different from what we said or interpreted a text message differently from what we thought we wrote. To use silence rhetorically, we have to start thinking more consciously about the purpose of silence and how our audience hears it. If we want to leverage the power of silence, we might consider the other possible ways we can support silence by using body language or facial expressions more deliberately. Gonzalez does this by staring straight ahead during the silence, even when chants erupt in the audience, and by letting their face show anger and grief. Gonzalez’s silence also becomes even more powerful because they explicitly name its purpose after the fact, in their call to action. If they had simply walked off stage at the end of the silence, the silence might still have been powerful, but it would also have been more ambiguous. Sometimes, we have to name silence to ensure the audience really listens to it.

Do the silences you choose to use in the classroom also hold rhetorical power? In the next section, we will explore the possibilities of answering yes to that question.

# Students Choosing Silence: Rhetorical Silence Inside the Classroom

Instructors can often presume silence equates to a lack of preparedness or to a certain student’s shyness that needs to be overcome. You, however, likely see your decision to remain silent or to speak as much more complex. The important thing here is silence *can* be a *choice,* and a rhetorical one at that. Your silences may be more subtle than Gonzalez’s, but if you begin to listen to all silences as holding rhetorical potential, you may be able to expand your definitions of silence, and in turn, make the classroom a more inclusive and welcoming place for other ways of being and knowing.

Mary Reda, a composition scholar, talked to quiet students about why they choose silence in class. In addition to many other reasons, she found that some students feel they must actively “*not care* what others in the class think of them” in order to speak in class (Reda 133, emphasis hers). In my own study of silence, one participant named Sonia explained that as someone whose first language was Spanish, she didn’t speak in undergrad because she was “embarrassed” of her accent. Can you think of a time you chose silence to protect yourself from academic, social, or personal risk in class? Silences like these are often heard as a lack of confidence. But what if we choose to listen to them as rhetorically guarding your sense of self-worth?

Have you ever chosen silence as a way to resist something you disagree with in the classroom? Or, have you ever chosen silence to protect something bigger than yourself, like your cultural identity? Timothy San Pedro studied the way Native American high school students use silence as a response to racial microaggressions in the classroom. One student named Eileen chose silence as a “shield” after her Indigenous knowledges were repeatedly dismissed (148). Eileen employs silence rhetorically to shield her Indigenous identity and to work against the ways her identity has been attacked.

Perhaps being quiet is just part of who you are. A student named Lucy in Reda’s study uses silence as an expression of her identity. Lucy tells Reda, “I’ve never felt like you have to hear my voice in order to know who I am. I feel like I almost want people to know me more as a listener than as someone who wants to control the conversation all the time” (156). Are you someone who uses their silence to listen deeply to what others have to say? Do you know someone like this and have appreciated how talking to that person makes you feel important and heard? Helping others feel heard through silent listening can also be rhetorically powerful.

There are many other ways you might choose silence. Take a moment to think about your own silences and the circumstances that led to them. What would you say about your silences if you had the opportunity to explain them? If that’s difficult to do, then think about others’ silences and what might have led to them. How could you deliberately listen to others’ silences as rhetorical?

# Making Space for Silence in FYW: Some Suggestions

# Leveraging Rhetorical Silence as a Quiet Student

Once you recognize silence as holding rhetorical potential, you also have to think about the whole situation around the silence. What is going on? What is your purpose? How does your audience hear your silence? How do you *want* them to hear it? As a quiet student, I noticed professors often viewed my silences as static representation of a shy and unconfident person. However, through conscious reflection about my own silences, I learned they signaled different things depending on the situation. Sometimes, I was nervous or lacked confidence, but more often, my silences meant I was thinking deeply about something while the conversation had moved on to something else. Other times, I simply didn’t trust my professor enough to talk in front of them. Sometimes I was using silence to show my disapproval of a conflict between classmates. Saying I was shy simply didn’t cover the multiple reasons my silences emerged, and that bothered me. I realized I needed to work more consciously to ensure my audience---including myself---understood what my silences were doing.

To assert authority over my silences, I began to consciously name my silences. At first, I only did this for myself, and that, in itself, was empowering. Even if professors still heard my silences as a lack of confidence, it helped me to know that my silences really signified something else. Then, I began to name my silences for others. In one-on-one conferences with professors, I would tell them I needed to take their questions home with me to think about them instead of responding in the moment. If I didn’t trust my professor, I sought ways to get to know them by visiting their office hours. Finally, during class discussions, I would try to make my silences more meaningful through facial expressions. I found that when I was more deliberate in my facial expressions, I was often more willing to voice my opinions when the professor called on me. When I heard my silences through others’ perceptions of me as shy, I felt passive in classrooms, like I was just taking up space. When I took ownership of my silences and became more expressive in other ways, I felt more active and realized I often had more words to go with those facial expressions than I originally thought. To be clear, I’m not saying the goal should always be to break the silence, but it did happen to sometimes be a byproduct of the strategies I enacted to think about and use my silences differently.

If you’re a quiet student, take some time to think about your silences and how you can take back some control over them. Below are a few possible strategies, but I urge you to think of more that fit your own needs and the situations you find yourself in:

- Write about your silences, how you think they’ve been interpreted by others, and what you think they actually mean. You don’t have to share this with anyone, but naming them for yourself can be empowering.
- Email your professors to explain what kind of participant you are and how they can help you share your ideas in class. Maybe you want them to call on you, or you might need a space to contribute to the conversation in writing instead. Maybe you need time at the end of a class to look back at your notes so you can play the important role of synthesizing the class discussion.
- During group work or peer workshops, be explicit about your needs as a group member. Do you need your peers to explicitly invite you into the conversation? Do you need to start a group chat? (Cecil-Lemkin and Gluck have some great suggestions in their *Writing Spaces* chapter on collaborative writing projects.)
- Practice paying attention to your own body language and facial expressions. Could you support your silences through gestures or facial expressions? Your usual impulse may be to avoid eye contact in class. So, if you’re worried that deliberate silent gestures will be invitations to be called on, try to make your purpose clear to your professors. Some may not be receptive, but many will see that you are taking an active role in your own learning.
- If you have things to say but can’t always find the right moment to say it in a fast-moving conversation, try to find a more talkative classmate who can work as your teammate and help interrupt the conversation.

# Listening to and Including Silence

In class, whether you’re quiet or not, try paying attention to silence and making more deliberate space for it.

- Take time to reflect on how silence makes you feel and why. It’s okay if it makes you uncomfortable, but try to figure out why you feel that way in specific situations.
- When you feel the urge to say something in class, pause first. Give others a moment to think before jumping in.
- Look around the classroom more. How do you read your peers’ body language and facial expressions? Can you voice some of your observations aloud without singling your peers out?
- When you are working in groups, ask your peers how they want to participate. Be open to methods like group chats or leaving pauses in the conversation to ensure everyone has space to think and process. Don’t assume that because someone isn’t talking they aren’t willing or capable of pulling their weight.

# Using Silence as a Contemplative Practice in Writing

We’ve discussed how we can use and listen to silence rhetorically in the classroom, but let’s also circle back to those activities that make up FYW. If silence can be rhetorical, then what can silence do for that social process work we do in FYW?

As an instructor, one way I include silence in my classes is through a contemplative reading exercise called *lectio divina* (Howes and Smith). This activity looks like reading small portions of a complex (and usually emotionally charged) essay out loud, and then sitting for long moments of silence, meditating on what we’ve listened to. The point is to listen more deeply to the author’s point of view before racing to respond. Some of my students find the activity’s process of valuing silence over speaking difficult. Yet, many find they have thoughtful things to say about the reading because the silence offers time to slow down and focus intently on small pieces of text.

A few years ago, one of my students took this contemplative practice and applied it to their own revision process. The student started reading their own work aloud then sitting in silence with their draft before changing or adding anything. In other words, our class’s *collaborative* silence influenced this student’s *individual* writing process.

Can you think of other ways silence might benefit your writing process? Could you also make space for silence in other common FYW activities like peer review or group writing assignments?

# A Challenge to Talk About Silence

In my own study of classroom silence, I wasn’t surprised that most of the graduate students I interviewed, even the ones who identified as quiet students, felt uncomfortable in silence. However, in my conversations with them, I noticed that in talking about silence, often their views of silence would shift, change, and expand. It’s not that silence is inherently uncomfortable, it’s that our perceptions of silence are shaped by Western cultural expectations, and when those expectations are disrupted, we often feel uncomfortable. If we talk about silence and its rhetorical potential more, and if we more deliberately listen to silence, then we can slowly start to see silence as having a place in conversations. In FYW, that means we can be more inclusive of diverse ways of being and communicating.

So, I leave you with a challenge to return to some of the questions I’ve posed to you throughout the chapter. Take time to openly talk, write, and simply sit with your responses to these questions, but not with the aim of eliminating or erasing silence. Instead, acknowledge your own and others’ specific experiences with silence and try to build a more inclusive space for you and your peers.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

While writing this chapter, I envisioned students reading it at the beginning of a semester when they are learning the expectations of FYW and working to define themselves as a community.[^tr-1] I also hope the questions I pose throughout the essay can serve as touchpoints for you and your students to return to when silences inevitably emerge at different points in the semester.

In my own classes, my students and I co-create a set of community engagement standards for the syllabus. This statement is not part of students’ grades; I have given up the practice of grading participation. The community engagement standards my students write are a set of guidelines for holding everyone accountable for how we engage with one another. This chapter and its accompanying discussion questions could help students consider their (and your) assumptions about silence before writing this type of syllabus policy.

When I’ve asked students to develop a syllabus statement *without* talking about silence first, they tend to fall back on standard participation policies that reward speaking students and punish quiet ones. In sum, students repeat what they know. By talking about silence more directly as a choice, students’ policies become much more nuanced and generous to their quieter peers. For example, in this semester’s syllabus statement, my FYW students included aims like the following:

- Some peers may be quieter than others, but we will make every effort to include them in the conversation or offer other ways for them to contribute (i.e., taking notes for the group or working in a shared document).

- We will not rush others to respond; some people take longer to think or come up with responses.

It might also be useful to assign this essay with Jason McIntosh’s *Writing Spaces* essay “Is This for a Grade?”: Understanding Assessment, Evaluation, and Low-Stakes Writing Assignments,” found in vol. 5. Together, mine and McIntosh’s chapters help situate and explain FYW activities while opening a conversation about grades and assessment.

My chapter could also be assigned as part of a unit that questions and critiques norms that govern writing and speaking in academic spaces. It could be assigned alongside other *Writing Spaces* essays that directly take on difference in FYW. Such essays include Kristin DeMint Bailey, An Ha, & Anthony J. Outlar’s essay “What Color Is My Voice? Academic Writing and the Myth of Standard English” and Mara Lee Grayson’s “Writing Toward Racial Literacy.”

Finally, I urge you to consider using silence as part of your teaching practice to try and normalize productive silence in classroom instruction. In the body of the chapter, I’ve offered *lectio divina* as one example, which I adapted from Howes and Smith. I also recommend Timothy Oleskiak’s “Slow Peer Review in the Writing Classroom” as another way to incorporate silence and listening into classroom practice. Importantly, however, I don’t believe these sorts of contemplative activities should be the only ways silences are incorporated. While such practices help create an environment in which silence, listening, and slow thinking can be valued, they are also largely instructor-directed and highly controlled. I challenge you to listen to and value the silences students produce when you are looking for oral conversation. Try to hear what those silences have to say as well.

# Discussion Questions

1. Think about the last time you encountered silence in a classroom. How did you respond? How did the professor respond? How did you feel in these moments? How do you think your instructor felt? a. What do you think you, your classmates, and your instructor should do when silence emerges in full-class discussions? In peer review sessions? In other group activities?

2. Do you consider yourself a quiet student? Are you a quiet student in all educational spaces? What about when you are not in school? Why do you choose silence or speaking in these different contexts? a. What effects do you hope your silences have on those around you? What would you say about your silences if you were given the opportunity to explain them?

3. Identify an example of silence being used rhetorically in a TV show you like to watch, a political debate or speech, a recorded interview, or some other type of media. It could be a long silence, or lots of small silences. Based on the speaker’s body language, facial expressions, and other verbal and nonverbal cues, what do you think the silence is doing? How does that silence make you feel as a listener?

# Writing Experiments

1. For your next writing assignment, spend some of your time writing in a different place from where you normally write. For example, if you often work alone in your dorm room, try writing in the library or in a coffee shop. If you normally write surrounded by friends, try sitting outside by yourself or in a quiet study room in the library. Pay attention to the differences in sound and how they affect your writing process and report back to your classmates. a. How were the noise levels different in your new environment as opposed to your regular writing environment? How do you think these differences influenced your ability to write? b. How did social interactions (or the lack thereof) influence your writing (both its content and your process)?

2. When you are asked to revise one of your writing projects, try to put yourself into your intended audience’s shoes. Imagine you are part of your intended audience, then complete the following activity as part of your revision process. a. Read each paragraph of your project aloud, or have a peer read each paragraph to you so that you aren’t tempted to change things while you read. Pause after each paragraph, sitting in silence for 1 minute, focusing intently on what you just heard. Imagine you *are* your intended audience. How would your audience feel having heard what was just read aloud? What questions would they have? b. After the minute of silence ends, take note of anything you might want to change about that paragraph. Then, continue the process c. Upon reading the whole essay, pausing in silence after each paragraph, take a minute to write about the major ideas you would take away from your project if you were the intended reader. d. Finally, return and make the changes you noted for yourself along the way.
