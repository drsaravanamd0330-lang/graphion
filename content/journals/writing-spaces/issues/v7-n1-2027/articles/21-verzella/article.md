# Introduction

If you’ve taken or are currently taking a composition course, you’ve probably heard your teachers describe writing as a process and a collaborative activity. Indeed, writing is a collaborative process where writ-

ers and readers work together. It’s all about mediation and negotiation: Writers shape their work based on readers’ needs and editors’ feedback. This dynamic process evolves through interactions and revisions. Composition scholar David Russell describes writing as something that “comes between” and connects people (26). Similarly, Paul Kei Matsuda explains that negotiating language is a key part of all writing (69). This emphasis on connection and negotiation aligns closely with the art of translation. Translators act as mediators, bridging gaps between languages, cultures, and contexts. They ensure that meaning and tone are communicated clearly while respecting each language’s cultural and rhetorical traditions. This connection between writing and translation is the focus of my argument. I believe that you should develop an interest in translation to become more sensitive writers. By reflecting on translation, you can sharpen your ability to adapt language, understand diverse perspectives, and communicate with clarity and sensitivity across cultural barriers.

Translation remains a niche topic in writing instruction, often only addressed in courses designed for students who speak English as a foreign language. However, nothing stops you from engaging with translation proactively and independently. In fact, reading about translation and reflecting on its practices can help you deepen your understanding of language and gain a richer perspective on communication. The next section of this chapter will outline a path to begin thinking critically about translation. Subsequent sections will showcase translation and editing activities that helped my students appreciate the complexity of translation and the rewards of collaborating on international projects.

# Unpacking Translation: Where Does Your Journey Begin?

Your journey into the world of translation could start with focusing on rhetorical concepts taught in composition courses. Greek concepts like *ethos*, *logos, pathos*, and *kairos*, or Roman concepts like *decorum, dignitas*, and *auctoritas*, are not native to English; rather, they are foreign concepts that have been gradually assimilated into contemporary education over time. This process has transformed these ideas, adding layers of new interpretations that have changed their meanings and values. As a result, many of the concepts taught in composition classes are, in a sense, translations, or adaptations of ideas that have circulated for centuries within Western culture. Translation often involves a process of *domestication*, defined as changing or adjusting parts of the original message so they make more sense to the people reading the translation. This process makes the foreign text or idea appear natural and familiar. Ideas are made to feel as though they are intrinsic to our own culture, when in fact they have been transformed in the process of translation. Why is this a problem? As Lawrence Venuti explains in *The Translator’s Invisibility*, one reason is that the source text is modified so much that it may no longer feel foreign, and the cultural “otherness” --- the distinct values and ways of thinking that make the source culture different from the target culture --- is erased, thus making the source culture (and the work of the translator) invisible.

Another way to reflect on translation is by asking your international friends to share words and expressions from their native languages that are difficult to translate into English. Examples from Italian, my native language, include the noun *simpatia* and the adjectives *simpatico* and *simpatica*. A *persona simpatica* is not just funny, but also nice, likeable, and sensitive. This person exudes positive energy through her ability to connect with others with kind and polite attention. Translating *delusione* is also difficult: Translation dictionaries will suggest “disappointment,” but *delusione* conveys a feeling of being let down, a sense of disillusionment, and a feeling of sadness; a quiet, reflective sadness that subtly colors one’s emotions, sometimes evoking a sense of nostalgia or a yearning for something unattainable.

I also remember the case of a Russian student trying to explain *avos*, a word that conveys a sense of blind faith or a hopeful, yet uncertain, expectation that things will turn out well without much planning or effort. My American students collaborated to translate *avos*, proposing terms like “happy-go-lucky” or “blind faith in divine providence.” They appreciated the process of negotiating different solutions to render *avos*, recognizing how translation helps challenge patronizing attitudes toward non-native speakers. They also realized how translation stimulates curiosity, promotes creativity, and fosters open-mindedness by highlighting the value of different ways of thinking and alternative ways of seeing.

In general, when we write, we need to accept that using language involves both gains and losses. But here’s the catch: The process of choosing words can also enrich our message and spark new ideas. Writing always stimulates thinking, and language doesn’t just simplify; it can also enrich, opening doors to new insights and perspectives, or helping us see connections between themes and ideas. Translation works in the same way. Translation cannot simply replicate the original message in its entirety. Instead, translators must interpret and adapt the source material, thus adding more layers of meaning to the source. Translating a text into a different language always means losing part of the original meaning and cultural references but also gaining new meaning through foreign words.

Unless they are working on technical documents, translators should not simplify or alter ideas to perfectly fit the norms of the target language or audience. This process of domestication would erase the distinctiveness of the source material, diluting its cultural significance, and misrepresenting its intent. This means that translators must walk a tightrope between remaining faithful to the original text and making necessary adjustments to ensure the new text works in the target language. This balance involves navigating between literal translation, sticking closely to the original words, and reinterpreting or adapting the message to better fit cultural and linguistic differences in the new language.

Preserving and celebrating linguacultural differences connects closely to the idea of linguistic justice. Ligia Mihut defines linguistic justice as a call to dismantle monolingual norms and promote a plurality of languages and writing styles that reflect students’ diverse cultural and linguistic backgrounds (288). This approach challenges dominant standards, exposes power imbalances between languages, and supports more equitable, inclusive classroom practices.

To help you understand these points, this chapter will explore three translation and editing projects that linked writers: college-level American students with translation students in Italian and Spanish universities. This type of international project is called virtual exchange or VE. As Robert O’Dowd explains, VE “involves the engagement of groups of learners in extended periods of online intercultural interaction and collaboration with partners from other cultural contexts [...]” (5).

Working across two languages in virtual exchange projects can be challenging. Take, for example, an exchange between English-speaking and Spanish-speaking students. The Spanish speakers are often studying both languages involved --- their native Spanish and English --- while English-speaking students are less likely to be proficient in a foreign language. This dynamic can create an imbalance: Bilingual students may take the lead in completing tasks, especially translation-related ones, gaining a form of linguistic privilege. On the other hand, most virtual exchange projects are conducted in English, a globally dominant language. This gives native English speakers a different kind of linguistic privilege. To support their non-native-speaking peers, native speakers should resist the urge to police correctness. Instead, they can embrace English as a shared, flexible tool for communication and appreciate the creative, strategic ways others use it. Meanwhile, students fluent in both project languages can act as mediators, helping bridge cultural and linguistic gaps and enriching the exchange for everyone involved. If you’re curious to learn more about how language shapes power and opportunity, check out Ingrid Piller’s *Linguistic Diversity* *and Social Justice*, a great introduction to the concept of linguistic privilege.

When problems of linguistic justice and linguistic privilege are addressed, VE projects foster intercultural understanding, improve communication skills, and introduce the challenges of translation. Anyone can participate meaningfully in VE projects. You do not need to speak both languages involved to make a valuable contribution, and after all, the idea of being truly “monolingual” is misleading. In proposing a translingual approach, Horner, Trimbur, Lu, and Royster remind us that no one is truly monolingual: We all draw on a range of linguistic resources (310). We use different dialects of English and shift between formal and informal styles depending on the situation. Our language choices constantly adapt to suit our audience, purpose, and context.

# Translating and Editing Tourist Booklets

The first VE project based on a collaboration between writers and translators connected college-level translators in Italy and students enrolled in two first-year composition courses offered at an American university. The students in Italy, all native Italian speakers with an intermediate level of English proficiency, were divided into two groups and assigned the task of creating tourist booklets to promote the Molise region in central Italy. The target audience for the booklet was global users of English. A small sample of this target audience was represented by the English composition students involved in the project.

As Gilmore and Pine observe, in our “experience economy,” tourists increasingly seek authenticity. They desire genuine, immersive encounters that connect them to the local culture and people rather than packaged or commercialized experiences. This means that the authors of the booklets had to balance authenticity with accessibility when translating the booklet, originally conceived in Italian, into English. Their goal was to present Molise as a unique, undiscovered destination, creating a sense of *estrangement*, which meant keeping certain words, phrases, or cultural details from the original language that might feel unfamiliar to the new audience. At the same time, they sought to align with audience expectations of southern Italian culture, focusing on elements like hospitality and good food, which reflected *domestication*. Estrangement or domestication? This is the translator’s dilemma.

As the Italian students translated ideas and information into English, they had to navigate between over-explaining (domestication), which diminishes the magic, and preserving authenticity (estrangement), which challenges readers to act as translators themselves. When necessary, the authors/translators had to retain Italian words or use literal translations while ensuring the text offered clear, actionable information for prospective users. Culture-specific items --- names of dishes or festivals, for example --- posed particular challenges: Deciding whether to keep the original terms or translate them using a range of different strategies was especially complex.

The collaboration unfolded as follows: Italian students worked on translating cultural elements into persuasive verbal and visual language. American students provided feedback to make the booklets both accessible to English-speaking tourists and true to local values and traditions. They also shared comments on visual aids used by the authors. Like verbal language, visual representations are context-dependent, with their meaning shaped by cultural discourses, values, and practices that vary across social and cultural contexts.

The feedback offered by American students touched on different aspects of the booklets. At the word level, the reviewers asked to explain words like “Samnite town” and “transhumance.” These requests for clarification were justified since very few tourists will know about the Samnites, ancient Italic people, or *transhumance*, the seasonal movement of livestock between lowland pastures in winter and highland grazing areas in summer. Equally useful were comments on longer expressions. For instance, the phrase “uncontaminated nature” in “Molise features some of the most uncontaminated nature in all Europe” was flagged as problematic, as it could imply that the land was previously polluted. Although the authors intended to convey “pristine landscape,” American students questioned whether central Italy had areas of contamination and what type it might be. A similar misunderstanding occurred with the expression “digestible pizza,” commonly used in Italy to describe light, crispy dough cooked perfectly. The American reviewers wondered if pizza in Italy is not always digestible and asked why this distinction was necessary.

A statement that puzzled the reviewers was that Molise “is considered a bit of a joke.” The translators chose these words to convey that the region is off the beaten path, remote from tourist and business routes, and somewhat hidden even for Italians, with no major cities. The reviewers noted that the humorous remark did not translate well and carried negative connotations in English. Other suggestions emphasized highlighting the region’s traditional lifestyle and typical dishes. Additionally, the reviewers recommended a more subtle tone in the booklets, objecting to the use of “imperative language” that told them what to do, as they preferred suggestions and possibilities rather than direct commands.

As for the photos included, the suggestion was to make them less “generic” by including people in the photos. As the American students explained during class discussions, the suggestion to include more images with people highlighted a preference for human agency in visual communication. By featuring human figures, the photos would not only make the scenes more relatable but also convey a sense of life and engagement, turning abstract ideas into tangible experiences.

The inclusion of two specific photos sparked engaging discussions among students on both sides of the Atlantic. The first photo featured a mozzarella ball on a white plate, garnished with nothing but basil leaves. The reviewers observed that the photo was not rhetorically effective, as Americans prefer seeing how an ingredient like mozzarella is used in a complete dish. While Italian audiences may appreciate a simple food item like mozzarella as an independent dish, American readers find it unappealing when it is presented alone. The Italian students decided to keep the original photo to provoke a sense of estrangement rather than resorting to domestication.

The second photo depicted the interior of a local restaurant with a Confederate flag prominently displayed at the center, likely used by the owners to evoke the essence of the American South. The reviewers immediately questioned why their Italian peers had included such a controversial symbol, which is strongly associated with racism and white supremacy. The Italian students were unaware of the flag’s significance, but once they understood the gravity of their mistake, they removed the photograph from their booklet.

Meanings are shaped not only by the producers of images but also by the context in which they are consumed, with individuals from different cultural backgrounds interpreting them in ways the authors may not have intended. This incident was significant for both groups who appreciated the importance of cultural sensitivity and the need to understand the meanings of symbols across different cultures. The exchange highlighted the importance of viewing writing as a collaborative process, where involving audiences early on for feedback is essential.

# Translating and Editing Restaurant Menus

Another VE project connected students in a translation program at a Spanish university with students enrolled in a first-year composition course at a US college. The project was structured as follows: Spanish and American students were paired to form 20 teams. The Spanish students were tasked with creating menus for restaurants in the Castile-León region of Spain. Their goal was to translate traditional Spanish dishes into English, producing menus suitable for international tourists.

Food terms are culture-specific items (CSIs) that are deeply rooted in the traditions and practices of the source society, making them challenging to translate. Dish names often carry meanings related to values, traditions, cooking techniques, or references to locations, historical events, and figures, all of which may be lost in translation. Translating CSIs involves finding ways to convey the intended meaning while bridging cultural gaps, ensuring the message remains authentic but clear, exotic but comprehensible. Finally, dish names, a special type of CSIs, are listed in the writing genre of restaurant menus, which serves informational, persuasive, and promotional purposes simultaneously. Due to space limitations, menus must prioritize clear and concise language, avoiding lengthy explanations. In the European tradition, especially in non-touristy restaurants specializing in local cuisine, menus typically exclude pictures. Without photos to help tourists understand the dishes, accurate translations become even more essential.

Once the Spanish students completed the first draft of the menus, which included photos for reference, the American students took on the role of reviewers, offering feedback on the clarity and rhetorical effectiveness of the translations. The students also commented on document design. To learn more about the importance of document design for menus, you can read another essay in the *Writing Spaces* series, called “Worth a Thousand Words: Understanding Visual Rhetoric” by Jenae Cohn.

From the start, this task proved challenging, as the reviewers grappled with providing meaningful feedback on unfamiliar dishes, often relying on online searches to find images, ingredients, and preparation methods. Reflecting on this problem, Taylor, one of the American reviewers, observed that this situation also provided a unique advantage:

> While my project partner knew the dishes better than I did, she also occasionally assumed that everyone else knew what the dishes were as well and described them sparingly. When I needed further clarification, it meant that the average American also would have needed further clarification; so she was able to correct herself and provide a richer explanation that every American could understand.

Many of the English translations created by the Spanish students proved to be difficult to understand. The translations often came across as unappealing to American audiences. A clear example was the dish “cuajada,” which was translated as “milk curd.” Several reviewers pointed out that “curd” has negative connotations in the US, often associated with the term “curdle” or “congealed,” which can evoke images of spoiled milk, making the dish seem off-putting to potential customers. Another challenging dish to translate was “judiones de la granja,” rendered as “large white beans with meat stew.” While technically accurate, the specific types of beans and meat used in the dish remained unclear. It was unclear whether the beans were cooked in the stew or served on the side. Yet another example was a dish called “Pastel de Cabracho.” The Spanish translator rendered this dish with “Red Sea scorpion fish pie on toast.” The American reviewer observed that “pie” is commonly associated with desserts, and “scorpion” conveys the idea of poison. Due to these associations, she recommended choosing different words.

At the end of the project, all students wrote a detailed report to reflect on their learning experience. Ava, an American student, highlighted the challenges of translation:

> A confusing dish was “Ajo Blanco.” My Spanish project partner originally translated the dish as “White garlic cream” and explained to me that it was a creamy, garlic, bread soup. However, I had never been exposed to “bread soup” and knew that other English speakers would share my confusion if they encountered this dish on a menu. Eventually, we decided to change the name to “Bread and garlic cream” and included the bread part in the description to provide as much information as possible.

In her report, Taylor perfectly captures the challenges of a translation and editing project:

> There was some confusion about the exact nature of the assignment. While I was under the impression that the goal was to create an enticing and comprehensible menu, even if the translations weren’t exact, my project partner believed that her professor had told them to create an exact translation, no matter if the resulting dish descriptions sounded a bit awkward.

Kara appears to echo Taylor’s stance when she states: “There were dishes I thought needed more clarification and longer descriptions, but my Spanish partner was under the impression that we had to keep descriptions short and only a few words.” While sharing some of Kara’s and Taylor’s views, Ava provided a detailed account of her collaboration,

> Looking back on the project, I can confidently say that the translation process not only improved my knowledge of the English language but also taught me firsthand the many levels of understanding a foreign language. The nuances and complexities within each language make it difficult to find a direct translation for common ingredients and adjectives. This caused Ana and I to spend many nights picking apart certain words and trying to match them between English and Spanish until we found the most precise translation. One adjective in English could completely change the meaning of the translation and threaten the integrity of the dish. This often would annoy Ana as she had a personal connection to the cultural and traditional foods she was sharing with me. While she was always kind and courteous, her protectiveness over the Spanish dishes demonstrated to me the deep-rooted relationship between culture and language.

Ava highlights the intricate relationship between language and culture, showing how the translation process not only involves linguistic precision but also a deep respect for the cultural significance behind the words.

In a similar vein, Abby insightfully describes translation as a balancing act:

> I think that translation is all about balance. The original text has to be altered in order to make it comprehensible for its intended audience. Sometimes the person receiving the translation has to be willing to draw conclusions, make inferences, and even conduct further research in order to fully understand a given translation.

Emma adds that a reviewer should show consideration for foreign cultures to preserve the authenticity of alien traditions. She explains,

> I was very mindful when suggesting revisions on the translations my partner drafted. I did not want to lose the originality that came with the Spanish form of the dish. When my partner and I collaborated, I asked several questions to ensure I had the right idea of the dish before suggesting a change. I also was open to discussion and compromise when it came to the edits. I did not want to overstep and take away from the Spanish traditions that she was trying to preserve.

By balancing constructive feedback with respect for the original work, editors can help writers refine their ideas while maintaining the integrity of their message.

Other reports confirmed that students developed a deeper understanding of the delicate balance needed to preserve authenticity while ensuring clarity for a global audience. The project not only highlighted the importance of thoughtful translation but also emphasized the crucial role language plays in conveying cultural identity, tradition, and meaning. The students’ reflections reveal the vital role of negotiation and compromise in creating translations that are both accurate and culturally sensitive.

# Translating and Editing Technical Documents

The third VE project connected college-level American students enrolled in a technical writing course with Italian students majoring in English and translation. In this project, it was the American students who acted as writers, creating technical documents such as instruction manuals and user guides. Their Italian counterparts translated these documents from English into Italian. The pedagogical goal was to help students to gain insight into writing for international audiences and preparing technical documents for translation.

Without going into the details of this VE project, I’ll share an enlightening incident that you can discuss with your classmates. An American technical writer was surprised by the approach a translator used when working on the set of instructions entitled “How to grow a vegetable garden.” The translator refused to use the literal Italian equivalent of “kill” in the sentence, “Start with plants that are more difficult to kill.” Despite the writer’s reassurances that the use of the word “kill” in the original English text was unmarked and conventional within the context of gardening instructions, the translator felt that the word was inappropriate in this context. For the Italian translator, “kill” seemed out of place in a set of instructions focused on growing a vegetable garden, a task that, in their view, should be conveyed in a more gentle and culturally sensitive manner. The sentence “Start with plants that are more difficult to kill” was rendered as “Inizia con piante che muoiono meno facilmente” (back translation: “Start with plants that do not easily die”).

What this incident shows is that even a technical translation will call for rhetorical adjustments. A set of instructions is not a poem or a novel whose translation requires an effort to reinterpret and rebuild the original meaning in a new form. Yet, even in this case, a rhetorical shift became necessary. A rhetorical shift in translation refers to a deliberate change in the way ideas, tone, or style are presented when transferring a message from one language to another. A shift occurs when a translator adjusts elements of the original text to better align with the cultural, social, or rhetorical expectations of the target audience. What the students understood here was the importance of writing and translation as forms of mediation that involve a dialogue between sender and receiver, source text and target text.

Rhetorical shifts like the one illustrated above highlight not only differences between rhetorical traditions but also strategies for bridging those gaps. Through collaboration with translators, students of technical writing can learn how culture is made relevant in diverse ways and for various purposes. A cosmopolitan approach to composition focuses not on cultural differences, but on examining why we highlight those differences rather than commonalities, how we *do* culture or make culture relevant even in technical communication.

The key lesson was that even in technical communication, language is always in the way, never neutral. Translations can never be entirely literal or perfectly faithful. While technical translation leans more toward accuracy and faithfulness, other types of translation are more akin to adapting a book for the screen, requiring creative interpretation and adjustments to fit the new context. Just as a filmmaker might reinterpret a novel to fit the cinematic form, a translator will not simply transfer words but adapt content and form to suit a new cultural, linguistic, and rhetorical context. Both processes involve more than direct replication; they require creative decisions that balance fidelity to the original with the needs of the new audience.

# Conclusion

Whether balancing authenticity and accessibility in a tourist booklet, decoding culinary terminology for a global audience, or navigating the subtle cultural connotations of the word “kill” in a gardening manual, students consistently confronted the complexities and rewards of translation. The learning moments described reveal how translation fosters heightened self-reflexivity, encouraging students to critically engage with their own linguistic and cultural biases while collaborating to bridge diverse perspectives. Participating in these projects helped my students become more thoughtful communicators in an increasingly interconnected world. Reflecting on translation can help you understand writing itself as a process of negotiation and mediation. Both writers and translators need to grapple with cultural nuances, rhetorical contexts, and audience expectations. Just as translators must navigate the balance between domestication and estrangement, writers must mediate between faithfully expressing their ideas, opinions, and emotions while also adhering to genre conventions and meeting audience expectations. All acts of communication are based on efforts to mediate. Reflecting on the theory and practice of translation serves as a vital tool in reimagining writing as a dynamic, intercultural, and translinguistic practice.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

To introduce students to key translation concepts, instructors can use *De-mystifying Translation: Introducing Translation to Non-translators* by Lynne Bowker.[^tr-1] Chapter 1 clearly defines essential terms like *source text*, *target text*, and *equivalence*, while Chapter 2 provides a brief history of translation, highlighting its cultural and historical significance. These chapters offer a solid foundation for understanding the practice and importance of translation. In particular, Bowker focuses on the concept of equivalence, which is the relationship between the original text and its translated version. Bowker explains that equivalence is not often achieved at the word level but rather at the level of meaning, emphasizing that translation involves cultural and contextual adjustments. The author highlights the challenges of translating idiomatic expressions, slang, and culturally specific terms, which often require creative solutions such as free translation or transcreation. The book further discusses the importance of translation briefs and how they guide translators in making decisions based on the target audience’s needs. Bowker also examines how some texts, like advertisements, demand more than mere information transfer; they must evoke emotional responses, sometimes requiring adaptations to fit the cultural and linguistic expectations of the target audience.

To introduce intercultural communication, instructors can begin by presenting foundational research from scholars such as Geert Hofstede and Edward T. Hall. Hofstede’s cultural dimensions, including individualism and collectivism, power distance, and uncertainty avoidance, provide a framework for understanding how values influence communication. Hall’s concepts of high-context and low-context cultures help students explore how meaning is conveyed differently across cultural settings.

Building on these ideas, instructors can invite reflections on how culture is often defined by nationality and the limitations of this approach. Students can discuss the risks of relying on national stereotypes and consider how culture is shaped by multiple factors. Instructors can also promote small-group analysis of case studies or examples of intercultural misunderstandings. Students reflect on what led to the miscommunication and how greater cultural awareness could have helped. The activity concludes with a small-group reflection in which students share stories of their own intercultural encounters and consider how their cultural backgrounds influence their communication preferences.

# Discussion Questions

1. The text describes language as inherently ambiguous. How does this ambiguity manifest in your own writing? How might it shape the translation process?

2. How might the cultural background of a translator influence how they translate a text? Can you think of examples where this could create challenges or opportunities?

3. When translating, some meaning is inevitably lost, but new meaning can also emerge. Can you think of an instance where translating an idea or phrase into another language could create new insights or perspectives?

4. In the virtual exchange projects described, students balanced estrangement and domestication in their translations. How does considering your audience shape your writing or communication in general?

5. The Italian project emphasized the cultural context of visual aids. How do visuals contribute to communication, and what happens when cultural meanings of images are misunderstood? Can you share an example?

6. Why do you think food-related terms are particularly challenging to translate? How might these challenges differ from translating other types of content?

# Activities

## Collaborative Translation Practice

Students partner with peers who speak other languages (international students or instructors at their university) to ask about words that are difficult to translate into English. For example, the Italian adjectives *simpatico/simpatica* for a nice, funny, and likeable person, or *Schadenfreude*, a German term for the feeling of pleasure derived from someone else’s misfortune. Students attempt to translate the terms into English. By presenting their findings to the class, students explore how translation fosters empathy and creativity while challenging stereotypes about language and culture.

## Menu Editing Project

In this exercise, students are divided into teams and tasked with editing American restaurant menus to make them accessible, appealing, and culturally relevant for international audiences. Each team selects a menu and analyzes its content, focusing on elements that might confuse or alienate readers from different cultural backgrounds, such as cultural references (e.g., dishes named after US celebrities or regional slang) and ingredient descriptions that rely on assumed knowledge of American cuisine. The teams then rewrite the menus to clarify food descriptions, adapt unfamiliar cultural references, and ensure the tone and imagery are engaging for their intended audience, all while preserving the restaurant’s unique identity. At the end of the exercise, each team presents their revised menu to the class, explaining the rationale behind their choices. They discuss how they balanced maintaining the authenticity of the menu with meeting the expectations and needs of their international audience. This hands-on activity emphasizes the complexities of intercultural communication and encourages critical thinking about how language can bridge or create barriers in global contexts.

## Machine Translation

Students are organized into small groups. Each group is assigned a short text (e.g., a recipe, an advertisement, or a poem) in English, language A, with cultural nuances, idioms, or colloquial expressions. Students use Google Translate or ChatGPT to translate the text into language B---any language spoken proficiently by one of their classmates, or a peer at the university, or their instructor. With the help of the proficient user, students analyze the text produced in language B to understand how the meaning or tone of the original text changed during translation and where cultural references were misunderstood or lost. This activity will help students understand the limitations of AI in accurately conveying tone and meaning, especially when working with idioms, slang, cultural references, and figurative language.
