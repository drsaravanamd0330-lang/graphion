One of the most important documents you will ever write is the application letter (also known as a cover letter or statement of interest) for the job you really want. Many companies looking to hire their ideal candidate ask for an application letter to go with a résumé. When this is the case, you have an amazing opportunity to tell a compelling story of who you are as a professional and how your skills can help the company succeed. For this to happen, though, you need to persuade your reader, a hiring decision-maker, that you are the ideal candidate. This chapter shows you how you can tell your story in a persuasive way that gets results---that all-important interview, which is the first step to getting the job you want.

Before we get to the meat of this chapter, let’s go back a few thousand years to the time of Aristotle (384-322 BCE). He was an ancient Greek philosopher who taught us about the art of persuasion, or rhetoric. The use of rhetoric in our writing influences how other people accept our ideas. He taught that we can use three strategies, among many possible strategies, when trying to persuade a reader: ethos, pathos, and logos. Ethos is the writer’s credibility, pathos is the reader’s emotional reaction, and logos is the message’s logic or sense of reasoning. I recommend these three strategies for two reasons. First, when used together, they create a powerful tipping point of persuasion during the competitive hiring process. Second, all these years later, writers still use these three persuasive strategies because they work and have worked for my former students in helping them get interviews. I am confident that they will work for you, too.

In an application letter, you can use all three strategies, but the most important is pathos. Why? Because more often than not, hiring decision-makers invite a candidate for an interview because they feel good about that person. They are satisfied with the information provided in the letter and enthusiastic about the skills candidate brings to the table. Satisfaction and enthusiasm are emotions you want your reader to feel after reading your letter. You also want to use the application letter to connect to your reader’s values (e.g., integrity, teamwork, passion) to say, in essence, “we are a good fit because we value the same things and want the same success.” An application letter is often read first before the résumé to help the hiring decision-maker determine a candidate’s viability, and when they find a good candidate, they act quickly. Your responsibility as an application letter writer is to convince the hiring decision-maker to act in your favor---to invite you for an interview.

Persuading the hiring decision-maker to act quickly requires a bit of finesse, but you can do it if you make certain skillful rhetorical moves in your writing that combine ethos, pathos, and logos. I recommend this approach because ethos, pathos, and logos are powerful and they work. While all three are important, I recommend writing with a heavy emphasis on pathos. It is important for you to connect with your reader in an emotional way. You can generate a sense of satisfaction and enthusiasm in your reader by writing an amazing application letter. Only then can you get what you want. The rest of this chapter explains the rhetorical (or persuasive) moves you can make and why they are important.

# General Rhetorical Moves

Before a hiring decision-maker reads your letter, they skim it and decide whether to continue or not. Therefore, it is important for your letter to meet certain criteria. The hiring decision-maker uses these criteria to determine your ethos (credibility) and react through pathos (feeling good about you as a candidate for the job). If you pass this first critical ethos and pathos test, then you get the hiring decision-maker’s attention.

It is helpful to understand the expected format for a letter. It is possible that you may submit your letter through an online system with boxes for you to add content that you cut and paste from your letter; however, many online systems may require you to attach a letter saved as a PDF. In this case, I recommend you follow a typical letter format. You start with your address at the top followed by the date, the hiring company’s name and address, the salutation, the letter’s content in sentences and paragraphs, and the closing with your name. Ask your instructor to show you a good example.

- The letter’s paragraphs are typically single spaced with a blank line between paragraphs and no paragraph indenting. All content should align with the left margin. This formatting style is called block-text format and is commonly used in the workplace.
- The letter is typically no more than one page. Use one-inch margins, a standard font (e.g., Times New Roman, Calibri, Aptos), and 11- or 12-point font size.
- It is a good idea to tailor the letter’s content to the job advertisement. A hiring decision-maker can spot a generic application letter within seconds. They will not read a letter that does not address their needs.
- If possible, I recommend you address the letter to a person. If that is not possible, then address the letter to a unit within the company (e.g., Dear Human Resources, Dear Marketing Department), to a group (e.g., Dear Hiring Committee), or to a person’s title (e.g., Dear Hiring Manager, Dear Director of Counseling Services). Avoid addressing your letter to “Whom It May Concern.” This type of salutation is impersonal and outdated. It can also show that you are not interested in the company or that you lack initiative to learn more about it.
- The letter should use language that is appropriate for the job, which might include specialized terminology, and often includes grammar, punctuation, and capitalization choices that the reader will view as professional. For guidance, check out the company’s website and see how it is using language to present itself to the public. Ask someone who can give you honest feedback to review your letter: a writing tutor, a career coach, a professor, or a professional in your field. Also, make every word and every sentence count. State your points clearly and concisely.

These general moves may seem superficial to the content moves you learn about in the rest of this chapter. Keep in mind, though, that a hiring decision-maker looks at your letter first before reading it, so it is important to make a good first impression. A first impression is an emotional reaction, and you want your reader to react positively---to feel good about you from the beginning.

# Introduction of the Application Letter

The introduction is the most important part of your letter. Here is where you make your first emotional connection with your reader. However, before we get into that, in the first sentence, you should identify the job for which you are applying (including a job number if it is available). If your application goes through a gatekeeper (e.g., a human resource representative, recruiter, or AI tool that controls access to the hiring decision-maker), which is common in big companies, then they need to know you are in the right place. In the rest of the introduction, make the next move.

# Rhetorical Move: Connect Your Values to Your Reader’s Values

As stated above, your reader---the hiring decision-maker---will not read a generic application letter that does not address their needs. The first persuasive move you make is to demonstrate that you know something about the company. Visit their website and learn about the company. Most companies have an “About Us” page that tells their story. Find something you genuinely like about the company and mention it and say why, carefully pivoting to mention that you value the same thing they value. When you connect your values to theirs, you indicate that you are a good fit for each other. People like to be with others like them, so when you show respect by doing research and praising their good work, then you quickly start to build a positive relationship that propels the reader to continue reading your application letter. They start to like you and want to know more about you. To be sure, this rhetorical move is pathos at its finest. Here is an example of how an undergraduate student made this move while applying for a job as a preschool teacher.

> While researching jobs, I came across your post looking for a preschool teacher. I was excited to learn that your curriculum is [focused on developing life skills][^a1] as well as allowing your students to be creative with their own projects. Having a skills-based [curriculum] focus … is a fantastic way to let students grow as critical thinkers and connect what they’re working on with appropriate life skills. At [*name of university*][^2], I study how children acquire knowledge, and I have found they learn best through play … I can be beneficial in applying these [creative learning] skills [to your curriculum] at [*name of children’s center*].

This example demonstrates that you do not have to say much to connect your values to the company’s values. In this case, the student learned that the company teaches life skills through creative projects, which matched their[^3] own understanding and experience of how children learn. They have “learning through play” in common and appear to be a good fit.

An additional example shows how student writers can sometimes get intimidated when well-known companies offer job opportunities. However, this student took a chance and started their application letter this way.

> After looking through my university’s website and coming across [*company’s name and program*] internship opportunities, I decided to take a chance and apply for [a summer internship] … I have been … impressed with the growth and accomplishments at [*company name*], especially when it comes to giving students like [me] opportunities to learn and grow in a vibrant working community … your focus on customers and [their] satisfaction aligns with my belief that, as a future software developer, it is my duty to make others’ lives easier. It made me incredibly happy when a new [*facility’s name*] was built … close to where I live [because] it means that I will have the ability to apply and possibly work for one of the most well-known and respected companies in the world today.

This introduction is a bit unconventional, but it shows that the student appreciates the opportunity and understands its worth, is willing to take a risk, and shares a common value of taking care of customers. While the student may not have much confidence, they do have guts, and I know this company values a determined, honest employee. The student uses pathos well.

# Body of the Application Letter

The body of the application letter can be one or two paragraphs. Do not make either paragraph too long. Long blocks of text discourage readers from reading and may stop your reader in their tracks. Remember that a hiring decision-maker has a stack of letters to review, so they look for any excuse to stop reading. You want your reader to stick around, so give your reader a reason to keep reading. The following two persuasive moves will do just that.

# Rhetorical Move: Share a Compelling Success Story

Too often, application letter writers summarize their résumé. Do not make this common mistake. Instead, use the prime real estate of the letter’s body to tell a success story of how you used your skills to solve a problem. A story is fun to read, persuasive, and memorable, and you definitely want to be remembered. Your story may be more compelling if it relates to a need the company mentioned in the job advertisement, so go that route if you can; however, you can still tell a good story because many skills transfer from one context to another. You can draw from your coursework, workplace, or community, church, or club activities. Your story tells who was involved, what the problem was, and how you solved it using your skills. When you do so, you show your skills in action, and you show your personality in a favorable way. Here is an example of how a student shared a success story while working as an oncology certified nursing assistant when she applied for a similar certified nursing assistant position at an Allergy and Asthma Clinic.

> [While working] as an Oncology CNA, I have had hundreds of different patients each with their own unique story. One specific day at work this past December, I was assigned to be a sitter outside of a patient’s room because of her past suicidal ideations and current health issues. My patient was feeling very lonely because she had COVID-19. Therefore, she could not have many visitors because they would be at risk for getting sick. Even though my job was to sit outside of her room and make sure she was safe, I elected to speak to her while she was awake to alleviate some of her loneliness. I listened to all her concerns about being discharged, the medications she was receiving, and her story about what she was getting for Christmas. With all her concerns, I made sure to properly communicate those to the registered nurse so that she was aware. Working in healthcare is [more than just] doing the bare minimum … because everyone is a human being and deserves to be treated like one. My priority is to always make my patients feel comfortable because being sick and in the hospital is not fun and can be very scary.

This example demonstrates several important nursing skills many hospitals and clinics desire in their employees: seeing patients as human beings, being aware of their various needs, and going above and beyond to help. The story also shines a light on the student’s personality. We get the sense that they are a caring individual who understands people’s feelings and knows how to comfort the lonely and scared---all desirable qualities that make this student an attractive candidate that the hiring decision-maker is sure to like. Even though they are applying for a job as a certified nursing assistant in a different clinical setting, they have been able to demonstrate through this story that they know how to care for patients. Their good use of pathos is working in the student’s favor.

Another student told the following story in their application letter about helping a young girl they taught come out of her shell and the positive impact it had on her in an American Sign Language (ASL) class.

> One experience from my time at [*name of school*] that stuck with me is when I met a student named Amelia who was withdrawn and often did not engage verbally with the class. As the semester went on, I noticed her taking a liking to ASL and would use it as much as possible during our time together … I gave the students [an assignment to bring] a short and easy recipe to translate into ASL. I provided them with the choice to present to the class or to me privately … Many students [signed their] recipes … to the class, while the quieter students presented their recipes to me privately. Amelia decided she wanted to present [her] recipe … chocolate chip cookies … to the class. After she presented it to the class, she also surprised everyone with actual cookies she made using the recipe.

From this story, we can infer that the student helped the young girl engage in class by actively using ASL with her to the point that the young girl felt comfortable enough to sign her recipe in front of her peers. That is a remarkable achievement for both the student and the young girl---a story definitely worth telling to evoke positive feelings (or pathos) in the hiring decision-maker.

# Rhetorical Move: Show Your Skills in Action

While sharing a success story is a powerful persuasive move, you can also show your skills in action by providing examples. For example, if you say you work well with others and leave it at that, then you are blowing hot air. Your vague statement requires your reader to take you at your word, which they are unlikely to do (even if they start to like you after reading your introduction). It is important to give a concrete example of how you work well with others. Even better, give a concrete example that has a positive outcome.

The student example below does just that. In fact, the student writer made two rhetorical moves (a success story that included concrete examples) in one paragraph, which put them in an excellent position to make a good impression quickly and efficiently.

> One rewarding experience I had was as a private tutor for a student named Arthur. The previous semester, Arthur had failed all of his tests in mathematics ... As his tutor, I had to both evaluate his current understanding of mathematics and educate him on the necessary material. For instance, while working on a section with decimals, I realized that Arthur was having trouble understanding the ideas behind them. In order to decipher what he knew, I quizzed him so that I could ascertain where his difficulties lay. Soon, I realized that Arthur had not fully grasped the concept of parts of a whole. With this realization, I began instructing him on the concept verbally, and then I evaluated his comprehension of the lesson. Arthur still had trouble, so I changed tactics. Instead of appealing to an auditory learning style, I switched to visuals in order to better educate him on the idea. This approach worked much better. Working with Arthur was like playing chess; in order to succeed, I had to adapt my tactics to the situation or, in this case, Arthur’s learning style. By the end of the semester, Arthur went from a failing grade in his previous attempt to a B average, and he even earned an A on the final test! I truly enjoyed the challenge and reward of helping Arthur and, more importantly, I realized what I want to do with my life: help others learn and love math.

Instead of making a general statement such as “I know how to teach math,” the student gave a concrete example of the steps they took to teach math, specifically decimals. Instead of saying “I know learning styles and can apply them when I teach,” the student gave a concrete example of how they switched teaching tactics to fit the math student’s learning style. It is easy to say you know how to do something; it is more difficult to come up with concrete examples. However, the extra effort is worth it because they are powerful and persuasive. It is pathos at work once again.

In addition, concrete examples told through an experience can help you demonstrate how skills in one context can transfer to another. Even though the student in the above example was applying for a similar position as a math tutor, they could use parts of their story to apply for a different position that requires “adapting tactics to fit a situation” (e.g., software developer, actuary, financial planner). While you should strive to use concrete examples and experiences that relate to the position for which you are applying, you may need to show how your skills can be used in different contexts. In these cases, you should foreground your skills and explain how the skills transfer to the context of the position you want. For example, the math tutor applying for a job as a software developer could begin their story by explaining that they have experience adapting tactics to fit a situation and then showing that skill in action by telling a shortened version of Arthur’s story. They could conclude by explaining how their experience with Arthur relates to the job and/or how that experience has prepared them to adapt as a software developer. This concluding move is vital because it helps the reader see how the skill transfers from one context to the next. I call it “closing the loop.” Always remember to close the loop by telling your reader what your stories, experiences, and examples mean in the larger context of the job for which you are applying.

When you show your skills in action by sharing stories, experiences, and concrete examples, remember that details matter. Quantify what you can, mention positive outcomes, and do not be afraid to use descriptive words (i.e., adjectives such as proactive, adaptable, collaborative, and passionate). Your responsibility as a letter writer is to give enough details that allow your reader to envision in their own mind’s eye what happened and what you did.

In addition to pathos, logos and ethos are at work in these stories and examples. They serve as evidence, which is your use of logos, and they increase your ethos (credibility). In the body of your application letter, you build a strong case for yourself as the ideal candidate by showing your skills in action. Your argument (or main point) is that you can do the job because you already have the skills the company needs. This move helps you avoid the pitfall of focusing on what the company can do for you. Companies do not care what you need; they only care about what they need, and they need skilled employees. Those who act get work done. Completed work leads to positive outcomes. Share your stories and experiences with this focus in mind.

# Conclusion of the Application Letter

In the conclusion of your letter, in the first sentence, I recommend you refer to your enclosed résumé so that the reader knows it is available. In the last sentence, I suggest you give your contact information (i.e., phone number and email address). In between the first and last sentences, you can make these next persuasive moves.

# Rhetorical Move: Focus on a Key Feeling (Enthusiasm)

So far, you have written a highly persuasive letter. It looks good, it is well written, and it combines ethos, pathos, and logos. Your reader is at the end. Now what? I recommend you end your letter by using pathos because your reader needs one last push to act in your favor. Since many people make decisions based on how they feel, you can give yourself a competitive edge when you focus on a key feeling: your enthusiasm for the job. Express your enthusiasm for the opportunity to help the company succeed and how you can do that. You can also express your desire to have an opportunity to share more about how your skills can help the company succeed. However, I recommend that you do not ask for an interview and that you do not say you will contact the company. Hiring decision-makers often work according to slow timelines, and they will contact you when they are ready. Do not undo your good work. Be patient with and respect the process and have confidence in yourself. I include two examples in the next section that show this move of focusing on a key feeling.

# Rhetorical Move: Say Thank You

Above all, thank the reader for considering your application. It shows good manners when you thank someone for doing something they may not want to do such as reading a stack of application letters. A little appreciation for their time and attention goes a long way, plus you demonstrate humility, an attractive quality that further strengthens your likeability (good use of pathos). Also let your reader know that you look forward to hearing from them.

All these emotions you have---enthusiasm, respect, appreciation, and humility---work in your favor as you await your reader’s decision to invite you for an interview. They also contribute to your reader’s overall emotional reactions of satisfaction and enthusiasm for you as the ideal candidate. Here is an example of how a student made these two moves in their conclusion.

> I am very excited at the possibility [of joining] your team of professionals [who strive] to take care of their community and patients. I [look] forward to hearing from you [soon]. You can reach me at [*phone number*] or at [*email*]. Thank you for your time.

Another student concluded their letter this way.

> I am excited about the opportunity to contribute my skills, creativity, and passion to [*name of company*]. I admire your commitment to innovative design solutions and believe I can be an asset to your team. I look forward to hearing from you, and please reach out if you have any questions [about] my application or portfolio.

These conclusions work well in their simplicity because they come from students like you who are learning how to communicate their value in a respectful way. They also show the importance of pathos at the end of your letter. It gives you one more chance to connect with your reader by conveying your enthusiasm for the position and your gratitude for their time. You want the hiring decision-maker to invite you for an interview, so inspire them to act. Leave them feeling good about you and what you can do.

# Conclusion

Writing an application letter is a tricky undertaking, but the rhetorical moves discussed in this chapter can help you to be more persuasive and memorable and move you closer to getting you what you want: your dream job doing what brings you joy. To be sure, there are no guarantees when it comes to applying for a job. Many factors beyond your control influence decisions during the hiring process. However, based on my experience helping students like you write their application letters, these rhetorical moves are highly effective. Over the years, I have known students to receive quick requests for interviews. These strategies do work, and I encourage you to give them a try and see what happens. You may just find yourself doing a job you never thought possible until now.

# Key Takeaways

The key takeaways from this chapter are as follows:

- Connect your values to your reader’s values by researching the company and finding something you like about them. It is a great way to make a good first impression.
- Share a compelling success story and give concrete examples to show your skills in action. Success stories are memorable, and concrete examples persuade your reader that you have the skills to do the job.
- Use a combination of ethos (your credibility), logos (evidence of your skills), and pathos (positive feelings in the reader and a good impression of you as the ideal candidate), but focus primarily on pathos. Your reader is more likely to invite you for an interview if they feel good about you, are satisfied that you have the skills to do the job, and sense your enthusiasm for the job, which, in turn, makes them enthusiastic about you.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

This chapter helps undergraduate students write a persuasive job application letter that moves them closer to getting the job they want.[^tr-1] An application letter is often required by a hiring organization to accompany a résumé and is also often read first to help the hiring decision-maker determine the candidate’s viability. Writing an effective application letter requires skillful rhetorical moves that many students do not typically learn in their courses. Therefore, this chapter recommends some rhetorical moves they can make in their application letter to get that all-important interview. The rhetorical moves are based on Aristotle’s ethos, pathos, and logos appeals. While all three are important, I argue that pathos is the most important for job application letters. The chapter explains multiple rhetorical moves (from formatting their letter and connecting with their reader to showing their skills in action and ending on a positive note) and why they are important (creating a feeling of satisfaction and enthusiasm in their reader). It also provides examples of how undergraduate students have made these moves in their own application letters.

Because of its focus on persuasive writing, this chapter may be helpful when teaching students about ethos, pathos, and logos. Students may find writing an application letter a meaningful exercise because it serves a practical purpose and allows them to write about a subject they know well---themselves. This chapter may also be helpful for instructors who teach a unit on career preparation. Students are often taught how to write a résumé, but the often-overlooked application letter is equally important and needs to be taught as well. This chapter, therefore, provides the support instructors need to teach students how to write a persuasive applica-

tion letter using rhetorical moves not typically seen in this genre, which is why they are so effective.

When teaching how to write a persuasive application letter, instructors should know that students struggle to:

- Find a success story and examples that show their skills in action
- Avoid summarizing their résumé
- Avoid making general statements about their skills
- Tailor their letter to a specific job advertisement
- Learn about a company through research
- Analyze and use information in a job advertisement

Therefore, instructors may want to consider working with students individually or in small groups over a two- or three-week period as they brainstorm, draft, and revise their application letters to help them overcome these struggles. They may also want to find additional writing samples to discuss in class or draft their own letter using the rhetorical moves discussed in the chapter and then share their work with their students. Finally, instructors should encourage students to seek additional writing support at the writing center or career center. The activities discussed below may assist instructors in these efforts.

# Discussion Questions

1. Why do some hiring decision-makers request an application letter in addition to a résumé? Isn’t a résumé enough?

2. Why should you tailor your application letter to a specific job? What’s wrong with writing a one-size-fits-all letter?

3. What challenges or struggles might you encounter as you write your application letter and how can you overcome them?

4. How much time do you think a hiring decision-maker spends reading your letter before deciding whether to interview you or not? Why? How can you get and keep their attention?

5. All three persuasive appeals---ethos, pathos, and logos---are important, but why is pathos the most important when writing an application letter?

6. Why are success stories and concrete examples of your skills in action so persuasive?

7. What kinds of information can you find in the job advertisement that can help you write your application letter?

# Activities

Below are six class activities that can help support students in their development of writing an amazing application letter.

## Job Advertisement Analysis

In small groups, ask students to analyze a job advertisement, looking specifically at required and preferred skills and job duties. Emphasize that the job advertisement provides key words and phrases about skills that students can use in their application letter. Also help students understand that the hiring company has problems that need to be solved by the person they hire. The job duties section often gives clues about these problems. Students can then focus their letter on how they would solve these problems using their skills.

## Brainstorming Activity

Give students about ten minutes to find a job advertisement they might use to write an application letter. Then, have students complete a worksheet that asks them the following questions:

1. Who is the employer, what do they do, and what do you like about them?

2. What does the employer want in a new hire?

3. What qualifications do you have that most appeal to the employer?

4. What key words does the job advertisement contain that should appear in your application letter?

5. What possible success stories could you tell in your application letter?

## Annotation Activity

Have students annotate an application letter using the information provided in the chapter. Ask students to look for and identify words, sentences, and paragraphs where rhetorical moves were made. Also, have students annotate (identify) the different parts of a letter to help them understand how to format a letter.

## Writing Workshop

Focus a class period on writing different paragraphs (or just one paragraph) of the application letter (e.g., the introduction, a success story, the conclusion). After explaining how to write that paragraph using the information provided in the chapter, have students write for fifteen minutes, then put them in small groups where students read their paragraph and get feedback. If time allows, repeat the process with another paragraph. As students write and meet in groups, check in with them and offer your feedback as well.

## Student Conferences

Meet with students individually to review their draft application letters and provide feedback that can help them improve their drafts. Another option is to meet with students during the brainstorming phase to help them think about and find success stories and examples to show their skills in action.

## Peer Review

Once students have drafted their application letters, pair them with a peer and have them review each other’s drafts. Students should provide feedback on effective use of the rhetorical moves, proper letter formatting, and writing correctness, conciseness, and clarity. To ensure that students take peer review seriously, have students grade each other on how useful their peer reviewer’s comments were in helping them improve their draft.
