In my early twenties, I quit my job as a high school teacher because I wanted to travel. A close friend introduced me to the lifestyle of a digital nomad---working remotely while running an online business. Inspired by this idea, I started a digital marketing company and moved to Colombia. With a knack for writing, I enjoyed developing content marketing campaigns for businesses: I managed their social media accounts, created newsletters to drive sales, and wrote blog articles to help grow their reputations. My marketing agency allowed me to travel the world for a couple of years. However, I left marketing in 2017 to attend graduate school. Looking back now, I feel ashamed of some of the writing I did back then. While my English training made me a successful marketer, I realized how different the values of academic writing are from those of marketing.

When I was a marketer, I engaged in some sketchy writing practices. I recall several times, for example, when I was writing a blog, that I found myself patchwriting: copying and pasting bits of information from various sources that were helpful and rearranging them in a way that disguised my plagiarism. I even had a client call me one time to address the patchwriting. He asked me to do some additional rearranging so that my blog article wouldn’t closely resemble a Wikipedia entry. In school, I was trained to give credit to others for their ideas, so it surprised me that he was so upfront about it, like it was a common practice. I knew patchwriting was unethical, but my immediate goal as a marketer was to save time and write content to convert audiences into sales leads. While academic writing values ethics and a carefully crafted ethos, marketing is about numbers: how many views, how many shares, and ultimately, how much money can be made from a piece of writing. I’m not saying academic writing is inherently ethical, but it is bound by conventions like transparent citations, peer review, and editorial fact-checking, which typically make it more credible.

Now, as a college English professor, I see content marketing everywhere, and I work to help my students distinguish between quality sources and marketing-driven content. In classes, I often talk about my experiences as a marketer, in hopes of helping students build more critical source evaluation practices. In recent years, I have noticed that content marketing is becoming more prevalent, and its ability to mimic credible sources is increasingly convincing. This growing trend complicates research for students, making it harder for them to differentiate between reliable sources and persuasive marketing materials. I want this chapter to validate student writers who struggle with finding credible online sources and help students develop an understanding of content marketing and its impact on research.

# What Is Content Marketing?

According to Amanda Wall, a User Experience (UX) researcher, and Clay Spinuzzi, a professor of rhetoric and writing at the University of Texas, content marketing generally refers to a method of marketing that tries to “sell without selling” directly. Wall and Spinuzzi conducted a research study about the different kinds of genres that content marketers produce, which I will refer to throughout this chapter. They define content marketing as

> a method of marketing a product or service by creating and distributing free informational or entertainment content, especially online. Companies produce blogs, white papers, social media posts, videos, images, websites, microsites, webinars, and other content. This content is designed to be valuable or interesting to consumers on its own merits, so they will consume it willingly. (137)

If that sounds a little vague to you, it’s because content marketing is not so much a single genre, but a “network of content in a variety of genres, media, and platforms” meant to build up the credibility of a brand (Wall and Spinuzzi 138).

To demonstrate: when I was working in marketing, I had a client who developed software that allows owners of construction equipment to list their machinery on an app and make money renting it out to other parties---think Airbnb for renting construction equipment. I wrote a variety of content for this client, starting with a guide for choosing the right cherry picker. Then, I repurposed the cherry picker guide into blog articles, Instagram posts, and email campaigns, each designed to subtly build the credibility of the company while generating sales leads. I put a link to the cherry picker guide on the website and in the Instagram profile, but users had to enter their email address to download it. This gave me a chance to send them our bi-weekly newsletter. All of these genres---the blogs, the social media posts, the email newsletters, and the guide---were content marketing. While none of them were advertisements, per se, they all contributed to the ethos of the app. As Wall and Spinuzzi note, their purpose is to persuade people to visit the website or app on their own accord.

# Why Is Content Marketing So Difficult to Identify?

Content marketing can be hard to recognize because it is designed to look like something readers naturally want to read, rather than obvious advertising. Wall and Spinuzzi explain that the core strategy of content marketing is to promote products without directly selling them. They point out that marketers achieve this by providing valuable content, often in the form of how-to guides that demonstrate product use. In some cases, companies create journalistic-style content that mimics traditional news articles, making it feel like unbiased information rather than a sales pitch (Wall and Spinuzzi 152). This approach makes marketing materials feel credible and unbiased, even when they are carefully crafted to guide consumer decisions.

As a content writer, I was intentional about ensuring that my writing enhanced the credibility of my clients’ companies. For example, the guide to selecting the right cherry picker came from a company that rents a lot of different equipment, and the owner had been in construction for years. On the surface, the guide seemed entirely informational, offering advice on selecting a cherry picker and highlighting key considerations like height, weight, and cost. If you followed the CRAAP method suggested by Randall McClure in another *Writing Spaces* chapter, you might find my blogs and guide appear credible. The following is a breakdown of the CRAAP method for checking sources:

- *Currency*: The guide and blogs were recently written and included reviews of several popular cherry pickers in circulation at the time.
- *Relevance*: They talked about height, weight, and cost as considerations in selecting the right equipment, all things the reader would want to know.
- *Authority*: It was a ghostwritten article, so my name was absent. If you look around the website, you might find the CEO’s name and his long history in construction.
- *Accuracy*: I researched this writing, so the information was accurate.
- *Purpose*: The information never said, “rent from us now.” Instead, it appeared to offer helpful considerations for renting equipment.

Content marketing is specifically written to garner your trust, which is why it can be so difficult to distinguish. It’s important to remember that even when a source passes the CRAAP test, it might not be credible enough for your research projects. This is why you want to develop additional critical reading skills for web research.

To understand why I was not an expert on this topic, let’s think back on that guide to selecting a cherry picker. I did, after all, do enough research to write an informative guide. First of all, I started my research process by looking up “what is a cherry picker?” If you’re not familiar with them, a cherry picker is a standing work platform on a hydraulic lift that allows a worker to perform manual labor high above the ground. I knew my audience ultimately wanted to know “which cherry picker do I need to get the job done with the least amount of expense?” and I wrote my guide to answer this question. I wrote about telescopic boom lifts vs. articulating boom lifts. I also wrote about the versatility of aerial work platforms. Since I had never driven a cherry picker, never rented one myself, never even carried out a construction project big enough to necessitate a cherry picker, I did not think to talk about more important issues like safety considerations, insurance policies, or the typical training and certification required to legally operate a cherry picker. Had I been an actual expert on renting and operating the equipment, I would have had to include these in my guide. The problem with content marketing is that while skilled writers often do know enough to make their writing interesting and helpful, they rarely have enough expertise to make content credible enough to cite.

Back then, I often spent hours researching and gathering information before drafting an article. Now, with the rise of generative AI, writers can locate information, synthesize background knowledge, and generate drafts in a fraction of the time. AI-generated writing makes content marketing even more pervasive now than it was in 2016, when I was writing about cherry pickers. AI does not generate good-quality content, and it certainly has led to a flood of sources that are not appropriate for research. Every day, there is more content on the internet to sift through, which makes finding credible sources that much harder. When I teach research in my classes, students are often surprised when they navigate the library databases for the first time and find it easier than expected to find quality, relevant sources.

Academic sources are typically written by real experts, people who devote their careers to studying a topic. They are also peer-reviewed by other experts in similar topics to ensure they are accurate, clear, and transparent about how they arrived at conclusions. That concentration of pre-vetted sources in a database allows the writer to spend less time weeding out low-quality sources. Furthermore, it’s common in an academic article to see a discussion of limitations, which acknowledges the study’s weaknesses. Academic writers are careful about articulating the validity of their work, noting factors like sample size, scope, or methodology, and explaining how these limitations affect the interpretation of results or point to a need for future research. All of these measures demonstrate the difference in values between academic writing---which prioritizes transparency and credibility---and content marketing, which prioritizes manufactured appeal and sales potential.

Of course, you can’t always find what you are looking for in the library databases. Things like first-hand experiences and materials on less academic subjects necessitate looking for information on the greater World Wide Web. Doing this research requires a little more work sorting through sources. In particular, my students overwhelmingly struggle with differentiating content marketing from more credible references. In this next section, I am going to share examples of content marketing that students in my class initially considered using for their research projects. Two of the examples are blogs published on company websites, while the third is a promotional video produced by a nonprofit research center. Together, they illustrate a range of strategies content writers use to create value for their readers and the variety of products and services they promote. As I walk you through these case studies, I will discuss why these pieces of content marketing seem credible and how to distinguish them from more reliable materials.

# Case Studies

# FightCamp Blog

My first example comes from a student who was an avid boxer and was studying exercise science. He was researching how to bring a more diverse clientele to his boxing gym and wanted to use the blog post “How Boxing Boosts Brain Health and Prevents Dementia” to introduce the idea that boxing may be beneficial to individuals with varying cognitive abilities. This blog post, by Mollie McGurk, argues that boxing training may support brain health by combining physical exercise with cognitive challenges, potentially helping to prevent neurodegenerative diseases like dementia. The blog is particularly convincing because it features quotes and insights from Dr. Julie Brody Magid, Psy.D., a clinical director at McLean Hospital’s Memory Disorders Assessment Clinic. By citing her, the blog seems to have some scientific credibility to the claims about boxing’s cognitive benefits. To the author’s credit, the blog acknowledges that boxing itself has not been extensively studied, but it cites research on the benefits of agility training for Parkinson’s disease, suggesting boxing may offer similar advantages. I was stunned when I first saw this title listed in my student’s works cited page because I am much more familiar with the links between repeated blows to the head and traumatic brain injury. I understand how the author of this blog reached the claim that boxing *might* boost brain health, but boxing itself was not tested in the referenced research. This is exactly the kind of mixed or misapplied evidence that peer review is designed to catch.

It’s important to note that FightCamp is a company that sells at-home boxing workout equipment; they are not a boxing gym. However, at the time my student was doing the research, the blog post did not clearly distinguish between sparring, which is boxing with an opponent, and boxercise, which is a boxing-inspired fitness workout. Had my student arrived at the blog through the FightCamp website, the context might have been clearer. But because he encountered it through a Google search, he assumed the post was claiming that actual boxing improves brain health. This example demonstrates why it’s important to do some contextual research when you find source material you’d like to use in your papers.

A closer look reveals the blog post does have a few signs that it’s content marketing. To check its credibility, I start by identifying who is publishing it and whether they have a bias. In this case, FightCamp sells boxing equipment and training programs, so their goal is to promote boxing fitness and attract customers. This means the blog is not just sharing information---it’s also trying to persuade readers to take up boxing fitness. When students are trying to determine the credibility of the source, the second thing I usually suggest is to click open the links in another tab to see if the author is citing their sources, boosting the Search Engine Optimization (SEO), or a bit of both. Generally, you know that a credible author should tell you where they are getting their information. When you write an academic paper, you use in-text citations to do this. Web writers tend to use hyperlinks to effectively do the same.

However, marketers don’t just use hyperlinks to provide helpful resources; they also use them to influence both users and search engines. SEO is a marketing strategy that helps websites appear higher in search results by using specific keywords and linking strategies. Search engines, like Google, rely on hyperlinks to understand what a page is about and decide whether to display it in search results. Pages with well-placed internal links tend to rank higher because they appear more organized and relevant. To figure out whether a hyperlink is meant to inform readers or keep them within a company’s website, I like to right-click 4-5 links and check where they go. If most of them lead to other pages on the same website rather than independent sources, it’s likely that the page is using SEO tactics to keep users engaged rather than citing external sources.

Let’s go back to the FightCamp blog. When I click the first five hyperlinks on the boxing blog, I get the following:

- Link 1: Another blog post on the same website by the same author about the same topic.
- Link 2: Dr. Julie Brody Magid’s website, where she advertises her therapy services---this is generally a good sign, but I do not see anything on her website about combat sports.
- Link 3: A nonprofit organization that provides boxing-based fitness programs to help people with Parkinson’s disease improve their quality of life.
- Link 4: The same blog post as link 1.
- Link 5: A post about why FightCamp is the best home gym. There is the sale!

Most hyperlinks led back to FightCamp’s content, boosting SEO rather than citing real research. One linked to a therapist’s site without any mention of boxing, and another used the bandwagon fallacy---suggesting that because others believe in boxing’s benefits, you should too. While some links lead to reputable sources, others simply redirect to FightCamp’s own content or promotional materials. This is a common tactic in content marketing, where the goal is not just to inform but to subtly persuade readers to trust the brand and its products. By critically evaluating hyperlinks and considering the publisher’s intent, writers can better distinguish between genuine information and marketing disguised as expertise.

# Lattice Blog

Let’s have a look at another example. This second example comes from a student researching annual performance reviews for a genre analysis paper. She intended to use the blog post titled “Tips for Writing a Strong Self-Evaluation (With Examples)” (see Figure 1) to discuss what makes an effective self-evaluation.

The headline Tips for Writing a Strong Self-Evaluation (With Examples) sits under breadcrumb links reading Library, Articles, Performance Reviews, above bylines for Catherine Tansey and Camille Hogg, freelance content writers. To the right, an illustration shows a green apple whose reflection in a mirror is golden. The navigation bar offers Platform, Solutions, Resources, Pricing, Log in and Request a demo, and the article below is dated September 20, 2024.](assets/figure-p008-1.png)\
Figure 1: Screenshot of a Lattice blog post titled “Tips for Writing a Strong Self-Evaluation (With Examples),” featuring a green apple reflected in a mirror as a golden apple. You can visit the full blog post at: https://lattice.com/articles/tips-for-writing-a-strong-self-evaluation-plus-specific-examples-to-make-yours-shine.

The blog post undoubtedly provides value for the reader. It breaks down the self-evaluation process step by step, offering practical guidance, reflection strategies, and real-world examples to help employees articulate their strengths and areas for improvement. How can we tell that it’s content marketing? If you do the hyperlink check here, you’ll find that this post does less self-promotion. When I open the first five links, four of them actually take me to studies. Only one takes me to another post on the Lattice website. The blog post cites Gallup surveys and other performance management studies, reinforcing its recommendations with actual data. However, students should be aware that Lattice is a Human Resources software company, meaning the content in this blog is also designed to promote their tools and templates for workplace reviews.

There are other signs that we can look at. One test is to check out the author’s credentials. As McClure notes in his *Writing Spaces* chapter, “Understanding the credentials of the author or source is particularly important in conducting sound academic research and especially during the age of the open Web” (228). In the case of the Lattice blog, both authors are listed as Freelance Content Writers. This means they likely do not have any expertise in human resources processes; they were hired to write good content. Sometimes this title is not so plainly written on the page.

As Wall and Spinuzzi note in their article about understanding the genre ecologies of content marketing, content writers go by many different titles. What if instead of *Content Writer*, you saw *HR Writer* or *Workplace* *Strategy Expert*? Many companies use various titles to make the author seem more credible, even if their actual expertise in HR is limited or nonexistent. Titles can give the impression that the author has formal training or industry experience, when in reality, they may just be skilled at researching and producing engaging content. As Wall and Spinuzzi point out, content marketing relies on strategic labeling to shape reader perceptions and build trust. This highlights why it’s important to go beyond the job title and look for additional credentials---such as degrees, work history, or affiliations with professional organizations---before accepting an article as an authoritative source on a subject.

If students are not sure about the credibility of the author, I always suggest a Google search. This practice takes a little (but not much) time. However, it is the kind of lateral reading that Carillo and Horning suggest in their *Writing Spaces* chapter, “Effectively and Efficiently Reading the Credibility of Online Sources.” They explain that a good researcher needs to “leave the site in question to explore more about the author of the piece. What can you find out about the author elsewhere on the Web? Does the author seem like an expert on the subject? What else has the author written?” (39) Despite this advice, I sometimes have a hard time convincing students to investigate an author until I show them examples of why and how to do this. When I look up Catherine Tansey from the Lattice post, her website lists her as “a Madrid-based journalist who covers food and beverage, travel, craft, and sustainability.” This raises an important question: Does a journalist with no stated background in human resources or performance management have the expertise to provide reliable guidance on self-evaluations? This is pretty clearly not her expertise. But Camille Hogg’s personal website, “Fabled Content,” says she’s freelance writer for human resources technology and software services. This gets a little trickier!

When someone has you stumped, do not be afraid to look up their LinkedIn account. You want to look for their previous job titles and their former education. When I look up Hogg on LinkedIn, I find that she has a master’s degree in French and Spanish and a PhD in Hispanic Studies. This explains why the blog post actually cited research, but this research does not make either author an expert on the topic. These majors gave her some excellent research and writing skills, but likely not the experience in human resources to offer insider advice on how to develop a good self-evaluation for work. It is also important to note that when you are dealing with web sources, you will not always have an author listed on the page. In such cases, it’s often safe to assume the piece was written by an in-house marketer or a hired ghostwriter on behalf of the organization. When this is the case, you can’t look into the author’s credentials; it’s another tactic used to obfuscate accountability and expertise. Do you trust a company selling a product to give you all of the information you need to make a decision? If you do not have an author, you need other tactics for discerning content marketing from citation-worthy sources.

Another simple way to spot content marketing is by looking for a call to action (also called the CTA in marketing lingo), like a prompt to sign up, buy something, or learn more. In my cherry picker example above, I asked readers to sign up for the newsletter with their emails, so I could send them more content marketing. Wall and Spinuzzi explain that marketers design content to guide readers step by step, using familiar phrases to push them toward a final goal. This process, called the buyer’s journey, moves people from reading an article to eventually making a purchase or taking action (Wall and Spinuzzi). When you are looking for the call to action, you do not usually even need to read thoroughly to find it. Scroll to the very bottom of the page, and see what you find! Figure 2 shows what the Lattice CTA looks like.

![A white panel from a Lattice web page that opens with a hugging-face emoji and the bold question Need a little more handholding?, followed by a sentence about Lattice’s performance reviews and a pale green button reading Schedule a Personal Tour.](assets/figure-p011-1.png)\
Figure 2: Screenshot of the Lattice call to action. The text reads “🤗 [Smiling face with open hands emoji] Need a little more handholding? Lattice helps thousands of companies (and over a million employees) conduct more effective and less biased performance reviews. We’d love to show you how.” Below is a green button that says, “Schedule a Personal Tour.”

Remember that the purpose of content marketing is to sell without selling, so the CTA at the bottom of the page will rarely read “buy it now,” which is too direct for readers who do not want to be advertised to. You will often see free options that invite you to learn more about the company. By first encouraging readers to “schedule a personal tour,” the writers hoped the readers would feel more excited about the product and eventually decide to purchase it for their company. Wall and Spinuzzi note in their research that the CTA is the single most common feature of content marketing across all genres, so perhaps this is one of the easiest ways to spot content marketing. Look for free resources, demos, or consultations. If you start noticing these subtle nudges---especially at the end of a blog or how-to guide---you’ll have a better sense of when a piece is designed to inform and when it’s really trying to sell.

# Human Development Institute at the University of Kentucky

In this last case, I present to you a YouTube video as an example of content marketing. A third student wanted to use the video “Cultural Competency in Health“ (see Figure 3, below) to develop a best practices guide for inclusive patient intake experiences in medicine. Produced by The Human Development Institute at the University of Kentucky, the video emphasizes why healthcare professionals should be mindful of the diverse lived experiences of the patients they serve. This case study is notably different from the first two, both in form and intent. While the previous examples were meant to sell a product, this last piece of content marketing from a non-profit organization aims to promote their mission and raise awareness of their cause. This is an example of what I would call a medium-quality source. The Human Development Institute doesn’t benefit monetarily from the content marketing, so I encouraged my student to think carefully about why they wanted to use this source and how to use it.

In many of my assignments, I ask students to find a variety of sources on their topics, including primary sources. Students are often drawn to YouTube and other social media sites because they are places where writers can find first-hand accounts of people’s experiences. Unlike most library resources, these accounts often reveal how individuals experience barriers or opportunities related to inclusion and access, offering perspectives that traditional academic sources may overlook. The video includes interview excerpts from people from varied backgrounds. Before explaining why this video is content marketing, I want to clarify that it is not a primary source. It’s a secondary source. Why? An uncut, full-length interview from any of the interviewees in this compilation would be a primary source, as it directly presents the speaker’s words and experiences. However, once these interviews are edited together and shaped into a narrative or argument, the video becomes a secondary source. My students often struggle with this distinction because they see the original speakers, hear their voices, and relate to their words, making it feel like a primary source. While the unedited interviews would be great for their research, the final video serves as content marketing rather than raw, primary material.

Promote. Protect.” Watch the video here: https://www.wellness4ky.org/resource/cultural-competency-in-health/.

Students might also struggle with evaluating this source for several other reasons. First, it is published by the University of Kentucky HDI Health and Wellness Program, an academic and research institution known for its expertise in disability, health equity, and inclusion. The video also features diverse firsthand perspectives from people of different races, ethnicities, genders, and abilities, providing real-world stories about bias in healthcare. Additionally, healthcare professionals share their insights about the importance of intercultural communication, reinforcing the message with expert validation. What’s more, because it is a video, it’s not immediately clear how to check the links or complete the lateral reading. Instead, the CTA is a single URL listed at the center screen at the end of the video “wellness4ky.org.”

I want to emphasize here that content marketing is not necessarily manipulative or bad, only that it’s not generally appropriate for academic research. Even though the video has excellent information, it was still likely produced by a media specialist, not a doctor or someone educated in disability studies. Excellent organizations, like the HDI, also use content marketing to promote their work. To determine if this is content marketing, I have to follow many of the same steps I demonstrated before. When you type wellness4ky.org into your browser, it takes you to the Kentucky Inclusive Health Collaborative, a part of the Human Development Institute, which focuses on inclusive opportunities and services for individuals with disabilities. Their website advertises the services they provide, including education and training for community organizations, community collaborations to raise money for accessible equipment, and consulting services for designing inclusive and accessible experiences for all users. While it’s not a bad source, it’s not an excellent one either. My best advice for students who come across video content marketing: instead of citing the video, navigate to their producer’s website and look for resources there. In this case, Kentucky Inclusive Health Collaborative cites several high-quality articles on their website, and they are connected to many experts in their field. Undoubtedly, they would be excited to share their research with student writers interested in learning more.

# Conclusion

To sum up, we have looked at three examples of content marketing and why they would not work as credible sources for our academic papers. Our first example, the FightCamp blog, introduces potentially misleading research and continuously redirects links to keep readers on the website. In our second example, the Lattice blog, we noted that the writers do not have credentials or specific expertise in the subject material. And although Lattice was not blatantly selling a product, they did want to users to “schedule a tour” with their sales team. In our final example, the Human Development Institute at the University of Kentucky, a media specialist edited a collection of interviews, which would not work as a primary source for an academic article. Additionally, it is unclear who the author of the piece is and whether they are a specific authority on the subject. To check a source to see if it’s content marketing, I offer the following strategies:

1. Follow hyperlinks to assess how sources are cited.

2. Analyze the publisher to check for a sales bias.

3. Investigate the author’s title, experience, and credentials.

4. Look for a call to action, especially at the bottom of the page.

5. When content marketing gives you a good lead for your research, follow it. It is important that students (you!) remain aware of how easily content

marketing can be mistaken for credible, research-worthy sources. This is not to say that content marketing is inherently bad; rather, it operates on different values than academic writing. When you are writing an academic paper, you want to make sure you are using sources that are reliable and transparent. Don’t be discouraged if you find yourself having to sift through a lot of content marketing to find a great source! It might mean some extra time and attention on your end, but eventually it will lead to a better paper.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

In this chapter, students learn how to identify content marketing as a part of their critical reading skills for conducting research. I typically teach this lesson as students build their annotated bibliographies for a larger research project.[^tr-1] I developed this chapter from my lecture notes, as I started realizing about three years ago how much students struggled with differentiating content marketing specifically. It is intended both to validate their challenges with this process and highlight strategies for identifying content marketing.

I scaffold this lesson by breaking it up into two or three steps. First, students learn about library databases and how to read a research article. Then, students move on to web searches. I have them start by looking for sources from non-profit and government organizations. Lastly, students search social media for first-hand accounts (primary sources) on their topics. Before students begin their web searches, I introduce them to content marketing, explaining what it is, why it’s so pervasive, and how they can differentiate it from other quality sources.

The goal of this lesson is to help students develop critical reading skills to recognize how companies create persuasive content that mimics credible sources. After students submit their annotated bibliographies, I reinforce the lesson by reviewing real examples they found, highlighting common pitfalls in distinguishing quality sources from marketing materials. As an extension activity, teachers might introduce highly cited content marketing pieces, such as the *MOZ Beginner’s Guide to SEO*, to discuss when---if ever---content marketing can be cited in academic work. This helps stu-

dents understand that content marketing exists on a spectrum and is not always easy to categorize as purely credible or unreliable.

# Reading Questions

1. The first section of the chapter explains how content marketing is designed to blend in with credible sources. Can you think of any times when you’ve encountered content that looked like an objective source but was actually promotional? How did you realize it was marketing? Why is this distinction important for research?

2. The chapter emphasizes how content marketing builds trust without making a direct sales pitch. Can you think of a time when a piece of content made you trust a company or product, even though it wasn’t explicitly trying to sell something? What tactics made it persuasive?

3. The article explains how content marketing is designed to gain readers’ trust. In your opinion, what distinguishes ethical content marketing (providing useful, well-researched information) from manipulative content marketing (misleading readers to drive sales)? Can you think of an example of each? Can marketing be ethical?

# Activities

Here are four activities that I have used in the past to teach content marketing literacy in my classes. Some of them are adaptable to online and hybrid classes, while others are more conducive to face-to-face courses.

## Spot the Content Marketing Strategy

Have students type their research questions into Google and find a webpage that isn’t obviously an advertisement but still promotes a product or service. Have them identify the strategies used to build trust and subtly persuade the reader, such as hyperlinks, testimonials, calls to action, or well-cited but biased content. The goal is to help students recognize how content marketing blends in with informational writing and how to evaluate sources critically.

### Discussion Questions

1. What signals show that this is content marketing?

2. Does the page directly or indirectly encourage a purchase or action?

3. How does the site build trust with the audience?

## Investigating Author Credibility

After students have found some web sources, have them investigate an author from an online article or blog post and assess their expertise, credentials, and affiliations. Have students rate their credibility on a 1-4 scale (1 = not credible, 4 = highly credible) and prepare a one-minute presentation explaining their evaluation. The goal is to teach students to look beyond job titles and writing style and critically examine who is behind the content.

### Discussion Questions

1. What evidence of expertise does the author provide?

2. Does the author have industry experience or relevant academic credentials?

3. Is the author affiliated with a company or organization that may introduce bias?

## Transforming Research into Content Marketing

This assignment takes 1-2 class periods and works best in groups. Have students take a peer-reviewed research article and rewrite it as a content marketing piece. Students should maintain factual accuracy but add strategies like simplified language, persuasive tone, and a call to action that make it more marketable. The goal is to help students understand how the presentation of information can shape reader perception and help them recognize subtle marketing tactics in online content. This assignment works especially well in a class like Writing for the Web or Business Writing, where you can talk about search engine optimization and sales-driven writing.

### Discussion Questions

1. What changes did you make to shift the tone from academic to persuasive?

2. How might a reader interpret the information differently in this format?

3. Does this version feel more trustworthy or more biased, and why?

## Comparing a Primary Source to Content Marketing

Have students spend 20-25 minutes finding a primary source (a blog, video, interview, social media post, etc.) and analyze how it differs from content marketing. Write a short reflection explaining why the source is not content marketing and what makes it a direct, original account rather than a persuasive or sales-driven piece. The goal is to teach students to distinguish between first-hand information (primary sources) and strategically crafted messaging (content marketing).

### Discussion Questions

1. What makes this source a primary source rather than content marketing?

2. Is influencer marketing and product placement different from content marketing?

3. Does it seek to inform, persuade, or sell?

4. What would change if this source were adapted into a marketing format?
