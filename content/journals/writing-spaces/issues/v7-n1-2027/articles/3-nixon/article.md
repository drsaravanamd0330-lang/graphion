# Part 1: In Which I Receive Some Very Negative Feedback

Let me start by telling you about the most humiliating writing experience I’ve ever had. Years ago, I was in my first semester of grad school, taking a creative writing class taught by a cranky, funny, brilliant, aging fiction writer who was one of my literary heroes. I’d applied to this school almost entirely because of him. Naturally, when it was my turn to submit a story for the class to discuss, I wrote the entire thing the night before it was due, in a caffeine-fueled burst of genius. I knew my professor would love it.

When we workshopped my story in class the next week, I had to listen to my classmates talk about it first. Although they were trying to be polite, it was obvious that none of them liked it. They asked a series of impertinent questions: *Who are these people? What is the point of this story? Why* *doesn’t anything actually happen?* I took notes on everything they said, while secretly disregarding all their advice. These people clearly weren’t capable of appreciating my art. But I knew our professor would appreciate it. He’d set them straight.

My hero, however, did not set them straight. Not only did he agree with my classmates---he seemed truly baffled by the story. Even worse, he seemed baffled that the writer of such a story could be a student in his graduate creative writing program. Feeling increasingly nauseated, I waited for the conversation to shift to the story’s strong points, but it never did. At the end of class, he returned his copy of the story with his writing all over it. Maybe he’d said something nice in his notes, something that he forgot to say in class? Maybe there was a silver lining in his comments?

I flipped to the last page, where he’d written a note summing up his thoughts, a note that described my story as a “cemetery of words.” It took me a second to process this. My literary hero was telling me that my writing was where *words went to die*. It was---and remains still---the most painful and poetic piece of writing feedback I’ve ever received.[^a1] A cemetery of words!

But that wasn’t all. When I got home, I realized that my professor had also scrawled something else along the entire margin of the third page. In all caps, he’d written a simple gut-punch of a question: “WHY AM I READING THIS???”

After an initial period of moping around my apartment, pitying myself for how much I sucked at writing, I started to think more about the question he’d posed. When I was writing the story, composing the whole thing in a glorious blaze of creativity, I’d never once worried about my readers. I was too delighted by my own details. If I ever thought of my audience, I assumed that they would automatically appreciate anything I wrote. Somehow, it had never occurred to me that a frustrated reader might ask themselves why they were reading my work at all.

My professor’s brutally honest question made me realize that I couldn’t just assume my readers’ appreciation. I had to give my audience a reason---or, ideally, multiple reasons---to keep reading my words. Every piece of writing, in any genre, I started to see, has to earn its own existence. We can’t just focus on how it feels to write something. We have to consider how it might feel to *read* it.

There’s a word for this kind of consideration, though it’s one that we don’t usually associate with writing: empathy. When a writer considers their audience’s needs and expectations, when a writer makes thoughtful choices with the reader’s experience in mind, when a writer revises and edits their work to better serve the person who’ll eventually read their words, that writer is empathizing. They’re placing themselves in the reader’s shoes, something I failed to do with my disastrous short story. And I’d argue that this writerly empathy is an essential ingredient---perhaps *the* essential ingredient---of successful writing in any genre.

So how do we do it? What does empathy actually *look like* on the page? And why is it such a challenge, even for the most attentive writers?

# Part 2: Empathy IRL vs. Empathy on the Page

When we try to empathize with a person who’s in our actual presence, we have certain advantages. For one thing---and this is a big one---we can usually see their faces and their body language. For another thing, we can listen to their words, and hear the tone that they use to say those words. Before we even learn to speak, we’re already gaining experience in the art of reading other people, learning how to make educated guesses about what they’re thinking and feeling and how our behavior might be affecting them.

The social psychologist C. Daniel Batson has noted that we tend to invoke the concept of empathy for two “quite different” questions: “How can one know what another person is thinking and feeling?” and “What leads one person to respond with sensitivity and care to the suffering of another?” (3). In other words, one element of empathy is trying to understand the internal state of other people. The second element is actually responding in sensitive and compassionate ways to other people’s struggles.

Understanding another person’s internal state, even when they’re standing right in front of you, can be a tricky process. Human beings very often say things they don’t actually feel and feel things they don’t actually say, which makes it difficult to know what’s going on in their minds. (We’ve all told someone we were doing fine when we weren’t, in fact, doing fine.) And even when someone hints at their actual internal state with their facial expressions, body language, tone of voice, or even their actual words, we might misinterpret these clues or overlook them altogether. This is the constant difficulty of understanding other people in real life.

Still, even with all these difficulties, we all know that the signals other people give us about what they’re feeling and thinking matter. They’re one of the keys to empathy. And they’re essential to effective communication.

Let’s say, for example, that you’re giving a big presentation. How can you tell if you’re boring your audience? Most of us can easily list the telltale signs: glazed expressions, drifting eye contact, decreased nodding, slumped bodies, fidgeting, yawning, sleeping, furtive looks at their devices, or, perhaps most humiliating of all, sudden movements toward the exit while you’re still talking. Likewise, other signs can tell us if we’re confusing our audience or if we’re captivating them. Puzzled looks are bad. Enthusiastic nodding, good. Laughter’s usually a pretty good sign---unless you aren’t trying to be funny. Still, all of these reactions provide crucial real-time feedback, giving us a sense of how our words are affecting our audience, nudging us to adjust our approach when necessary. Even the humiliating signs are helpful.

Writing, of course, is different. We rarely---or never---get real-time feedback on the words we write. In fact, we’re usually alone, addressing someone who isn’t there, who won’t see what we write until a future moment that we likely won’t get to witness. Whether our words bore or delight, infuriate or enlighten, we won’t know until after we’ve finished writing. If our audience thinks we’ve built a cemetery of words, we usually won’t find out until it’s too late. And often we don’t find out at all.

So what can we do about it? How can we empathize with an audience who isn’t there when we’re writing? How can we know what our readers are thinking and feeling when they’re not in front of us and when we may never even meet them? And how can we write “with sensitivity and care” to prevent our audience from suffering the trials of boredom, confusion, and meaninglessness?

The first and most important step, I’d argue, is simply being aware of your reader. Unless you’re writing something that’s just meant for yourself---notes, a diary, etc.---*someone* will be reading what you write. What do you want to *do* for that reader? What reaction do you want them to have? What can you do to make the reading experience a positive one?

The best writers are never oblivious to the needs of their audience, even if that audience is separated from them by time and space. And they understand that different audiences might require different things. They ask themselves: *Who am I actually writing this for? What will they be expecting?* *What can I do to impact them in a way that helps me achieve my purpose?* Considering these questions is a great first step for anything you’re writing---from an email to a research paper to a crime novel. Understanding our particular audience can shape what we decide to say and how we decide to say it.

Empathetic writing also requires another important kind of awareness: the awareness that any piece of writing can go wrong. This requires us to look honestly at what we’ve written and consider it through the eyes of our intended audience: Will this part bore my reader? Will that part confuse them? Does this part actually add anything? Will this part be hard to read? Will my reader be able to see how I got from *there* to *here* or do I need to make the connection clearer? Will my reader understand *why* I’m talking about this?

Empathetic writers realize what I failed to understand back when I submitted that first piece in grad school: our audience’s attention is never a given. We have to earn it from the first sentence and *keep* earning it until the last sentence. And the first step toward keeping your audience’s attention is always being aware that you might lose it.

# Part 3: Readers Over Rules

Somewhere along the line, many of us were taught that good writing means following a long list of rules. The period goes here. The comma goes there. Capitalize this word, not that word. The thesis goes at the end of your intro paragraph. Avoid passive voice. Always cite your sources.

I’m not about to tell you that rules like these don’t matter. They matter in lots of different ways. And it can be very difficult to fully get your point across if you totally ignore them or aren’t aware of them. (This paragraph would look very different if I rejected every rule of grammar, punctuation, and spelling that I’ve ever learned.) But we do ourselves and our readers a disservice when we think of writing as *nothing more* than rule-following.

Having a single-minded focus on the “rules” can make you forget what you actually wanted to say or why you wanted to say it in the first place. It can turn writing into an anxiety-ridden process, where the point isn’t self-expression or connecting with another person but simply trying to avoid making “mistakes.” And this mindset won’t help the quality of your work. As in every other activity---sports, speaking, dancing, dating---devoting all our mental energy to avoiding mistakes only holds us back and prevents us from being our best selves.

But there are also other problems with the rules-based approach to writing. What if some of the rules we’ve been taught about academic writing over the years contradict each other? What if some of the rules are arbitrary or simply misinformed? What if we find ourselves disagreeing with a particular rule? What if we can find many examples of published academic writing that breaks a particular rule? What if the rules we’ve learned enforce a narrow or even culturally biased idea of what constitutes acceptable academic writing?

We could write entire books about each of these questions. (And scholars have.) But there’s another problem with this kind of rules-based writing instruction: It leaves out the reader. I’ve certainly been guilty of this mistake as a writing instructor myself. Rules-based writing instruction makes it seem as if writing is about obediently following a set of teacher-imposed dos and don’ts, rather than about making thoughtful choices on behalf of a specific audience.[^2]

Consider the paragraph. From our earliest days of academic writing all the way to our college writing classes, many of us are taught a series of rules about paragraphs. We’re told they must begin with a topic sentence. Maybe we’re given a specific number of sentences that a paragraph must have. Maybe we’re required to structure our paragraphs according to a certain catchy acronym: ACE. PIE. MEAL. (Assertion, Citation, Explanation. Point, Illustration, Explanation. Main idea, Evidence, Analysis, Link to thesis.) Maybe we’re instructed that a paragraph needs to begin with a transition from the previous paragraph. Maybe we’re informed that a paragraph should *end* with a transition to the next paragraph.

The problem with these rules isn’t just that they sometimes contradict themselves. The problem is that they aren’t actually rules. They’re options. They’re *ways of approaching* the construction of an academic paragraph. Depending on what effect you want to have on your specific audience, these options might be useful, or they might need to be ignored. These “rules” don’t guarantee that your writing will be interesting or clear or meaningful, and rejecting them won’t automatically doom your writing, either. Despite what teachers like me might seem to suggest, these aren’t requirements written in stone on some great, ancient tablet of Writing Commandments.

The literary scholar Richard Hughes Gibson makes this point in a great piece called “Past Lives of the Paragraph.” Reflecting on the paragraph’s “curious history” over the centuries, Gibson arrives at this conclusion: “One clear takeaway from today’s history lesson is surely that there is not now, has never been, and will never be a single Platonic form of the paragraph to which all others must conform. The paragraph has evolved over time to meet a variety of practical needs and aesthetic impulses.” In other words, there’s no one-size-fits-all rule for writing paragraphs; how we write them changes according to our own aims and the needs of our readers.

You might be asking: what does this have to do with empathy? Well, Gibson urges us to think of paragraphing not as a series of strict requirements but as a form of courtesy: “Paragraphing is normally taught as a technical rather than an ethical activity, but…it can be something we undertake courteously, even lovingly, on behalf of our readers. A topic sentence, on this understanding, is a kindness rather than an eternal mandate.” Isn’t that beautiful? In this way of thinking, you don’t *have* to begin a paragraph with a topic sentence, but you might *choose* to, depending on the situation, to make things easier for your reader.

Likewise, you might choose to divide a really long paragraph into multiple paragraphs---as I did when I was revising this paragraph and the previous one---not because of any rule you learned about how many sentences are in a paragraph, but because you know that really long paragraphs can be hard to read. Why aren’t we urged to think of writing in this empathetic way more often? Reframed as an ethical and empathetic activity, academic writing becomes not only a way to discover, organize, and express your thoughts, but also a series of choices you make for the benefit of your audience.

And, as in the case of dividing a really long paragraph, many of these choices happen during revision. Instead of looking for places where you broke a “rule,” you start to read your work through your potential reader’s eyes, looking for places where they might get bored or confused, or places where they might be less than convinced by your logic or evidence. You try to imagine how it would *feel* to read your work from start to finish. (Where does it get repetitive? Where does it seem to lose momentum?) You start to see which parts are really giving your reader something and which parts might not be giving the reader much at all---or at least not enough. And so you add or cut or rewrite or reorder, for the reader’s sake.

Even small gestures in academic writing can show consideration for your reader. Beginning a paragraph with a rhetorical question. Defining a tricky term. Providing enough background information so your reader can understand and evaluate a piece of data you’re citing (“According to…”). A well-placed transition. Rephrasing a difficult passage you’ve just quoted in easier-to-understand language (“In other words…”). Telling your reader what you’re *not* arguing in order to clarify what you actually are arguing. Mixing up your sentence length. Eliminating unnecessary words. Each one of these choices can improve the reader’s experience in some small but real way.

The writing “rules” we learn in school can help us achieve this goal of serving the reader well. The important thing, though, is to remember that these rules are really just options. We can’t just follow them mindlessly. And we might decide to *reject* certain rules for the reader’s sake. (Ever hear the “rule” about never beginning a sentence with “And”? I just broke that one.) It all depends on our specific audience and how we think we can best get our point across to them. In any piece of writing meant for another person, the reader always matters more than the rules.

# Part 4: Empathetic Observations From My Students (and Scholars)

At this point, I should clarify something. Empathetic writing doesn’t mean simply trying to figure out what someone else wants us to say and leaving our own thoughts out of it. Not at all. Our own reasons for writing should be at the heart of what we’re doing. But if we want to convey *our* interests in a way that actually impacts our readers, we have to consider *their* interests, too.

When I asked one of my first year writing classes to email me their thoughts on the role empathy can play in academic writing, a film major named Jeriel offered this really insightful point: “When writing about something, you’re making two arguments: the one your paper is about and the reason why your audience should be interested in this topic as much as you. Why write something if you don’t believe it will be worth sharing to others?”

Jeriel reminds us that we can’t really engage others unless we’re engaged by our own work. In this way, choosing a topic we genuinely find interesting can be an empathetic act, since our interest is likely to rub off on our readers. But he also reminds us that this isn’t enough: we also have to actually persuade our audience that our topic is worth reading about. That’s how we avoid the dreaded “Why am I reading this?” response.

And, of course, the way you express your particular interests, passions, or perspectives will largely depend on the specific audience you’re writing for. Addressing climate change as a visiting speaker in a fifth grade class would require a different approach than writing an op-ed for your college newspaper or addressing the science of climate change in a scholarly journal. This requires us to be thoughtful about what each audience already knows, what they’ll need to know, and what they’ll want to know. Otherwise, we might not be heard at all. As the composition scholars Lisa Ede and Andrea Lunsford remind us in a classic article on the role of audience in writing: “The writer who does not consider the needs and interests of his audience risks losing that audience” (165).

We don’t want to lose our audience, but we don’t want to lose *ourselves*, either. And striking that balance is one of the trickiest parts of writing. The legendary scholar and activist bell hooks once wrote about the importance of communicating in a way that recognizes the “particularity and uniqueness” of one’s audience (11). What she’s describing isn’t just good communication---it’s empathy. But it’s important to remember that what makes hooks such a special writer herself is her ability to recognize each individual audience while also expressing her own “particularity and uniqueness.” To truly connect with others, we have to be willing to offer our distinctive perspective.

Genuine empathy also requires humility. In her book *Changing the* *Subject: A Theory of Rhetorical Empathy*, scholar and teacher Lisa Blankenship acknowledges that “empathy can be boiled down to a focus on audience and trying to best anticipate what communicative approach will be most effective,” but she suggests that it can also mean much more than that (115). In her view, empathizing with our audience might mean actually listening to them, or sharing our own personal experiences, or being willing to change our minds, rather than just trying to change the minds of others. This deeper kind of empathy “goes beyond audience analysis and considering audience and instead asks that we become vulnerable enough to consider our own motives, our blind spots, and our prejudice” (11). We can’t truly empathize with our audience if we think we’re always right.

Likewise, we can’t empathize with our audience if we’re merely trying to impress them or demonstrate our own intellectual superiority. Writing empathetically means aiming to understand our audience and be understood by them. As my student Akina observes:

> Academic writers can demonstrate empathy in their work by being less self-centered and not trying to sound smarter than everyone else every chance they get. Most readers are actually incredibly eager to understand and digest academic writing, but this becomes a dreadful task when it seems as if the writer is constantly trying to outsmart and show off to the reader. It is not an accomplishment to confuse and leave readers behind.

We all know how it feels to read academic writing that seems more intent on performing its intelligence than ensuring we understand the content. Akina captures that frustrating feeling and reminds us not to be “self-centered” academic writers. The real accomplishment---in her view and mine---is to be able to express meaningful ideas in a way that *welcomes* the reader, rather than leaving them behind, annoyed and confused.

As my student Darius puts it, “The only time you want your reader to have to reread what you’ve written is when what you said was so profound they need to hear it again.”

Which leads to one more key point: empathetic writing isn’t just easy to understand; it *says something worthwhile*. It aims to be useful, meaningful, or interesting---or, better yet, a combination of all these qualities. This is true of any genre. No reader ever wants their time wasted.

# Part 5: Complexities and Conclusions

We’ve almost reached the end of this essay, and yet there’s still so much we could say about the role of empathy in writing. And I worry that I’ve made it seem like empathizing will make it *easy* to write well. It won’t. After my professor called my story a cemetery of words and I had my empathy-related epiphany, I didn’t suddenly start writing perfect short stories. (I wish.) But I did become more aware of my readers and more thoughtful about why my work wasn’t always having the effect I intended. And that was a start.

The important thing, in the end, is *trying*. Trying to understand the needs of our specific audience. Trying to keep them interested and make the most of their time. Trying to convey our perspective with empathy and humility. Trying to use language that our reader will understand while still expressing ourselves in a way that reflects our own “particularity and uniqueness.” (In a world increasingly dominated by algorithmically-generated writing, making the effort to be yourself on the page is its own form of empathy.) The results may not be perfect, but your reader will almost always feel the effects of your effort.

Considering another person’s thoughts and feelings is always an endlessly complex task, since humans themselves are endlessly complex. You’ll never be able to determine, much less deliver, what every reader expects, needs, or wants. And even if you could, you wouldn’t always want to. Sometimes the most empathetic act might be telling someone what they don’t want to hear. (Maybe my professor’s words were actually empathetic!) Sometimes we may have to *challenge* our audience’s expectations, in order to achieve our aims and serve the reader in a different way.

And there’s one more crucial complication. Sometimes our readers may lack empathy. If your reader is not attending to your words with an open mind, that may not be your fault at all; it may be theirs. Readers can have their own biases. It’s important that we recognize this and try to be empathetic readers ourselves, embracing other writers’ varied abilities, backgrounds, perspectives, and ways of using language. Just as we need to remember that another human being will be reading our work, we also need to remember that another human being wrote whatever we’re reading. When we do both, we can help bridge the distance between ourselves and others in a world that sorely needs more mutual understanding.

Putting our thoughts and feelings into words is never a simple task. As the great novelist and essayist Virginia Woolf once observed, “We all indulge in the strange, pleasant process called thinking, but when it comes to saying, even to some one opposite, what we think, then how little we are able to convey!” (88). As Woolf knew, conveying our thoughts in writing can be especially difficult. But the challenge of the task shouldn’t prevent us from trying to express as much as we can, as well as we can. And this requires us to go beyond ourselves, fully considering---and being considerate toward---the complex human beings on the other side of the page.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

This essay urges college-level writers to consider the role that empathy can play in effective academic writing---and writing in general.[^tr-1] While students are often urged to consider their audience (i.e. think about who their target audience is), they aren’t often urged to be *considerate* toward their audience. This essay aims to help students think about the human beings that read their writing and imagine what those human beings need, want, and expect from the writer. At the same time, the essay also aims to help students think about what empathy means in general, its relevance to all kinds of communication, and how complex and difficult it can be to achieve, both on and off the page.

This essay could work well as an introduction to first year writing or college level writing in general, offering students a chance to see, early in their first semester, that their writing will not merely be evaluated in a mechanical way but will actually be *read* by a real person who has their own thoughts and feelings. It could also help students see that the purpose of a college writing class isn’t simply to teach more “rules,” but to help students make more thoughtful choices that will both serve their readers and help the students themselves achieve their own aims. In this way, the essay could be part of the essential pedagogical project of helping students embrace their agency as writers, rather than deferring to the teacher to decide what is “right” and “wrong” about their writing.

This essay could also be used as a vehicle for self-reflection and revision. It could be useful to have students read the essay and then reflect on passages or aspects of their own writing that are particularly empathetic, in their view, and where their work might lack empathy. It could also help teachers and students have a discussion about which conventions of academic writing---or English language writing in general---have a purpose that actually serves the reader and in what way. (Likewise, students and instructor might consider which conventions seem arbitrary or a product of tradition without actually aiding the reader in any way.) Finally, students and instructors might consider how breaking certain norms or expectations of the academic essay form can be an empathetic act and why.

# Discussion Questions

1. Empathy is a term that can be defined in many different ways. What does the word mean to you? Have you ever thought of the relationship between empathy and writing before?

2. Have you ever had a writing teacher (or anyone else in your life) who helped you learn to pay attention to the needs of the reader and not just the “rules” of writing? Who was it? How did they help you grow as a writer?

3. Even small moves in academic writing like defining a term or eliminating extra words can be considered empathetic acts. What are some other elements of academic writing (mentioned in the article or not) that could be considered empathetic and why? What “rules” of academic writing have you often followed for the reader’s sake? What “rules” have you broken for the reader’s sake?

4. Were there any parts of this essay that you particularly agreed with? Which ones and why? Were there any points in the essay that you’d like to challenge? What were they and why?

5. What do you think prevents writers from being aware of their audience and writing empathetically?

6. Would you consider yourself an empathetic writer? Why or why not? In what areas do you think you could grow to make your writing more reader-friendly?

# Activities

Here are a few in-class activities and discussion topics that could help students develop empathy for their audience and consider the larger complexities of empathetic communication.

## “Imagined Peer” Review

Have students “peer-review” one of their own drafts during the middle or later stages of the writing process. The student will offer comments on their own paper as an imagined reader, not as themselves. What is that imagined reader thinking while reading the student’s work? What are they enjoying about the reading experience? What are they struggling with? Which parts do they find valuable? Which parts aren’t contributing to a positive reading experience? Why? Actually putting themselves in a reader’s shoes can help them see their own work in a new, clearer way. It can also help them begin to develop the habit of imagining their reader as they write.

## What Does This Do For the Reader?

Have students look at some examples of effective writing. These examples can be in the genre that students are currently working in for your class or they can be examples in a variety of genres (from emails to magazine articles). Have students annotate the texts with one question in mind: “What does this do for the reader?” The goal is to have them notice everything that a writer is attempting to do for the reader’s sake and that effective writing often consists of a series of moves intended to serve the reader and help that reader better attend to whatever the writer has to say. Likewise, you could look at a few examples that seem to lack awareness of their audience or their audience’s needs, and consider what these examples are *not* doing for the reader. Students could also do this with their own work, comparing it to an exemplary piece of writing in the same genre.

## How Is This Empathetic?

List out---or have students list out---some of the common conventions of academic writing, from titles and abstracts to defining terms to providing context before quoting. Then have students discuss, in groups or pairs, how each of these conventions could be seen as an empathetic act. Students could also debate whether these conventions serve the reader well or don’t---or in what context the students find these conventions to be empathetic and what contexts they don’t. (For example, when is defining a term an empathetic act toward your reader? And when will it simply annoy your reader?) This activity can help students see that even academic writing is filled with gestures that are made on behalf of the reader, not simply because they’re “requirements.”

## Reflecting on Empathy and Tone

It can be instructive for students to reflect on and discuss how the tone of a piece of writing---especially a piece of writing engaged in argument---can be empathetic or the opposite. What are some stances or attitudes that a writer can convey in print that can make the reader feel understood or cared for? What are some stances or attitudes that a writer can convey in print that can make a writer feel like they’re being treated in a not fully human---or humane---way? It can be useful to have students reflect in particular on this complex question: How can we challenge a reader’s perspective and passionately advocate for a position without being (or seeming) condescending, biased, self-righteous, hypercritical, or hypocritical? And why does this matter? Is it possible to communicate empathetically while assuming a rhetorical pose in which we are perfect and all-knowing? Or is such a pose counterproductive? And if so, why?

## Empathetic Communication and Our Differences

Genuine empathy requires not just considerate writing and speaking, but effective---and compassionate---reading and listening. Students can benefit from reflecting on what it means to read or listen empathetically, what it means to fail to listen or read empathetically, and why this matters. What are some factors that prevent a reader or a listener from empathizing with the person who’s communicating with them? How can we avoid making these mistakes ourselves? Having a discussion about the divisions in our world and the difficulties of achieving empathetic communication amidst those differences can be highly valuable and instructive for our students. Having students reflect on times when they listened or read empathetically and times when they struggled to listen or read empathetically can be very meaningful as well. Note: For more on the topic of difference, compassionate listening, and the relationship between rhetoric and empathy, check out Lisa Blankenship’s *Changing the Subject: A Theory of Rhetorical Empathy.* (Utah State University Press, 2019).

## AI and Empathetic Writing

What is the relationship between empathetic writing and AI use? In what ways can AI tools help a writer serve the reader in an empathetic and considerate way? In what ways can AI use in the writing process *prevent* writers from fully empathizing with their audience? Having students discuss these questions can help them understand more fully what it means to be an empathetic writer, as well as the potential benefits and drawbacks of AI use in the writing process. If a writer completely outsources the composing of a text to AI, what effect might that have, if any, on the relationship between the writer and reader? The essay quotes Richard Hughes Gibson’s contention that paragraphing could be seen as an “ethical activity,” rather than a “technical” one. It might be useful for teachers to have students apply this distinction to AI use in the writing process as well. What constitutes ethical and unethical AI use when we’re writing an academic paper? What kind of AI use ultimately serves the reader and what kind of AI use neglects or alienates the reader? Why? We all need to consider these questions, and devoting class time to posing them could help students begin to formulate their own answers.
