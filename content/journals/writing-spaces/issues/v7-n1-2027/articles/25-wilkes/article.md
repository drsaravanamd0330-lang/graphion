When I write, I usually have my water bottle next to me. It holds about 16 ounces or 500 milliliters of water, the same amount in most single-use plastic bottles of water, the kind you buy from a vending machine on campus. When I write between ten and fifty prompts in ChatGPT on a warm day, the data center that powers ChatGPT needs the same amount of water that’s in my bottle to keep its servers from overheating (Li et al.; Ren).[^a1] Depending on what I’m doing, I might drink several bottles of water myself on warm day, and ChatGPT might “drink”

several more on my behalf. It’s not a stretch to say that when I write with GenAI I write with water.

Digital rhetoric scholar Dustin Edwards made this point before ChatGPT appeared, writing in 2020 that “water […] makes writing possible” in a digital era (67). How so? He explains: “From drafting on cloud-based platforms, conducting research on search engines, and archiving projects on file-hosting services, to queuing up writing music on streaming sites, searching for multimedia assets on cloud-based programs, and circulating projects on social media, whole processes of composing are accomplished through infrastructures that have an insatiable thirst for water” (67). If we’ve been writing with water for some time, what’s new about GenAI’s thirst?

What’s new is its scale. Sure, those other infrastructures of human writing Edwards mentioned also occur at scale, but the scale of AI, and of writing with GenAI, is even greater than that of the internet and the cloud. One way to perceive this scale is by understanding the race to build power plants to support the expansion of GenAI. Northern Virginia and Texas, where many data centers are concentrated, need “the equivalent of several large nuclear power plants to serve the new data centers planned and under construction” (Halper). These new plants are needed because the energy demand of data centers has grown exponentially in recent years. The computer chips used to power GenAI, known as graphics processing units or GPUs, use three times the energy of other chips used to power the internet and the cloud (Hao, *Empire*). As Big Tech companies make GenAI models larger, those models need more chips and thus more power. Before ChatGPT’s debut, data centers were designed to consume about 150 megawatts of power. After ChatGPT, developers are planning for new data centers to use 1,000 to 2,000 megawatts of power---an increase of up to or more than an order of magnitude in just a few years (Hao, *Empire*). So, while the internet and the cloud need plenty of power, building and operating them did not come at such a hefty energy cost as training and running GenAI does.

If several large nuclear power plants are needed for the data centers planned to power the GenAI expansion (and that’s just in Virginia), just imagine how much water they’ll need on warm days. Researchers at the University of California Riverside estimate that by 2027 the water consumption of all GenAI in the world could equal that of half of United Kingdom’s annual water consumption or 1.1-1.7 trillion gallons of drinking water (Hao, *Empire*; Li et al.). Now, consider the fact that our planet is warming rapidly, meaning more warm days every year (Shaw et al.). And finally, if you know that only a tiny percentage of water on our planet is fresh water, you’ll begin to see the dilemma. How long can we continue to write with water under these circumstances?

As someone who’s aware that not everyone has access to clean water for drinking and sanitation and that this lack of resources usually harms low-income communities and communities of color the most, I’m worried about all that extra water I use when I write with GenAI. If I want to be responsible when I write with GenAI---and I do---then I have to consider how much water and energy it uses. In other words, I have to contend with what Dustin Edwards calls “digital damage.” And I have to recall composition scholar John Duffy’s idea that when I write, I propose a relationship with my readers, and so writing means making ethical choices. With all the water and energy needed for GenAI’s data centers, I also have to think about what sort of relationship I want to have with the planet and future humans, animals, plants, and the rest of Earth’s inhabitants when I write with GenAI. Finally, following computational rhetoric scholar Annette Vee, I have to consider how tech companies take risks that benefit them and harm most everyone else, with the greatest harm often befalling the most vulnerable communities. It’s a lot to consider, but I hope you’ll find it worth your time and effort.

The fact is that whether we’re making an outline, summarizing a baffling paragraph in simpler language, shifting our writing’s tone, seeking feedback, finding sources, generating images, or getting some help with grammar and spelling when we write with GenAI, we’re using water and energy. If we’re doing these things without GenAI, we’re still using water and energy, just less of it. So, we must carefully consider when and how we use GenAI. We can choose to reduce the harm we do. We can choose to write wisely with water.

In the next sections, I discuss writing as involving ethical choices and digital damage, provide details about GenAI’s water and energy use, and discuss how you and I can write wisely with thirsty GenAI in a time of climate crisis. Answering the ethical questions raised by GenAI’s environmental impact is not easy, but on a finite planet running out of natural resources, it’s urgently necessary. I won’t live up to my commitments all the time, and you probably won’t either, but if you join me in thinking through these hard questions, we might be able to write wisely with water more often than not.

# Writing, Ethical Choices, and Digital Damage

Let’s start with ethical choices about writing. Composition scholar John Duffy, who’s written extensively about writing and ethics, states that when we write, we address other people, and doing so creates a relationship with them (31). Duffy notes that human relationships always involve ethical questions: “What kind of person do I want to be? How should I treat others? How should I live my life?” (31). He revises these questions for writers: “What kind of writer do I wish to be? What are my obligations to my readers? What effects will my words have upon others, upon my community?” (31). Although writing may not have explicitly ethical content, a relationship with a reader is always present, even if you never meet your readers. For this reason, writing always “involves making ethical choices” (31).

We can extend Duffy’s ideas to include the place of writing. Writing in digital environments often feels placeless, but you and I always write from somewhere. I’m drafting this chapter in the Mountain West where springlike temperatures are melting the snow in February. Where are you reading and writing from right now?

- How much fresh water is nearby in rivers, lakes, and aquifers?
- How clean or contaminated is that fresh water?
- How much does it cost to access clean water for drinking, cooking, and sanitation?
- Which communities struggle to affordably access clean water?
- What are the drought conditions where you are?[^2]

With the place of writing and water in mind, we can expand Duffy’s questions. The ethical questions:

- What kind of person do I want to be in a time of diminishing natural resources like water and energy that are unevenly distributed across communities and nations?
- How should I treat others and the planet?
- How should I live my life in light of the climate crisis?

The writer questions:

- What kind of writer do I wish to be, knowing that I write with water and energy?
- What are my obligations to my readers and my environment, the place where I’m located?
- What effects will my words have upon others, upon my community, upon my planet?

These are hard questions to answer. I’ve had to sit with them for a long time and be gentle with myself when I don’t live up to my commitments, which is more often than I’d like. I encourage you to be gentle with yourself, too. While these questions ask you to make judgments that inform ethical choices, your judgments of *yourself* don’t have to be harsh ones.

With these questions buzzing in our brains, let’s turn to digital damage.

Dustin Edwards coined the term digital damage to refer to “how the material infrastructures of the Internet and connected platforms and devices are tangled up with lands, waters, energies, and histories that are often unseen, unfelt, or unacknowledged in our everyday lives” (60). We don’t usually notice these infrastructures because they’re not in plain sight: miles of fiber optic cables, microprocessor chips made of minerals, nondescript buildings housing data centers that store and process our digital lives, and much more (Edwards).

That said, you may have heard about or even seen new construction for data centers, which are popping up all over the world. They’re more noticeable when they’re being built than they will be when they’re up and running because they’ll fade into the background of our lives. I was surprised to learn that Meta, the parent company of Instagram and Facebook, is spending ten billion dollars to build a data center near my hometown in northeastern Louisiana (Cohn). When it’s finished, this new data center will be the largest in the Western Hemisphere, the size of 1,700 football fields (Cohn). By comparison, the data centers housing the supercomputers used to train ChatGPT-5, the version released in 2025, were the size of over 450 football fields (Hao, *Empire*). One reason Meta chose this location, a place known more for soybean farming than for tech, is both because so much land was available and because the local power company could quickly provide massive amounts of energy for it (Cohn). Are any new data centers planned for the place you’re going to college or the place you call home? Why were those locations chosen? Who’s resisting them?

To look ahead, I’ll encourage you in a later section to consider two kinds of action you can take. The first is individual action: writing wisely with water when you use GenAI because you understand the scale of digital damage and its impacts on communities, especially vulnerable ones. The second is collective action: joining others to demand governmental and industry action to match the scale of GenAI’s digital damage. You can have both kinds of action in mind as you read the next section on digital damage because the details in it are downright scary and likely to add to any climate anxiety you’re already feeling. Knowing that the individual and collective actions you take matter can help with that anxiety. Both individually and collectively, what we do helps shape the future.

# What’s the (Digital) Damage?

Let’s dive into some specifics about digital damage. The numbers in this section are head-spinning, but we have to know the scope of the problem before we can solve it. You’ll notice lots of citations in this section, but don’t let them intimidate you. They’re here because I want you to have the receipts. The academic writing you’ll do should also come with receipts in the form of citations. You might consider reading this section twice: once for the head-spinning content and a second time for how I use citations to support my argument. You might also consider the kinds of action you can take in response to the scale of GenAI’s energy and water use.

# How Much Energy Do Data Centers Use?

Tech companies are not transparent about their water and energy use, making it difficult to know exactly how much of these resources GenAI uses (Chen; Hao, “AI”; Hao, *Empire*; Li et al.; Ren). Nonetheless, researchers have come up with ways to calculate water and energy use (Electric; Li et al.; Luccioni, Jernite, and Strubell; Shehabi et al.). GenAI is especially thirsty and energy hungry because it generates new words, images, videos, sounds, etc., using those graphics processing unit chips (Luccioni, Jernite, and Strubell). For example, asking ChatGPT something you could search for using Google consumes ten times the energy of a Google search (without an AI overview), according to multiple estimates (Electric; Goldman). And these energy-intensive GenAI searches are less accurate than search engines (Jaźwińska and Chandrasekar). Researchers do their best, but the lack of data from tech companies means researchers don’t know with certainty how much energy and water GenAI consumes compared to digital technologies already in use (Chen).

Still, their estimates are considerable. According to the International Energy Agency, by 2026 the global demand for data center energy is expected to more than double, much of it driven by AI (Electric 4). The US Department of Energy noted that energy used by data centers more than tripled between 2014 and 2023, comprising about 4% of all electricity used in the US in 2023 (Electric 2; Shehabi et al. 49, 52). It predicts that data center energy use could double or triple by 2028 to comprise 6.7-12% of electricity used in the country (Shehabi et al. 49, 52). If these estimates are outdated by the time you read this chapter, try using your research skills to find current information.

Data center energy demand is localized rather than evenly distributed. In Ireland, where data centers are concentrated, power demand from data centers is expected to comprise almost a third of the country’s electricity use by 2026 (Electric). In the US, Virginia’s electricity demand could double by 2034 due in large part to new data centers (Chen). Remember the need for “the equivalent of several large nuclear power plants” in Virginia and Texas (Halper)?

Yet it isn’t as simple as providing new power sources. The aging electric grid in the US can’t handle all this new power. Utility companies need to invest in updating the grid to keep pace with new power demand. The costs to build that new grid infrastructure won’t be covered by the Big Tech companies that benefit but rather by residential power customers: you and me (Cunningham). How much we’ll benefit from GenAI is an open question.

Historically, data center electricity demand has been offset by increased efficiencies in their operations (Electric). However, the Electric Power Research Institute notes that these gains have leveled off recently. Further efficiencies are likely, but so is greater demand. Why is this a problem? First, a phenomenon from economics known as the Jevons paradox states that increasing efficiency also increases demand because greater efficiencies drive down prices and when goods and services are cheaper, people use more of them (Rosalsky). When China’s DeepSeek debuted, a cutting-edge model built for far less than ChatGPT and other US-based models, Microsoft’s CEO posted on social media, “‘Jevons paradox strikes again! […] As AI gets more efficient and accessible, we will see its use skyrocket, turning it into a commodity we just can’t get enough of’” (qtd. in Rosalsky). This skyrocketing demand may or may not happen---it’s too early to know with certainty as I write this in 2025 (Rosalsky)---but the Jevons paradox gives us good reason to be skeptical about efficiency gains solving the problem of GenAI’s massive energy and water demands. Second, as environmentalists like David Attenborough note, you can’t have infinite growth, a requirement of capitalism, on a finite planet (Cardwell; Nogrady). In other words, increasing efficiency doesn’t solve this problem; it may make the problem worse by increasing demand in ways the planet can’t sustain. Yet tech companies tout efficiency gains in data center operations as a solution to both energy and water consumption. As a critical thinker, you can practice skepticism in response to Big Tech company claims, knowing that increasing their profits, not environmental sustainability or stewardship, is always their top priority (Hao, *Empire*).

# How Much Water Do Data Centers Use?

So much focus on GenAI’s energy consumption has meant that its water consumption has received less attention (Li et al.; Proof). Data centers use air cooling much of the time to prevent overheating, but when air temperatures are over 85 degrees, data centers turn to water for cooling through evaporation---the same way sweating on a warm day cools your body down (Hao, “AI”). Location matters: data centers located in Texas process a different number of queries per bottle of water (500 mL) compared to data centers in Illinois or another state (Li et al. 5).

To put GenAI’s water use in perspective, consider that by 2030 global demand for water is expected to exceed supply by 40%, something attributed to water mismanagement (Global 10). While GenAI can help solve this problem, GenAI contributes so much to it that the possible gains may not be worth the certain losses. This is the heart of GenAI’s environmental ethical dilemma.

Let’s look at some more numbers---don’t worry, we’re almost done. Google’s data centers consumed over 21 billion liters of water in 2022, nearly 80% of which was drinkable water (50). This is similar to the amount of fresh water used by PepsiCo (Li et al. 1), which produces Aquafina, Mountain Dew, Gatorade, etc. Google’s water consumption grew 20% from 2021 to 2022 and 17% from 2022 to 2023 (Li et al. 1). Microsoft’s water consumption grew even more in the same time: 34% from 2021 to 2022 and 22% from 2022 to 2023 (Microsoft 6). That’s 56% more water in three years just for Microsoft, which backs OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT. Researcher Shaolei Ren states that “if you view the global AI as a single company, the direct water consumption of AI would be easily the biggest beverage company in the world” (Proof 14:21-14:33).

Did you know that only three percent of the water on Earth is fresh water, and two-and-a-half of that three percent is not available for human consumption (Water Science)? This means *only half of a percent* of all water on earth is fresh water available to humans (Water Science). As Li et al. write, “If not properly addressed, AI’s water footprint can potentially become a major roadblock to sustainability and create social conflicts as freshwater resources suitable for human use are extremely limited and unevenly distributed” (1). The uneven distribution of fresh water means that some communities have enough water while other communities don’t. As you may have guessed, wealth makes a difference: communities without enough fresh water are usually poorer and may be made up mostly of people of color. Although companies like Google claim that their technologies will solve the water crisis, they continue to fall short of their own sustainability goals. For example, Google aims to replace 120% of the freshwater it consumes by 2030, but in 2022, it replaced only 6% of its consumption for that year (52).

This is a lot to think about. I have one more idea for you, then we’ll turn to what you can do.

# Technical Debt and Environmental Debt

Computational rhetoric scholar Annette Vee offers a way to understand GenAI’s technical debt that can be applied to its water and energy use. GenAI’s technical debt refers to the biases and inaccuracies in inadequately tested GenAI models that people like you and me find without being paid for our work. Yep, GenAI companies are putting us to work for free. This is something Google has done for years: when we choose the traffic lights or motorcycles from a set of images to prove we’re not robots, we’re training Google’s deep learning image networks (Hao, *Empire*). When GenAI models make something up and you report it as inaccurate, you’re working without pay for the company that made the model. As the saying goes, if you’re not paying anything for a service, you’re the product (in the form of data you produce). As a result, tech companies keep the profits and pass the losses on to everyday people (Vee).

We can extend this idea to GenAI’s environmental debt. Tech companies take risks with the finite resource of water because they do not risk running out of water themselves (they are, after all, made of very wealthy people) and they profit from deploying thirsty GenAI models. People in places without reliable access to fresh water pay the costs of tech companies’ water consumption, almost certainly leading soon to the social conflicts Li et al. mentioned. For example, the wealthier community of Chandler, Arizona, in the Phoenix metropolitan area, refused to allow tech companies to build more data centers because the economic benefits were not worth the energy and water costs (Hao, “AI”). Meanwhile, the poorer community of Goodyear, also near Phoenix, felt they had no choice but to allow data centers into their community because of the supposed economic benefits (Hao, “AI”). While you may have reliable access to fresh water now, a flood or drought could take that from you for weeks or months or even longer, as happened when Hurricane Helene hit western North Carolina in 2024 (Alexander). Obviously, poorer communities struggle more to recover from natural disasters than wealthier ones.

Regulators have the authority to mitigate this problem, but the US government has not substantially regulated GenAI (compared to the European Union and China). With deregulation a priority in 2025, we cannot count on the government to step in on its own.

# Writing Wisely with Water in a Thirsty Time

What does this mean for you as a writer negotiating demands on your time that GenAI helps you meet more efficiently? What does this mean for you as a college student who’ll enter industries where you’ll be expected to know how to use GenAI to work more effectively?[^3] What does this mean for you as a thirsty human on a planet rapidly running out of fresh water? These are difficult questions that take time to answer, so it’s okay if you don’t have answers right away or if you’re uncertain. This section should help you think through these questions by combining the twin focuses of this chapter on digital damage and writing wisely with water.

# Writing Wisely with Water as a Student and Worker

When you’re taught to write in elementary and secondary school, discussions about writing responsibly usually center on using other people’s words and ideas appropriately. Giving them credit with citations and accurately representing what they say helps you avoid plagiarism---that is, avoid harming others by stealing their work and claiming it as your own, which also harms your ethos as a writer. Giving credit is an ethical practice of writing that honors your relationship with your readers and answers the question of “what kind of writer do I want to be?” with “an honest writer who does not steal.”

We can extend this value of avoiding or reducing harm to questions about writing wisely with thirsty machines in a time of diminishing natural resources and accelerating climate crisis. How might you answer the question posed earlier: What kind of writer do I want to be, knowing that I write with water and energy?

In addition to taking a principled stance in answer to this question, you can also answer the following questions as part of determining whether GenAI is the right tool for a given writing task:

- How much time might you save by using a GenAI model to process your queries compared to using other tools like a search engine or grammar checker, asking someone whose expertise you trust, or doing the writing yourself?[^4]
- How much saved time is worth a bottle of water? Several bottles of water?
- Which writing tasks will benefit you the most if you use GenAI for them? Which writing tasks are worth collaborating with others to complete or doing on your own?
- Which writing tasks will grow your own skills as a writer the most if you do them yourself when you’re in school?

Let’s consider the last two questions with a common writing task: summary. To write an accurate, effective summary, you must carefully read the material you want to summarize, often multiple times, and expend effort, sometimes a lot of it, to understand that material well enough to restate it. In fact, if you want to really learn something, try summarizing it. The cognitive effort you expend improves your capacity to summarize, and over time, you become faster at summarizing because you’ve strengthened your mind, just as never (okay, rarely) skipping leg day at the gym strengthens your legs.

Researchers have confirmed that this practice works, noting that when people rely on automation for routine tasks and address exceptions themselves, they miss “the routine opportunities to practice their judgement and strengthen their cognitive musculature, leaving them atrophied and unprepared when the exceptions do arise” (Lee). While your short-term efficiency may improve, your critical thinking skills deteriorate over time. Further, as a GenAI task, summarizing uses a great deal of power compared to other tasks like text classification (Luccioni, Jernite, and Strubell).

Summary is a key skill not only in academic writing, where you’re working with sources you must represent fairly and accurately, but also in many other kinds of writing, like technical, business, legal, scientific, medical, and professional writing. These are the kinds of writing you’ll likely do at work, whether or not your job description includes the word “writer.” When you’re a student, you’re building your writing skills in much the same way you build muscle at the gym. In school, “the effort is the point” (Mollick).

What if you choose to use GenAI to avoid much of that effort? And what if others in your classes build their cognitive muscles while yours atrophy because you’re skipping several repetitions of this practice? Who will be most likely to get a job offer? A promotion?

# Writing Wisely with Water as a Thirsty Human

Although capitalism encourages us to think of ourselves as consumers and workers first and humans second, the fact is that we reside in thirsty human bodies first and foremost. I don’t know about you, but I drink *a lot* of liquid every day. And our planet is running out of fresh water faster than expected. One study projects that by 2025 “over half of the world’s population will reside in water-stressed areas” (Famiglietti). The study also found that several aquifers around the world are “being overly exploited,” such as California’s Central Valley and the North China Plain (Famiglietti). The result? “This disappearance of groundwater places regional and global water and food security at increasing risk” (Famiglietti). Another result of disappearing fresh water will be water refugees migrating across national borders. It’s all very scary and stressful to consider, yet it’s the reality we live in, and we still have some time to address this problem.

Individuals acting alone cannot solve this problem because of its scale. Only governments, large nonprofits, and international coalitions can address it. What individuals *can* do, though, is “require our elected officials to commit to solving our water concerns” (Famiglietti). Similarly, individuals can demand government regulation of GenAI’s water use so that human (and animal and plant) water needs supersede those of machines. Governments and economies rely on stable environmental and natural resource conditions to flourish, providing an incentive for regulating GenAI’s water use.

A wise use of thirsty GenAI could be to prepare evidence and arguments in words, images, videos, and sounds to persuade others to join you in urging governments, both in the US and abroad, to regulate GenAI’s water use. The urgency of solving this problem is undeniable, and the efficiency gains of writing with GenAI in this situation would seem to outweigh the bottles of water needed to do so. What do you think---would they?

- What wise uses of thirsty GenAI can you imagine?
- What wasteful uses of thirsty GenAI can you imagine?
- Under what circumstances would you choose to limit or avoid using GenAI as part of writing wisely with water?
- As GenAI continues to be integrated into everyday writing tools like word processors, email programs, search engines, etc., how might you go about writing wisely with water (and energy; see Michel)? How might you know how much water these applications use?
- Beyond your individual choices, what can you do to encourage others to write wisely with water?

# Demanding a Sustainable World

As it turns out, all this focus on what you can do as an individual to write wisely with water may be exactly what water- and carbon-intensive industries want, putting pressure on you rather than them. A leading scholar of AI, Kate Crawford, explains that “the term *carbon footprint*, which was popularized by British Petroleum, ‘contributed to a strategic shift away from systemic harms and industrial activities at scale toward individual accountability’” (Michel). The danger is in focusing *only* on individual accountability rather than on both individual actions and systemic harms. What you and I do individually matters because taking control of our individual actions makes us feel empowered. What if, in addition to scrutinizing your own GenAI use, you *also* put some time and effort toward collective action demanding changes from the companies that profit from using so much water and energy? People experiencing droughts in places like Oregon, England, Chile, and Uruguay have already done this, so you don’t have to figure it out on your own (Hao, “AI”; Hao, *Empire*).

Movements and groups like School Strike for Climate, Extinction Rebellion, Scientist Rebellion, and many more have harnessed collective action to call for changes from governments and industries that can do the most to mitigate environmental harm. Microsoft, Meta, Google, Amazon, and other Big Tech companies have incentives to behave recklessly as long as they profit and everyone else pays, but if many of us who use their services choose to boycott them, they can no longer profit as much. You can think carefully and talk with people who matter to you about how such action may affect you and them.

Just as digital damage happens at scale, so, too, can mitigating digital damage, which is best done with collective action. Like learning to think about your digital life as material and to consider what relationship you want with the planet, learning to take collective action takes practice---think reps at the gym but for your mind. Try revisiting the questions in the previous section with collective action in mind. How do your answers change? How might writing wisely with water mean writing with or without GenAI toward collective action?

You can do difficult things. You made it college, after all. Remember, be gentle with yourself as you consider the hard questions I’ve asked. And don’t go it alone. Talk with people who matter to you: tell them how it feels to think about this dilemma. Give yourself time and space to sit with these questions and know that your answers will probably change over time. Little changes you make add up, and as you gain the strength and stamina to tackle bigger changes, you’ll be well on your way to writing wisely with water.

# Teacher Resources

# Goals and Strategies

I wrote this essay to help students think through the environmental costs of GenAI as they face various pressures to use it extensively in their writing and research.[^tr-1] My goal is for students to sit with difficult questions about writing ethically and wisely with GenAI in a time of diminishing natural resources. Each person will have their own answers now, and those answers will likely change over time as evidence and personal experiences change, meaning this essay plants seeds of thought that will grow differently according to changing conditions. Instructors can use this essay to help students consider how they can use GenAI in environmentally responsible ways as individuals and how they can engage in collective action to demand environmental responsibility from the companies that promulgate GenAI and profit from its widespread use. One challenge associated with this essay is the fact that as GenAI is incorporated into writing technologies students use every day, especially word processors and search engines, assessing the environmental costs of individual uses of GenAI grows considerably more difficult, perhaps even impossible, and the need for collective action to demand accountability increases.

As a writing program administrator and teacher of first year writing, I’ve included an assignment on writing with GenAI in my program that contains instructions for evaluating GenAI output and critiquing it based on a range of issues it raises, including its environmental impact. Some students gravitate toward the environmental impact while others don’t, so I expect this essay to resonate differently with different students based on their priorities and life experiences. I’m not sure I’d have been called to action by this essay when I was an undergraduate because I grew up in

Louisiana where water is plentiful. However, when I moved to Idaho for my first academic job, the arid climate brought home to me the issue of water scarcity. Dustin Edwards had the reverse experience, growing up in New Mexico attuned to water issues and then attending graduate school in Florida where the abundance of water shocked him. Instructors could share their own perspectives on water based on where they’re from and where they live now. Further, some students (like some of mine at my current job in Alabama) may have had teachers who cast doubt on the reality of climate change and may have grandparents who believe that because climate change won’t affect them, it isn’t their problem. Some students may also be unwilling to question capitalism as a driver of climate change, as they’ve worked so hard to get to college so they can benefit from a capitalist system.

It’s possible that some students may respond to this chapter with an argument that goes like this: Why should I take pains to reduce my environmental impact when others around me aren’t doing the same thing, and when those others may be gaining benefits from their irresponsible use that I will miss if I choose to be responsible by limiting my use of GenAI? This question points to my modification of Duffy’s ethical questions, especially this one: What kind of person do I want to be in a time of diminishing natural resources like water and energy that are unevenly distributed across communities and nations? The fact that the benefits of using GenAI at work are still unclear muddies these ethical waters (see Monahan and Burlacu). That said, some students may choose to prioritize perceived personal gain over ethical responsibility to others and the planet, as the neoliberal capitalist order encourages them to do. Instructors might consider asking them what new information or experience would change their mind. While we can’t force our students to make ethical choices, we can encourage them to be open to new information that might change those choices over time.

This essay is also about writing technologies. While many people associate the word “technology” with digital technologies, pencils and pens are also writing technologies (Baron). They, too, come with environmental costs, though at a smaller scale than digital technologies, especially GenAI. Just as the notion of writing with water will likely be new to students, this way of thinking about writing’s materiality (as marks inscribed on surfaces that are more or less durable over time) may also be new to students. Asking students to list the writing technologies they use on a typical day and reflect on the differences they notice in their thinking and experience of writing when they use different writing technologies can illuminate writing’s materiality and lead to interesting conversations about the affordances and limitations of different writing technologies, such as pens vs. pencils, handwriting vs. typing, and paper as a surface vs. screen as a surface.

Perhaps the best way to help students connect with the issue of water and energy use is by starting locally, meaning both where their campus is located and where they’re from. The questions throughout the chapter are meant to spur thinking about ethics that can lead to wise decisions. The discussion questions and activities below are intended to help students understand what’s happening now, what the outlook is, and what they might do if they face water restrictions. Navigating ethical quandaries is such difficult work that instructors will do their students a service by sharing how they’ve approached navigating such quandaries as instructors or in other areas of life. What wise and unwise uses of GenAI do instructors have in mind? What principles, values, or criteria do instructors use to discern wise uses from unwise ones?

Finally, depending on where they’re located, students will likely have different attitudes toward collective action. If collective action has been demonized locally (“that’s communism!”), students may be less willing to consider it as an effective strategy than if a tradition of collective action is present and embraced. A political climate that chills speech may also exert great influence on students’ willingness to consider collective action.

# Discussion Questions

In addition to the other sets of questions in the chapter, instructors can prompt students with the following questions.

- While GenAI may have been banned in your high school, it’s become so commonplace that you’ve probably used it, whether for school or in your personal life. What writing tasks do you use GenAI to accomplish now? How often do you use GenAI as a writing tool? What were the results? What would you do differently in the future?
- Visit the Data Center map at https://www.datacentermap.com/and find your location. Where are data centers located near you? How many data centers are in or near your state or province? What about your city/town? Are there plans to build data centers near your campus or home? Try searching the internet to answer the last question.
- What kinds of writing technologies have you used in your life? Which writing technologies do you use most often? Which writing technologies do you prefer to use for different writing tasks? How does using different writing technologies affect how you think and how you go about writing? Make a list, and keep in mind that pencils and pens are as much writing technologies as laptops and phones.
- What kinds of collective action are you familiar with? What have collective actions accomplished in the past? How are you encouraged to think and feel about collective action by your family, friends, social media, and culture? What are your attitudes toward collective action?

# Activities

## Water Gap Presentation

Visit “A Map of the Future of Water” (Famiglietti) and/or the “World Water Map” (National Geographic, Esri, and Utrecht University). What’s the water outlook and/or water gap for your campus? What about for your home? Using the World Water Map, how might the water gap change by 2050 under different scenarios? How might the water gap affect your campus or home community? What actions might that community take to address the water gap? Working individually or in small groups, prepare a short presentation about the water outlook and/or gaps on campus or at home. As you prepare your presentation, consider which combinations of words and images will best convey the information you found to your audience.

## Water Access Zine

Researcher Shaolei Ren says he was motivated to investigate AI’s water use because for a few years when he was a child, his town in China restricted access to water to half an hour each day (Proof 2:06-2:21). If this seems far removed, consider that a portion of the Diné or Navajo Nation does not have running water in their homes or that people in Flint, Michigan, didn’t have access to clean water a decade after their water crisis began in 2014 when the city switched the water supply to the Flint River (Nelson and Brown; O’Neill). Flooding can cause a water crisis, too. After a flood caused the water treatment facility to fail in Jackson, Mississippi, in 2022, 150,000 people had no drinkable water for several weeks (O’Neill). People in western North Carolina went for several weeks without drinkable water in 2024 after Hurricane Helene broke water pipes, stirred up sediment in reservoirs, and contaminated wells (Alexander).

In small groups, discuss the following questions. If you had access to water for only half an hour a day or if you had to boil water before you could drink it or cook with it, how would your life be different? How would you use that water? What technologies would you use to write under those circumstances? Based on your group’s answers, make a zine showing how you’d live with limited access to water, then trade your zine with another group and discuss similarities and differences in the two zines.

## Collective Action Pitch and Skit

Part 1. Consider the following scenario. You’ve grown so frustrated by digital damage caused by Big Tech companies that you’ve joined an organization dedicated to demanding environmental responsibility from them. You want to bring fresh ideas to the group for collective actions it can take in pursuit of this goal. What kinds of collective actions might you suggest to the organization? Write a pitch based on your ideas and present it to the class as though they were the organization.

Part 2. Your friends have noticed the problem, too, but they’re not yet committed to collective action to try to solve it. What might you say to them to persuade them to join the organization? What might they say in response, and how would you keep trying to persuade them to consider it?

In a small group, write a skit in which group members take on different roles in this scenario. Try to make your skit as realistic as possible.
