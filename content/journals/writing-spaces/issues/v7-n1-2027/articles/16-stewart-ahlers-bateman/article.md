# Introduction

Have you noticed that you change the way you talk based on who you are talking to? Imagine that you are telling your best friend a story about what you did last weekend. What do you say? (And do you say it out loud? Maybe you’re texting?) Now imagine you’re telling the same story, but to an older person you know well. What’s different? Let’s try it one more time, thinking about how you would tell your classmates or coworkers. What’s that like?

The process of making decisions about what words you use, what details you emphasize, and how you deliver the information (verbally, via text, through emojis, etc.) highlights the fact that there isn’t one single “right” way to communicate. Texting is a good example---you probably have multiple group chats and we bet there are slight variations in what’s normal and expected in the different threads, such as whether you use punctuation or capitalization, what emojis mean, how often you add images or videos. The same kind of variation happens in Academic English---for example, in the sciences, it’s typical to use passive voice and APA style, while the humanities favor MLA and avoid passive voice. Point being, the “right” way to communicate depends on the context. And it’s not just “academic” English versus “everyday” English---there’s a ton of variety within both of those categories.

Despite all that variety, it’s common for people to believe that there’s a single version of English that’s more “professional” or “academic.” This isn’t specific to English---many languages have a variety that gets associated with “proper” speaking and writing---but we’re focusing on English because that’s the language we’re using to write this essay. The belief that one version of English is inherently better than others is what Writing Studies scholars call *standard language ideology.*

That’s what we’re going to talk about in this essay. First, we’ll define *standard language ideology* and discuss how it leads to *linguistic discrimination*. Then, we’ll present results from interviews with 18 college students, sharing their perspectives on what Academic English is and how those perspectives have been shaped by *standard language ideology*. We’ll conclude with some ideas of how teachers and students like yourself can re-imagine and re-define Academic English in a way that rejects standardization in favor of linguistic diversity.

# Standard Language Ideology

Let’s take this term one word at a time, starting at the end. According to the Oxford Dictionary (which is what Google suggested when we typed “define ideology” in the search bar), an “ideology” is a “system of ideas and ideals.” This system determines what seems “normal” to you, including what you think is a good or correct way of being in the world. Your ideology is necessarily informed by the community that raised you, which might include things like your family’s religion or the political leanings of your hometown. This ideology establishes big things like your belief system, and it informs a bunch of tiny things that make you who you are. For example, I (Mary, one of the authors) grew up in central Texas and have spent most of my adult life in southern California. These are both sunny places, so I find 70-degree weather in January perfectly normal, whereas I find snow very not normal and kind of exciting. If we apply that idea to language, then we can start thinking about the second word in the term “standard *language* ideology.” Growing up in Texas, there were certain words and phrases that were normal, like using “coke” to refer to any kind of soft drink. When I moved to California, I realized that people say “soda,” and after a couple of years I made the switch. There are other words that I can’t shake--“y’all,” in particular, is so foundational to the English I learned as a kid that I can’t stop using it even when I’m the only one around who does.

When we put “language” together with “ideology,” we get *language ideology*, which is a set of beliefs about language. These might include, for example, a belief that it is good/not good to grow up learning more than one language, or that it is better to be direct in your speaking (or better to be indirect!). If you add “standard,” you get *standard language ideology*, which is a very specific belief about language. In this ideology, there is one “best” version of English that is required to be successful in academic and professional settings. If you apply that belief to the earlier examples, it would be like saying that “y’all” and “coke” are fundamentally better than “you guys” and “soda,” so much so that you would be perceived as uneducated if you were to say “soda” instead of “coke.”

If you’re thinking, “who cares if I say soda or coke? They mean the same thing,” then you’re agreeing with linguistic researchers like Rosina Lippi-Green and April Baker-Bell who argue that there’s no one version of English that’s better than another. There are many different versions of English out there in the world---for example, think about Indian English, Sri Lankan English, and Black English. Indian and Sri Lankan English have roots in British English, but Indian English incorporates Hindi while Sri Lankan English draws on Sinhala and Tamil. Black English has roots in a range of African languages that enslaved people fused with Southern American English. These Englishes have different histories that led them to develop unique grammars, vocabularies, and pronunciations. Linguistic researchers---people who study how language works---have proven that, even though these Englishes are different, they are equally grammatical, which is to say they are equally capable of communicating ideas.

The idea that one version of English is “better” than others is nonsensical from a *linguistic* perspective. However, from a *social* perspective, that idea is very real. Lots of people believe you need to use a version of English that is “proper” in academic and professional settings. That “proper” version looks and sounds an awful lot like the language that’s “natural” or “normal” for the dominant social class, which, in the USA, would be white, middle-to-upper class, straight, cis-gendered, neurotypical, non-disabled men. A long time ago, before radio and television, Standardized English in the US was most similar to the language people used in the Northeast (because Harvard University is in Boston). Once radio arrived, it shifted to the Midwest (because Chicago was the home of many early radio stations). These days, Standardized English is most similar to the English in California (because of Hollywood). And of course, the same situation exists in other countries: in Britain it’s the BBC in London that shapes people’s perceptions of Standardized English. People who hold a *standard language* *ideology* believe that “standard English” (i.e., the variety of English affiliated with Hollywood or the BBC) is better and more capable of critical and creative thinking than other versions of English. And this is where we run into a real problem---linguistically, all varieties of English are equally capable of critical and creative thinking. Believing that one version is superior leads to *linguistic discrimination*.

Professor Naashia Mohamed, who studies language education policy at the University of Auckland, addresses linguistic discrimination in a 2024 blog post. She explains that many people express prejudice toward versions of English that they believe are “broken” or incorrect. Those prejudices can lead to “mistreatment … and acts of discrimination toward people based on their language use,” including “making assumptions about a person’s character and intelligence level.” To return to our earlier example, this would be like saying that people who say “soda” are less smart than people who say “coke.” While the soft drink example may seem trivial, the results of linguistic discrimination can be severe. Linguist Lippi-Green provides examples in her book, *English with an Accent*, detailing how linguistic discrimination has affected people’s ability to secure housing, employment, healthcare, and fair legal counsel in the US.

People who study language also show that linguistic discrimination is connected to other forms of discrimination, such as racial bias. Donald Rubin, who studied language education at the University of Georgia before he retired, provides an example. He played the same audio recording of a professor giving a lecture to two groups of college students in the Midwest. One group was shown the image of a white woman while they listened. The other group was shown the image of an Asian woman. Even though both groups heard the exact same audio recording, the “accent was perceived as more foreign and less standard in the case of the Asian instructor’s photograph” (518). In addition to demonstrating the relationship between race and language, Rubin’s research illustrates a critical point about Standardized English---it is as much about how your language is *perceived* by others as it is about how you actually use your words. This reminds us that communication has two sides---the speaker/writer and the listener/ reader. When we talk about “successful” communication, we often focus on the speaker, but the listener also has a responsibility to make an effort to understand the speaker. Making that effort involves examining your own personal assumptions and unconscious biases about what “counts” as “good” language and who you expect (or don’t expect) to be using it.

# Academic English

In college settings, *standard language ideology* makes people believe that students need to use “proper” English in their academic writing and speaking. They also believe that this standardized version of English is necessary for the kinds of thinking and learning expected in college. Some folks argue that first-year writing courses should teach students how to produce Standardized Academic English, and that teachers in all disciplines should penalize students who don’t use it. The main problem with this argument is that it creates a false relationship between the *features* of a particular language variety (grammar, punctuation, syntax, pronunciation) and the ability to think and learn and communicate. Because focusing on those features can lead to linguistic discrimination, we believe college writing courses---like the one you’re taking right now---should reject the idea of standardization. Instead of focusing on what Academic English is “supposed” to look like, we want you to learn how the *process* of reading and writing (in any variety of English!) facilitates thinking and learning.

To help us de-standardize Academic English, we interviewed 18 students at our university. If you want to learn more about who we talked to, including their racial identities, ages, and gender identities, check out the table at the end of this essay.

Our interviewees’ perspectives on Academic English included three big ideas that we’d like to share:

1. Academic English uses big words,

2. writing in Academic English means memorizing arbitrary rules, and

3. the assumed goal of Academic English is clarity, but it doesn’t always work that way.

We’re going to talk through those ideas one at a time.

# #1 - Academic English Uses Big Words

Most of the students we interviewed explained that, when they use Academic English, they have to use what Hannah called “bigger words” than they would use in their regular life. Nathalie explained: “it’s a big factor of, like, how your environment is and if you feel safe to just speak regularly? … I’m not saying [my professors] don’t make me feel safe, but I feel like I need to have a more academic vocabulary when I’m talking, especially to the whole class.” Kristi similarly said, “when I speak in class, … I definitely think about my words more than I would outside of class.” A lot of them talked about using a thesaurus to help them with this, particularly in writing.

They also noted that these bigger words can make it hard to understand what’s being said, especially in assigned readings. Mckenna explained, “I think sometimes it gets unclear when the vocabulary used is too high of a level for people to understand.” Kristi agreed: “maybe I just don’t know a lot of the words that they are using. So I have to learn all of the additional vocabulary and that’s what comes off as unclear.” The students further argued that learning and using this additional vocabulary is not really necessary because the same ideas could be expressed in regular language. As Hannah said, “you could just talk slang and get the same point across versus like adding so many extra words to say the same thing.” Kai similarly reflected, “just me talking the way I do at home doesn’t change what they understand about me, of what I’m trying to say. I’m just saying it in a way that is easier for my family to understand.”

These students feel like they need to use big words in academic spaces, but at the same time they argue that big words make things harder to understand. As college professors, we’d like to let you in on a little secret: it’s obvious to us when a student has used a thesaurus to “update” their language and, more often than not, the result is a little confusing. We highly recommend using language that *makes sense to you* in your academic speaking and writing. If you understand what you’re saying, you’re much more likely to express a complicated and interesting idea. When it comes to reading, our interviewees are right that a lot of academic articles and books use highly specialized terms. This is partly because academic communities spend a long time thinking about an idea, and they create big words in an effort to be more precise in how they describe it. The good news is that, the more you read and the more classes you take, the more familiar you’ll become with the vocabulary in your major. In the meantime, we encourage you to ask questions in class. If you’re confused, odds are that your classmates are, too.

# #2 - Writing in Academic English Means Memorizing Arbitrary Rules

In addition to big words, the students we interviewed explained that writing in Academic English involved changing their language to follow grammar and punctuation rules. Everyone we interviewed could name at least one rule that they had been required to memorize, even though they didn’t understand why the rule existed. Cecilia recalled, “I remember there was one of my teachers who was obsessed with … the little, like, comma, semicolon things. And my teacher would insist it’s semicolon-however-comma. And for the rest of my life I’m going to remember semicolon-however-comma. [laughter] And I’m like, okay, but why?” Kristi told a similar story: “my English honors teacher didn’t want to use any ‘be’ verbs. And I remember now it was because she was trying to teach us not to use passive voice. … But it didn’t teach me passive voice at all. It just taught me to use different verbs [laughter].”

Over and over, the students we talked to ended up questioning the purpose of learning these rules. As Sydney put it, “I never really thought about it … I always saw it as something, like, okay, this is what I have to do. … Now I’m like … okay, *why* do I have to do this?” Or, as Alexis shared, “I was told this is the right way to do things, so I have to do it. Not necessarily because I feel like it’s the best way to get my point across. And in fact sometimes I feel like it muddles what I’m trying actually to say.” Kristi agreed: “I don’t think there’s necessarily any, like, inherent value in [Standardized English]. Like, obviously, we can communicate without it.”

Alexis and Kristi make compelling arguments about why standardization is not necessary for---and actually can be a hindrance to---academic pursuits. We’d also like to point out that when a rule feels “arbitrary,” it’s probably because it isn’t part of your internal grammar. We all have an internal grammar that lines up with the variety of English (or other languages) we learned as kids. If you grew up speaking a variety of English that’s close to the “standard,” then you’re less likely to feel confused by the rules of Standardized English. If you’re not, then you have to put in more effort to sound “academic.” This is why *standard language ideology* is discriminatory---it benefits the dominant class and disadvantages everybody else. Now, we can’t promise that you won’t run into linguistic discrimination if you use “non-standard” grammar around someone who rocks a *standard language ideology*. But the more people who recognize standardization as discriminatory, the better chance we have of creating a world where linguistic diversity is valued and expected in academic and professional spaces.

# #3 - The Assumed Goal of Academic English Is Clarity, but It Doesn’t Always Work That Way

The students we interviewed speculated that the reason Academic English values big words and arbitrary rules is because it’s trying to achieve “clarity.” As Kai put it, “Academic English to me would probably be the sort of English you use … in a setting where in a professional sense you want it to be universally understood.” Or, as Hannah said, “it’s there, like, to get the point across.” While “clarity” might be the *intended* goal of Standardized Academic English, the students we talked to were not sure that this goal was often achieved. Alexis commented, “If you’re trying to explain something to a broad audience, shouldn’t you do it in the way … that most people can understand it? And maybe that’s why we have a Standard English, because we decided, you know, we want something that the majority of people are going to be able to understand. … But is Academic English the way of speaking and writing that does apply to the majority of people? Or is that, like, outdated?”

Alexis’ question points to the importance of *audience* in writing and communication. Audience is also key to helping us de-standardize Academic English. Being “clear” depends on who you are talking to and what you are trying to say. So, instead of trying to use language that is 100% “clear” to *everyone*, you’d be better off working to understand the academic audience you’re trying to reach. From that angle, being “clear” means saying something that your audience will find interesting and meaningful, and it might be that “non-standard” language is the best way to reach them.

# Standard Language Ideology’s Impact on Academic English

The students we interviewed *knew* that Standardized English is not necessary for communicating and thinking and learning. But they also said that they had to use it (or at least pretend to use it) in order to gain entry into the professional world. We find it helpful to think about this through the metaphor of a “passkey,” a sort of all-access currency that bypasses the need for separate passwords. The students hoped that, if they could perform Standardized English convincingly in their academic writing and speaking, then they’d be granted entry to any number of professional spaces. As Hannah explained, “nobody really likes to talk academic English … but you do it because you know it’s what you need to do. Because it’s what, you know, professionals expect from you.” Cecilia added that, in addition to using this language to please authority figures, you also use it to gain your own authority: “like when you’re trying to go into an interview … or like someone who’s kind of high up on the chain, where they’re like, ‘I have the authority.’ Do *you* have, like, the power to gain authority yourself?”

The students we talked to further explained that using Standardized English in academic or professional spaces ensures that their ideas will be taken seriously. Kai explained, “it builds up my credibility. It makes me sound like, oh yeah, they have a good idea. Let’s go with this.” Kristi agreed: “At some of my jobs, as, like, a younger person, I feel like it’s hard to necessarily gain respect. But when I send emails using that, like, academic English, people are like, ‘oh wow, like, maybe she does know what she’s talking about.’”

The passkey works because people in charge associate Standardized English with things like credibility, competence, and professionalism. So when students try to use that English at work or in school, they feel like they will be respected. But there’s a few problems.

First, not all academic and professional spaces use the same version of English. There’s differences between, for example, how nurses and software developers and psychologists communicate. So, to truly gain entry into one of those professions, you have to learn the particular type of speaking and writing that nurses or developers or psychologists expect. And this isn’t really about changing your internal grammar to match up with the community’s “arbitrary rules.” It’s more about understanding what kinds of evidence people in your community find compelling, or how they tend to organize their arguments.

Another problem with the passkey metaphor is that, even if Academic English was a singular thing that you could memorize---which it isn’t, but even if it was---it wouldn’t give all people the same access. And we bet you can guess why. That’s right: because *standard language ideology* causes people to judge others based on how they use language. And, as we saw in the example with the Asian instructor’s photograph at the beginning of this essay, that judgement doesn’t separate your words from your body. Even if you use big words and follow all the rules, you might still be perceived as “not clear” and “non-standard.”

And maybe you’ve noticed, but this *standard language ideology* puts all of the work onto people who already face discrimination. The group in charge uses a variety of English that’s pretty close to the “standard” (because they’re the ones who decided it should be the standard version in the first place). They don’t have much to change to be perceived as “professional,” while people from other communities have to change a lot and do all the work of accommodation.

# So, What Do We Do?

*Standard language ideology* is deeply ingrained in our society. As researchers and teachers who are trying to change the way people think about and respond to language, that reality can feel daunting. We can tell people that expecting “standard” language leads to linguistic discrimination, and we can invite our own students to use their real and regular languages in our classrooms. But we can’t pretend like the social expectation for Standardized English doesn’t exist. This is essentially what the students we interviewed had to say---they *know* that Standardized English isn’t necessary for thinking and learning in college, but they also don’t see a way around trying to sound “proper” as they work to enter their professional futures.

So what do we do? We think a good first step is to de-standardize Academic English. The truth is, there are millions of different ways to speak and write that use (or translate) big words, follow (or break) rules and expectations, and make a meaningful contribution to a conversation. Instead of trying to memorize or perform Standardized English, we hope you’ll think about Academic English as something you achieve through the *process* of reading and writing. The goal of most academic communication is to contribute to a conversation---you start by understanding what other people have to say about an idea, and then add your own ideas into the mix, which will prompt your audience to understand the idea in a different light, leading them to offer new insights. This process of listening before contributing and then listening again is how people in academic communities build knowledge. It’s also how you, as a college student, learn. Writing requires you to slow down and grapple with your thoughts. As you make decisions about what words to use and which ideas should come first or second, you’re thinking deeply and gaining a more complex understanding of the idea you’re writing about. And since the whole goal is to introduce ideas that prompt community members to re-think and re-imagine the topic under discussion, the more diverse, non-standard voices, the better.

Our hope for you, our readers, is that y’all will walk away from this essay with a bit more awareness of your own beliefs about language. If you’re someone who tends toward a *standard language ideology*, we hope you’ll question what it means for language to be “correct.” We’d especially encourage you to think about how the definition of “correct” changes based on the version of English you are using, as well as who your audience is and why you are trying to talk to them in the first place.

We also encourage you to think about *your* role as an audience. What do you do when you hear a variety of English that’s new or unfamiliar? How can you be an active accommodator, taking up your share of the work in the communication? One thing that might help is to remember that “accents” are just a matter of perspective---everyone has an accent. If it’s one you hear all the time, it’s easy to understand. We only struggle to understand accents that are unfamiliar. So getting to know a new variety of English just means putting in a little time and effort. This skill will serve you well in college, where the goal is to learn from and build upon perspectives that are different from your own.

If you’re someone who feels like Academic English is easier for other people, then we want to assure you that you’re not wrong. Because of power dynamics, Academic English gets associated with the “standard” language that looks and feels like the language of the dominant class, and that means some people have more exposure to that version in their childhoods and home lives. They aren’t better at writing or thinking or learning, but they have had more practice with the type of communication that gets perceived as “standard” (and therefore “academic” and “professional”). There are also lots of other parts of our identities that impact how people perceive our language, like racial and gender and sexual identity, as well as ability, neurodiversity, socioeconomic class, etc. While it’s important to acknowledge the realities of these power dynamics, we also want to remind you that, as a matter of linguistic fact, “non-standard” varieties are as equally grammatical as the standard, and equally expressive.

The other thing we’re hoping y’all will do as a result of reading this essay is to see diverse language as a *good thing*. If we reject the idea of “standard” language as the ultimate goal, we can focus more on Academic English as a linguistically diverse practice that stimulates thinking and knowledge building. And we can focus on how to help people understand one another even when they are using different varieties of English. Instead of trying to contort yourself into the shape of Standardized English (and expecting everyone around you to do the same), we’d like students and educators and professionals to recognize that there are many ways to express Academic English effectively. This is a wonderful thing! It’s what makes innovation possible.

# Teacher Resources

Hi there, writing teachers! We envision this essay being assigned early in a first-year writing course, as a way to open up conversations about language beliefs so that students can work toward combating the myth of standard language throughout the semester.[^tr-1] It might also be assigned in an upper-division writing or linguistics course as part of a unit on raciolinguistic justice.

One way into that conversation might be to invite students to examine the non-standard linguistic choices we made in drafting this essay. You might share with them that we intentionally wrote the essay in this way because we wanted to help push back against ideologies that suggest that academic-level thinking can only be expressed in a specific kind of language. One of the copyeditors picked up on this during the publishing process, writing us the following note: “as a proofreader, I routinely stopped myself from “standardizing” the English in this chapter (especially the comma splices!), because I agree with your ideological position on this issue!” The copyeditor further suggested that you might want to invite your students to hunt for “errors” and then have a conversation about the value of recognizing and checking our biases about what “counts” as “academic English.”

For additional in-class or online activities that students complete after reading this essay, we recommend adapting one of the following from the *Writing Spaces Activities & Assignments Archive:* Esposito’s improv activity, Frandsen’s map-making activity, or Krasova’s digital storytelling activity. If you’re building a full unit, we recommend putting this essay alongside one or more of the following *Writing Spaces* essays: Sanchez-Martin, Bailey et al., Tremain, Grayson, Li, and/or Sayed.

We imagine that a unit with this essay in it would culminate in a writing project that invites students to reflect on and engage with diverse language practices, such as one the following from the *Writing Spaces Activities &* *Assignments Archive:* Franklin’s Non-Standardized Grammar assignment, Chaudhuri & Bhowmik’s literacy autobiography, Hanson’s Communication Guide, and Hawkins’ GenAI assignment.

In our own courses, we plan to use this essay to facilitate a Genre Analysis assignment. Below are the instructions:

# Project Overview

For your project, you will be analyzing two sources that talk about the same topic, but in different genres and for different audiences. Your task is to (a) analyze how the two genres differ from one another and (b) analyze how the norms and expectations of each genre require the writer to communicate differently with their intended audience.

Step 1: Select Articles. Your instructor will provide you with a list of paired sources and you’ll choose one pair to analyze.

Step 2: Create an Analysis Plan. We’ll work together in class to create a list of possible genre norms and conventions to focus on in your analysis.

Step 3: Conduct a Comparative Genre Analysis. You’ll analyze the ways the authors of your two sources work within their respective genres to communicate similar information to different audiences. This will become the basis for your essay.

In the past, we have provided students with paired sources that include *Writing Spaces* articles as “student-audience” sources and blogs or podcasts or videos as “non-student-audience” sources. For example, we paired Cristina’ Sanchéz-Martín’s Writing Spaces article with Raz & Lyiscott’s podcast, and we paired Jacobson et al.’s Writing Spaces article with Veit’s *Smithsonian Magazine* article.
