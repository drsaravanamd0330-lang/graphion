# Introduction

When you need something, it always feels good to find it fast. There is no denying a sense of magic in using a search engine, especially when you are trying to save time and complete an assignment for class. With just a few keystrokes and clicks, you can find a variety of websites and databases that help you to answer your question. But have you ever stopped to ask yourself where these search results are coming from? Why do they appear in that specific order on your search results page, and how do they make their way to you across the great mys-

tery that is digital space? As a researcher, it is important that you know how search engines work, and what happens when you use them.

The first thing that we need to address is that search engines don’t work like a claw machine at an arcade. They don’t just sweep down, rummage through a collection of toys and stuffed animals, and randomly pull up a prize. When you go to the library, the books on the shelf are static objects waiting to be found. In contrast, a search engine works by “crawling” the web, saving the information it finds to an index, and using that index of information to match your search with ranked results that it determines most relevant to what you are looking for using an algorithm, or a set of rules based on your clicks and previous searches. We’ll talk more about how search engines and algorithms work later, but for now, it’s important for you to know that search engines are not as straightforward as you might think. In fact, the way these search engines are described is often misleading, as the language used to describe them gives human-like qualities to machines in a way that disconnects responsibility from technological design.

Understanding how search engines and artificial intelligence work is important because these technologies are connected to biases and have longstanding environmental impacts. It can be easy to think of technology as something objective and free from human bias, but as we will explore, technology is not neutral: it often reflects the priorities, assumptions, and biases of its creators and the data it is trained on. As digital rhetoric scholars Jason Crider, Jacob Greene, and Sean Morey point out, algorithms can work to “route, nudge, push, and prod us into particular types of interactions, motions, and behaviors,” especially if we don’t fully understand how these algorithms work, or who is behind the search results we are looking through (Crider et al 4). Additionally, while searches can sometimes feel magical, there is an often-overlooked physical impact on the environment, as each online search is powered by data centers that use large amounts of electricity and water, and emit damaging sound pollution. Although these ecological impacts may occur far away from where you live, they have longterm repercussions in a world connected by complex ecosystems.

By learning how search engines work, how they can replicate and reinforce existing biases, and how they impact our environment, you will be able to build the knowledge you need to become a more informed and engaged digital citizen.

# How Do Search Engines Work?

You may have heard the term “algorithm” thrown around a lot in reference to how search engines bring you results. To clarify, an algorithm is a stepby-step set of instructions used to solve a problem or complete a task. You are more familiar with algorithms than you might realize. Every time you open Instagram Reels or TikTok, your feed is curated based on the posts that you interact with and watch. These sites work to nudge more videos and posts toward you based on your established patterns of behavior. In some ways, an algorithm tends to work like a recipe. If you have ever cooked, or watched someone cook, you know it takes some practice to nail down the best way to serve up a new dish. Chefs are renowned not only for their skill at cooking, but also for their ability to find new ways to present foods that satisfy a variety of cravings. Just like chefs rely on recipes to match a customers’ request for a certain dish, search engines rely on algorithms to match what you type into a search bar to the content that exists on the internet.

There are three key steps in this process: crawling, indexing, and serving. The first step happens when automated software known as “crawlers” or “spiders” explore and “crawl” the internet to find new and newly updated websites using links that are present on previously crawled websites. Think of it like a giant spider web. Search engines send these digital “spiders” out to explore the web, and they report back on what they find so that this information can be saved and stored for later use. Most good recipes rely on observation, and for many chefs, finding the right recipe includes spending time exploring existing recipes, looking for new ingredient combinations, and shadowing more experienced chefs to learn from their cooking techniques. Similarly, algorithms must first spend time seeing what is available to them, so they can later use this information to match searchers with a fitting result.

When information is found and downloaded to the search engine’s larger database, that is called indexing. This index gives the search engine a library of information to pull from when you ask it for information. This information is stored and reorganized continuously so that the information the search engine is pulling from remains relevant and updated. To bring us back to our kitchen metaphor, a chef may save different recipes that they can reference later, perhaps substituting certain ingredients based on what they have available or opting for a faster cooking method. As we will later explore in more detail, search engines often omit, deliberately or accidentally, some parts of web pages or even entire web pages during the indexing process.

The final process, and the one that you are likely most familiar with as a user of technology, is called serving. As the name suggests, this is when the search engine “serves” you with several pages in response to your search. Moreover, a chef will attempt to match your order to a recipe that most closely resembles what you asked for. In a search engine, these results are presented to you in several search engine results pages, or SERPs, that give you different links to the indexed pages that the search engine has determined best match what you are looking for. However, if you are unaware of how this system works, you may become comfortable in accepting that your experience on a social media app or platform is *the* experience, when it would make more sense to consider your use of these digital spaces as one possible slice within a much larger pie that you may never see on *your specific* plate.

## Search Engine Optimization: How Search Results Find You

Now that we’ve examined how search engines work from your perspective as a searcher, let’s discuss how they work from the point of view of someone who wants their website to reach you. As you might know from personal experience, the results that appear at the top of a search engine results page are more likely to get clicked. (Think about it--when was the last time you started by going to the *fifth* page of results instead of the *first?*) Other websites are aware of this and want to make sure that their website is listed at the very top of your results page. The process of designing and writing content for websites with search engine rankings in mind is known as Search Engine Optimization or SEO for short.

There are a lot of components that go into SEO, but for now, let’s focus on what this means for you as a searcher. Take a step back into our library analogy. You can start to see that websites and online sources are not static like books in a library, but in constant motion as they compete for top rankings on your search results page. You need to understand that websites are engaging in intentional procedural rhetoric, a term first introduced by game studies scholar Ian Bogost. Intentional procedural rhetoric focuses on how a game’s rules, systems, and design convey a specific argument or message to a player (Bogost 3). Websites are built through a series of informed and researched choices to actively reach *you* through the search engine algorithm. The search engine results page is also influenced by several factors, including your search history, your location, the device you are searching on, and algorithm updates. So for example, you and I might search for the same thing, but see a search results page that puts sources in a completely different order.

You don’t have to know all the ins and outs of SEO, but you should be familiar with these general concepts as a user of search engines. SEO presents an interesting shift in the way that website developers, content creators, and writers engage in rhetoric with two audiences in mind--you, the searcher, and the search engine algorithm. Keeping this in mind while searching will hopefully encourage you to be more critical of your search engine results page and see the process of searching as more than a one-way conversation with an all-knowing, answer-giving 8-ball.

# Misleading Metaphors: The Impact of AI on Search Engines

Metaphors help us to understand our world. As cognitive linguist George Lakoff and philosopher Mark Johnson put it, “The essence of a metaphor is understanding and experiencing one kind of thing in terms of another” (5). Metaphors are more than things we use in creative writing--they actively shape our thoughts and actions. When I say that I am using a “search engine,” this title draws from your understanding of engines in other contexts. You might think of a car engine: a machine that converts fuel into motion. The word “search” might conjure up thoughts of exploring or looking around to find something specific. Thus, the very naming of the technology as a “search engine” combines our existing understanding of these two words to help us understand what the technology does. The same thing can be said about artificial intelligence. The first word, “artificial” implies that something is not natural. You might think of artificial sweeteners versus natural sweeteners. The second word, “intelligence” implies that this artificial or unnatural technology can think, learn, and adapt: something we usually associate with humans.

You or a friend may have referred to artificial intelligence (AI) as an “assistant” or a “companion,” using anthropomorphic metaphors---metaphors that give human-like characteristics to non-human objects---to make technology feel familiar or easier to understand. You are not the only one who does that: many recent news stories and research articles rely on a human-like description of new technologies (Cheng et al 2). But giving human-like qualities to algorithms and AI can be misleading, and even dangerous. When we talk about technology “thinking” and “deciding,” we ignore the fact that these systems are built and controlled by people and that they often have biases baked into their design. As Michelle Gibbons, a professor and scholar who studies the rhetoric of science, technology, and medicine points out, search rankings can act as a kind of argument: search engines decide what is valuable and what isn’t by presenting content to us in a ranked order. If we want to be informed users of these technologies, we need to see search engine algorithms and AI for what they are: not independent thinkers, but tools shaped by human decisions and designers (Berlatsky).

As technologies have continued to develop and become more complex, we often rely on anthropomorphic metaphors to relate things we don’t fully understand to things we can experience and understand as humans. While these metaphors can be helpful, they can also oversimplify, distort, or obscure the technologies we are using. For example, the expression “deep learning” implies that technology might be using a technology-version of a brain to think like you or I do. It makes it sound like the system has an advanced, layered understanding of the world when it is just recognizing and repeating existing structures.

This kind of language has real consequences. If we think of AI as intelligent or independent, we might start to treat it like an actual person rather than a tool created by humans. Some scholars, like professor of philosophy and neuroscience Ophelia Deroy, have pointed out how our anthropomorphizing of AI has led to debates over whether it should be given legal personhood. As Deroy explains, “a legal person does not need to be ontologically a person” (886). In other words, AI doesn’t have to be alive or conscious to be treated as if it has rights and responsibilities. This raises big ethical and legal questions about who is responsible when AI makes a mistake.

# Your Search Has Been Served---Now What?

The integration of AI in search engines like Google means you might now see an AI overview at the top of your search before you see a list of different website links. For example, when asked, “Is writing an important skill?” Google’s AI summary responded with a clear “yes,” along with an explanation and links to the sources it used to generate its answer.

![A Google results page for the search is writing an important skill?, with an AI Overview answer on the left and two linked sources on the right; the text is given in full below.](assets/figure-p007-1.jpg)\
Figure 1: A screenshot of the Google AI overview result for the question “Is writing an important skill?” The left side of the page displays the narrative AI summary of the search results. The right side of the page includes links to specific pages used to generate that narrative result. Photo by Katherine Mavridou-Hernandez.

This change is part of a larger trend that seeks to make the search experience more user-friendly by taking away the need to search through a results page for an answer. But as many have pointed out, having two moving parts complicates things. The first is the algorithm’s ability to find helpful web pages in the search engine’s index. The second is the ability of the AI software to use language to summarize that website in a way that is helpful and usable. The larger obstacle here is that the AI overview is completely opaque, meaning we can’t always see how it came to certain conclusions beyond certain selected links. While it is certainly tempting to accept easy and immediate answers to your questions, it is important to note that this new feature has not been without its hiccups (Ellis). The AI overview feature on Google doesn’t know the difference between reliable sources and Reddit threads, which might explain why one searcher was told to drink their urine to treat kidney stones, another was encouraged to run with scissors, and some searchers were encouraged to eat one small rock a day (Grant).

If you click on the first search result without thinking, you’re accepting the answer that was simply pushed to the top by the search engine---not necessarily the best or most accurate one. It’s important to remember that the search results you see are constantly changing. They’re influenced by things like algorithms, rankings, and what the search engine thinks you’ll like. Search engines are not magical; they’re tools that use certain rules to decide what shows up first.

# Why Do I Need to Know How Search Engines and AI Work?

# Bias in Search Algorithms and Artificial Intelligence

We often rely on technology to give us answers to our questions, seeing search engines or artificial intelligence as a way to find fast and objective answers. But it’s important to realize that search results are not as objective and fool-proof as they may seem. Algorithms, the systems that determine the order of results or the prompt generated in response to your question, are designed by humans, and humans are often biased. This can lead to algorithmic bias: systematic and repeatable errors in computer systems that lead to unfair outcomes and disproportionate privileging of one group of users over others.

As author and professor of information studies Safiya Noble points out in *Algorithms of Oppression*, “Search results, in the context of commercial advertising companies, lay the groundwork (...) for implicit bias: bias that is buttressed by advertising profits” (116). The key thing to remember is that search engines are, at the end of the day, media companies. To maximize the searcher’s perceptions of a successful search, search engines often tune their ranking algorithms to promote majority interests, which can lead to a silencing of minority voices. In terms of AI, generative AI often reflects the biases that are present in the data it uses to recognize and replicate language or visual data. If that data contains damaging stereotypes or racist and sexist ideas, then those ideas will be part of the information that builds the body of that machine’s response. Unfortunately, as digital rhetoric scholar Meredith Broussard points out, much of the information that is fed to these AI models includes racist and harmful ideas. It is not so much that AI is selecting racist results, but rather that it often mines the biased information that is already there and then presents it without the ethical discretion that might stop a human searcher from sharing something harmful.

Amit Datta, a scholar interested in how computer engineering can address algorithmic discrimination, highlighted how Google’s online advertising system displayed high-paying positions more often to men than women. Similarly, Bloomberg technology reporters Leonardo Nicoletti and Dina Bass highlighted how AI image generators rarely portray women as doctors, lawyers, or judges, and overwhelmingly portray criminals as men with dark skin (Datta et al; Nicoletti and Bass). If we rely on search engines to provide us with answers and information, and these systems can replicate bias, racism, sexism, and xenophobia, you as a searcher must be aware and critical of the answers you receive and the way you use these answers to construct your worldview.

Being critical is not the same thing as criticizing. Rather, we use the word *critical* to emphasize the importance of asking questions and looking carefully at something. You have likely had a teacher in the past mention the importance of critical thinking. Approaching your search results with a critical lens means that you do not simply accept what you are given without first considering where these results are coming from, who stands to benefit from this message, how these results are explained or validated, and how your search result aligns with or contradicts other pieces of information you have come across.

As users of technology, we must recognize that search engines are not neutral or magical. The results that appear are shaped by the algorithms built into these systems, and those algorithms can reflect societal biases---whether intentional or not. When we don’t fully understand how these algorithms work, we risk accepting biased or misleading information without questioning it. This can have real-world consequences, such as spreading hate or reinforcing harmful stereotypes. The next time you search for something, take a moment to think critically about the results you see. Why might one result be ranked higher than another? Who is the author or organization behind this piece of information? Are claims supported by evidence or citations? How was this source created, and when was it published or updated? By thinking about the rhetorical situation surrounding the search result---considering elements like the audience, purpose, and context surrounding each webpage---you can go beyond accepting search results and instead actively engage with them. Search engines are powerful tools, but understanding how they work and how they can sometimes reflect biases is an important step toward becoming a more informed and critical consumer of information online.

# Environmental Impacts of Digital Tools

Have you ever pressed “search” and wondered if your question has floated off into a magical dimension? It’s easy to think of AI and search engines as invisible helpers, but every online search has a real, physical cost. When you type in a question, massive data centers---huge buildings packed with powerful computers---go to work, processing your request and pulling information from all over the internet. These data centers require enormous amounts of electricity, much of which comes from nonrenewable sources. These data centers also generate a tremendous amount of heat that requires huge amounts of water to cool. That means every Google search and every AI-generated response has an impact on our environment. We don’t usually think about the environmental footprint of something virtual like an online search, but in a world where we all share the same planet, understanding the hidden costs of our digital habits is more important than ever.

You might have seen a baby’s wonder and surprise when someone plays peekaboo with them. Part of this is because the baby hasn’t yet developed what’s called object permanence, or the knowledge that an object or person exists even when that object or person is unseen and unheard. When something is taken out of a baby’s line of sight, that object no longer exists for them. As we grow and develop, we start to understand that something can be out of our field of view and still exist (and with this, peekaboo becomes arguably less exciting). However, you might have found that when something is removed from your immediate environment, it can also be removed from your thoughts--hence the expression “out of sight out of mind.” It’s easy to forget your laundry in a closed dryer, lose track of a deadline that you don’t write in your calendar, or buy an extra tub of butter when your old tub of butter is hiding from you in the back of your fridge. When we are working with technology, it can be easy to forget that our keyboard strokes and clicks have a real material impact and output, especially when this output is often removed from our immediate field of vision. One of the places where this impact can be physically experienced and examined is at a data center (see Figure 2).

![Photograph of a long, narrow aisle in a data center. Server racks rise on both sides, densely hung with black cables and blue and yellow connectors, and two men stand in the aisle under a ribbed metal ceiling.](assets/figure-p010-1.jpg)\

Data centers are places that store high-performance servers and networking equipment that are used to power things like search engines and artificial intelligence. As you can see in the above image, these spaces are tightly packed with wires, towering boxes of servers and routers, and blinking lights. When you search for something or interact with generative AI, you are most likely not thinking about the physical warehouses that make these interactions possible. These data centers use a tremendous amount of energy to power, cool, and maintain this digital hardware. Part of the reason for this massive energy consumption is due to the long chain of failsafes put in place to prevent system failure. This relies on a massive network of power systems like diesel generators and redundant servers ready to take over computational processes should others suddenly be unavailable.

One of the key needs of a data center is a reliable cooling system, as the energy produced by electronic equipment is released as large amounts of heat. Some data centers rely on cool air, but others pump in chilled water to keep the servers from overheating. This comes at a cost, especially at data centers in areas like Bluffdale, Utah, where residents suffered water shortages and power outages due to the nearby Utah Data Center. Steven Gonzalez Monserrate, a researcher interested in the many ways that technology impacts society, points out that this facility requires seven million gallons of water to operate every day. That same amount of water could last a family of four 58 years, assuming they use 10,000 gallons of water every 30 days (Monserrate). While you personally might not be living close to a data center and may not have felt the impacts of these facilities firsthand, the United Nations’ 2021 Emissions Gap Report predicted that by this year (2025), 50% of the world’s population will live in water-stressed areas. This has implications for agriculture, manufacturing, and public health within a much larger community outside of the areas directly impacted by data centers. That means that the water being used at a data center is water that could have been used to irrigate the fields that provide you with your daily groceries, among other things, and with increased pressure placed on these resources, you could soon find yourself in an area that has to restrict water consumption.

There is also a tremendous sonic impact: noise pollution. The impact of acoustic waste goes beyond a simple annoyance to have real physical impacts on those who live near these data centers, as well as having a larger impact on animal behavior and overall ecosystem health far away from data centers. In fact, researcher Steven Gonzales Monserrate highlights that those who live near data centers have reported significant mental and physical harm,

> Their stories are cautionary tales; they are neither uncommon nor exceptional. The acute and longitudinal physiological effects of industrial noise pollution are well-documented to include hearing loss, elevated stress hormones like cortisol, hypertension, and insomnia.

With an increase in data centers built on a global scale, there will be fewer quiet spaces in nature, pushing the habitats of animals closer to residential areas and raising the risk of extinction for endangered species. Noise pollution is sneaky because its impacts are often long lasting and build up over time. You may live in an area that you find to be quiet, but as more data centers are built, the chances that these facilities will be built near you and impact the noise levels and habitats in your area continue to rise (Monserrate).

In terms of energy consumption, a single Google search uses 0.0003kWh of energy: that’s enough energy to power a 60-watt lightbulb for 17 seconds. While one search might seem small, consider that Google processes over 3.5 billion searches each day. That is roughly equivalent to the daily electricity consumption of over 30,000 American homes. The International Energy Agency reported that data centers around the world could double energy consumption in 2026 to approximately 1,000 terawatts, which is almost equal to Japan’s *entire* energy consumption. The increased use of electricity by data centers is often passed off to local residents in the form of higher electricity bills. The fast construction of these new data centers only raises the chances of an unstable power grid as more and more energy is demanded, increasing the potential of widespread blackouts and power outages.

When we use search engines or AI tools, it’s easy to forget that these technologies rely on physical infrastructure---massive data centers filled with servers that consume enormous amounts of electricity. This is by design. Many of these data centers are built in remote locations, making their environmental impact less visible, but undeniably felt by residents who live near them. Because we don’t see the energy use firsthand, we don’t always think about the real-world costs of our digital actions. But just because we don’t see these buildings, wires, and cooling systems doesn’t mean they don’t exist. Every Google search, every AI-generated response, and every streamed video requires energy, contributing to a growing demand for electricity and resulting in an increased carbon footprint that affects our planet.

This matters because our reliance on digital technologies is only increasing, and so is the energy required to power them. As students, many of you depend on these technologies for learning, research, and communication, but we often don’t consider the environmental consequences. If we start to recognize the physical and environmental costs of our digital habits, we can make more informed choices---like reducing unnecessary searches, using energy-efficient devices, or advocating for greener technology policies. The internet might feel limitless, but its impact on the world is very real.

# Your Turn: Stepping Behind the Algorithmic Curtain

Understanding how search engines and AI-generated search results function is not just an academic exercise. It lets you better navigate the modern digital landscape that impacts every part of your life. The internet allows us to find information with a few clicks and keyboard strokes, but this fast and streamlined process does not guarantee accuracy, neutrality, or reliability. As we have explored, search engines and the move to more AI-driven summaries are shaped by algorithms that prioritize certain results based on SEO strategies, user data, and in some cases, biases within the system itself. Accepting your search results at face value means surrendering control and becoming a passive user rather than an active and engaged researcher.

While online research can provide helpful resources and information, these technologies lack transparency in their decision-making process. This lack of transparency is what many refer to as “black boxing.” When a process is too complicated to explain, or when the intricate work done by technology is hidden from view, the user is left with a simple input and output, without a detailed understanding of how that result was found (see Figure 3).

Bruno Latour, a sociologist and anthropologist who established many key theories in science and technology studies, emphasized that the black boxing of technology often occurs when a “scientific and technical work is made invisible by its own success” (304). This leads to an interesting paradox; the technologies we rely on often become the ones we understand the least. Think for a moment about the app you open most often on your phone. Do you understand how that app works, or what processes run when you interact with it? As search engines and AI get more streamlined for users, these technologies also become harder to fully examine and understand.

The danger of black-boxed technology is that it discourages critical questioning. If we are not careful, we risk allowing opaque systems to dictate what we believe to be true. By unpacking how online systems work, recognizing how they reflect human values and biases, and identifying how they impact our physical environment, you can approach digital spaces more critically and responsibly. Understanding these issues is not just about analyzing search results, it’s about becoming more informed, thoughtful users of technology in your academic and everyday lives. This means not only engaging actively with your sources but also interrogating the systems that deliver those sources. Who controls the flow of information? What voices are missing? What alternative methods might provide a fuller picture? Developing this critical awareness will not only improve the quality of your research; it will also empower you to navigate an increasingly complex digital world with confidence and discernment.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

In an era where online search tools and artificial intelligence play an increasingly central role in education and everyday life, this essay encourages students to take a step back and carefully examine the digital spaces they interact with.[^tr-1] By unpacking how search engines function, the impact of metaphors on our understanding of technology, the biases embedded in algorithms, and the environmental impacts of digital infrastructure, this piece invites students to become more reflective and critical users of technology.

This essay could be useful as an introductory piece in a lesson about digital literacy, research methods, or the ethical implications of technology. It serves as an excellent starting point for discussions on AI in the classroom, prompting students to think beyond convenience and question the source of their information. Additionally, it encourages students to reflect on their digital habits and could serve as a foundation for independent research projects where they investigate a specific aspect of search engine optimization, algorithmic bias, the anthropomorphism of technology, or the environmental impacts of technology in greater detail.

Teachers might find this essay helpful in guiding students toward more thoughtful research practices, emphasizing to students that their actions in the classroom have broader impacts and ongoing importance. It also serves as a springboard for broader discussions about digital citizenship and encourages students to engage critically and responsibly with the technologies that shape their learning and worldview.

I find these conversations about digital citizenship and informed research to be cornerstones of students’ ability to transfer course content outside of the classroom. Equipping students with these digital literacies empowers them to not just do research but to also make informed and intentional choices throughout the research process. Below, I offer several in-class activities designed to expand students’ understanding of search engines and artificial intelligence.

# Discussion Questions

1. The chapter discusses how metaphors like “artificial intelligence” shape our perception of technology, anthropomorphizing what we don’t understand to make it more human. Can you think of another example where anthropomorphic language is used to describe a specific technology or software? Does the language help potential users, or does it work to obscure what the technology does? Why might that be the case?

2. For one day, keep track of how often you use a search engine to find an answer or look something up. Do you find yourself relying on the AI overview? How might reading this piece change or influence your search habits in the future?

3. In the “Search Engine Optimization” section, this chapter explains that some websites are written to two audiences at the same time, the search engine algorithm and the human searcher. Do you ever engage with writing or creating content for two parallel audiences? What is that experience like as a writer? What challenges do you face?

4. Take a moment to conduct some research into data centers that are near your campus or your hometown. How far are they from you? Have you ever personally encountered these buildings? Did you know about data centers before this piece, and if so, where did you first learn about them?

5. A key part of this piece is focused on equipping you with the tools to be an informed and engaged researcher. What are two things you will take from this chapter into future assignments? What piece of information from this piece might you be interested in researching in greater detail?

# Activities

Below are three in-class activities I use with my students to ask them to pause and reflect while using search engines or AI programs.

## Reflecting on Search Engine Results Pages

I like to have students explore their search engine results pages beyond what they are used to, sending them on a scavenger hunt with a partner and then having them reflect on what they found. This can be built differently depending on the content of the course, but I usually have my students search for the same key term or question and then explore past the first SERP to see what the content is like on the 7th or 8th page of results.

This is best done with students searching on a variety of devices (if possible) to help vary the search results between laptops, phones, or other electronic devices. As a group, we discuss how our SERPs differ from each other and try to explain our rationale for clicking on certain links. I emphasize to students that we all start with the same goal when searching but navigate different digital spaces (if we are seeing different SERPs from our peers). First, I ask students to complete individual reflections where they are asked to justify why they selected certain web pages. Afterward, students compare their answers in small groups of 2-3 before discussing these search experiences with the whole class.

## Grounding Technology in Material Reality

Many students struggle to understand the physical output of the technology they use. Consider starting this class period by taking a look at this video that provides a detailed tour of a Google data center and a discussion of this facility’s impact on the environment (https://youtu.be/Sk-WzwoDIGmE?si=503vjy68CgqdUcIJ). This would be a great companion piece to reading the chapter as it shows the processes needed to maintain data centers in greater detail. Students could then be asked to do a deeper dive into the nearest data center to the school’s campus and investigate the different communities affected by this data center, considering how this facility impacts noise complaints, water usage, power, etc.

I often use this to cover the concepts of audience, purpose, genre, and context. Students can be put into several small groups and asked to write different pieces of correspondence from the point of view of a company that is trying to emphasize the benefits of a data center to a local community, a group of residents who are writing to oppose a new data center, a government agency who seeks to profit from the data center but seeks to balance the concerns of both groups, etc. This can help situate students within the context of a real problem that might impact them before asking them to write from different perspectives. Allowing students to pick their genre of writing can encourage them to consider genre conventions within a dynamic rhetorical situation, and explain their choices, rather than assigning them a prescribed form of writing.

## Reflecting on Black-Boxed Processes

The concept of the black box can be shifted into a more general writing context, allowing students to reflect on their writing process. It can also bring your attention as an instructor to areas of the course where students require more explanation or review. This work should start as an individual exercise to allow students to reflect on their own needs first, before coming to a large group. Students can use digital tools like Canva or PowerPoint, or paper and pencil to draw a diagram similar to Figure 1 in this piece. Have students start by labeling the “output” of the process as an assignment or a task you are working toward as a class (ex: Project 3 Argumentative Essay). Then, ask them to write out as many inputs as they can think of, with arrows going toward the box (ex: Looking at scholarly databases, Finding 6 sources). Students can then use the black box to represent the process of writing that they find most difficult to understand or model for their purposes.

For example, some students might label their black box as simply “writing my paper,” but as an instructor, the inputs can help you better understand what steps they see as important to complete before writing. Students can then share their black box diagrams with 2-3 of their peers to help discuss what steps they need to do as a part of the writing process. This can be used to prompt a discussion about how the writing process differs based on the writer’s individual process and can help students realize the pre-writing stages and the different pathways that can be taken to the final assignment in the course. Students can engage in a larger discussion about what makes the actual writing process black-boxed for them, and how they can engage with specific writing strategies to make their output more accessible and easier to understand.
