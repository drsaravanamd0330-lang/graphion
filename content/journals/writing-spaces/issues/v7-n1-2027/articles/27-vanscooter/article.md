# Introduction

Alex drops their backpack, opens their laptop, and stares at the blinking cursor on their rough draft. Before they can settle in, the professor clears her throat, drawing everyone’s attention. “I want to address something before we begin,” she says. “I know some of you use generative AI tools like ChatGPT. That’s not necessarily bad, if used wisely. But let me be clear: submitting an AI-generated essay as your own is plagiarism.”

Alex shifts, exchanging nervous glances with classmates. They’ve used AI for brainstorming and editing, but where’s the line? Is it okay to reword sentences with AI? Generate an outline? See an example of the genre? As the professor speaks, Alex pulls up their draft. They used AI to refine a few sentences and clarify their argument. Is that cheating or just a tool for improvement? They reread their work, tuning out the lecture. Is this still their voice? Is this still their writing?

Writing has always evolved with new technologies, shaping how we think about the process. The rise of AI in writing and research is no exception. Whether brainstorming, revising, or researching, AI can be a powerful tool, but it also raises key questions. How do we use it responsibly? When should we rely on it, and when should we trust our own judgment? Many students worry about plagiarism and “robotic” writing, but AI is increasingly a go-to resource for drafting emails, generating ideas, and refining grammar, even in academic settings. This chapter explores how AI can enhance writing through brainstorming, organization, revision, and accessibility while fostering a critical, ethical approach.

# Understanding AI and Its Role in Writing

Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI)-powered tools analyze language patterns to generate text, assist with organization, and refine writing. Large Language Models (LLMs) like ChatGPT predict words based on extensive training data, but they don’t “think” or understand meaning the way humans do. Writing studies researchers who were part of the MLA-CCCC Joint Task Force on Writing and AI (Adisa, et al.) stress that “while [AI tools] appear to work like magic or to think like a human, they are data-driven and machine-enabled” (5). Additionally, communications scholar Stacey Pigg found that those who had disciplinary or genre-specific knowledge about what they were trying to write with the addition of AI were more successful than those who used an AI tool without knowing the purpose of the writing. For example, one participant in Pigg’s study noted that “AI is not going to be able to write [a literature review] like a researcher who knows that field can write. It doesn’t understand the complexities.” Essentially, while AI seems human-like, it is not a person and cannot help you like a person (tutors, teachers, peer-reviewers) can. It lacks the critical thinking skills at which humans excel. Adisa et al. also discuss how the tools can give incorrect answers or what’s known as “hallucinations” (5). It’s important to remember these warnings when using AI and becoming critically AI literate.

# How Does AI Work?

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is basically a way for computers to mimic human thinking by learning from data and patterns in data rather than following step-by-step instructions for every task. It works through systems like machine learning, where programs analyze patterns in data to make decisions or predictions. Generative AI tools like ChatGPT use Large Language Models (LLMs), which are advanced systems trained on massive amounts of text. These models analyze language patterns to predict and generate coherent, human-like text. They rely on Natural Language Processing (NLP) to understand context and meaning in conversations or writing. However, researchers Byrd et al. (also a part of the MLA-CCCC Writing Task Force on Writing and AI) have underscored that these programs are not sentient, saying “Although it is often tempting to speak in terms of what an LLM is ‘doing’ or ‘intending’ or even ‘thinking,’ what we are witnessing is the production of word sequences that look like intentional human text through a process of statistical correlation” (6). Thus, we must be critical in the way we view these programs and their outputs in order to truly use them effectively.

# What Is Critical AI Literacy?

Whenever we are being critical about something, we are going deeper than just the surface understanding of what something is and how it works. Critical AI Literacy goes beyond the functional understanding to include analyzing and questioning the ethical, societal, and cultural implications of AI. Educational experts Federica Picasso et al. define “‘critical’ data and AI literacy” as “the ability to critically analyze, interpret, and evaluate data, including that which is produced by and through the use of AI” (293). Adding to this, Davy Tsz Kit Ng et al. define AI literacy as the ability to “live, learn, and work” in an increasingly AI-affected digital world. Critical thinking about AI can help you regain agency and a sense of identity in your writing---especially when using generative AI as part of your writing process. Zhaozhe Wang and Chaoran Wang add that this process involves looking at AI as a nuanced tool and having “reflective, critical conversations” about the tools (2). Whether or not you use AI tools yourself, we are living in an increasingly AI-mediated world and being AI-literate is an asset no matter what.

To become critical of AI, ask the following questions before using an AI tool:

- Who made this AI, and what is it trying to do?
- What might be unfair or wrong in what it creates, and why?
- How can I use AI in a fair and responsible way?

We want to practice critical AI literacy because it encourages us to be thoughtful users of AI programs but also smarter users of these tools. This literacy can help you make more informed decisions about when and how to use AI not just in class but also when you’re on the job market and in your future career.

While using AI, the first questions you might ask after it spits out its first output are “Is this accurate and reliable information? Does this reflect a wide range of perspectives?” If you use an AI tool for writing a research paper and you need help finding or citing sources, you’ll want to check that the sources it’s given you are real. I myself have asked ChatGPT before for help finding sources on a particular topic only to find that these magically perfect citations it’s given me were completely fictional! (This then led to extreme frustration and dejection on my part.)

As you continue to use the tool, you’ll want to check yourself for usage. Ask yourself, “Am I relying on this AI too much? Is this affecting my learning journey and creativity?” If you find yourself over-relying on the program, you may want to stop.

Overall, it’s important to understand your role as a user because your inputs can affect others’ outputs. You are helping guide the AI, which trains on your input, just as it’s guiding you. If we use it too uncritically, we can certainly perpetuate harm.

# Benefits of AI Use

With a working knowledge of AI, we can now figure out where and when to use it. One thing to note: you do not have to use AI to be a good writer or researcher. Remember, it’s just a tool to help you; however, these skills may become a necessity as you get closer to the job market. The demand for AI skills is rapidly increasing across a wide range of jobs beyond traditional tech roles. Research economists Paul Mohnen and David Lee found that because AI capabilities are so diverse, jobs in fields like science, business, finance, and even writing are starting to require some knowledge of AI. To strengthen your AI literacy, it’s important to understand where AI shines and where it struggles. Some argue that understanding how to effectively prompt the tool is the first step to using it effectively (Knoth et al.).

Prompt engineering is the practice of writing AI prompts in a way that guides the tool toward the most useful response. It’s a thoughtful, deliberate process of asking clear questions and knowing the kind of answer you want. In this section, you’ll see examples labeled poor, okay, and good, where the main difference comes down to how precise and specific your guidance is. “Poor” prompts often result in vague, generic responses. “Okay” prompts give structure but may leave too much ambiguity. “Good” prompts provide clear expectations, specify key elements, and ensure AI-generated content aligns with academic standards.

# Benefit #1. Research Planning Help

Overcoming writer’s block can be one of the biggest hurdles in academic writing. AI can help by generating research questions, summarizing articles, or offering topic suggestions. Sometimes, the suggestions AI gives, no matter how good your prompt, still aren’t great, but the process itself can help defeat that dreaded blank page that stares at the writer like Theseus facing the Minotaur’s maze---daunting until you find a thread to follow.

- Poor Prompt: *I like advertising, gender, and economics. Give me research ideas.*

    - This prompt is far too vague. It doesn’t tell the AI tool what kind of assignment you’re doing, what angle you’re interested in, or how you want to use the information. The AI will likely give you overly broad or irrelevant ideas that don’t help you make progress.

- Okay Prompt: *I need a research topic on advertising and gender for a paper.*

    - This prompt gives the AI tool more structure by naming the topic and general goal of the research topic, but it’s still not specific enough about your focus or goals. The AI might produce generic ideas that don’t connect to your actual interests or research needs.

- Good Prompt: *For a literature review, I need a research question on advertising, gender, and globalization. I’m interested in how global ad campaigns shape gender roles. Can you refine this topic?*
    - This prompt is clear and specific. It tells the AI tool the type of assignment, your main topics, and your angle of interest, while asking for help refining the focus. This gives the AI tool enough context to produce tailored, relevant ideas you can build on.

# Benefit #2. Organizing and Outlining

AI can also help with structuring an essay, but you should make sure its suggestions align with your assignment guidelines, academic integrity policies, and disciplinary expectations. Always review and adapt AI-generated outlines or structures so you can reflect your own thinking and meet the standards of your course.

- Poor Prompt: *Make me an outline about advertising and gender.*

    - This prompt is too vague and lacks assignment details, leading to a generic AI response. This kind of prompt also gives AI too much control; because the AI has to guess everything, it may produce a pre-fabricated outline that tempts you to just copy it.

- Okay Prompt: *Create an outline for a literature review on how advertising influences gender roles.*

    - This prompt is more structured but still doesn’t specify subtopics or organization, leaving the AI’s response unfocused.

- Good Prompt: *I’m working on a literature review on gender in advertising. My working research question is: [insert your question]. Based on this, suggest a possible outline I could adapt. Include ideas for what could go in each section, possible subtopics (like historical trends, media framing, cultural differences), and questions I could research for each part. Please keep it flexible so I can make my own changes.*

    - This keeps the AI as a brainstorming partner, not a one-click “write my paper” engine. You provide the research question up front (i.e., the main thinking), the AI gives content ideas and research questions, not just headings, and this prompt explicitly signals “adapt this”, framing the output as a draft to revise.

# Benefit #3. Time Management

AI tools can help you break large projects into manageable steps, track progress, and set reminders. As someone with ADHD, this is especially helpful for me when I’m trying to get project done in a timely manner. Productivity apps like Notion and Trello assist with scheduling, while AI-powered timers encourage focus through methods like the Pomodoro technique.

- Poor Prompt: *How can AI help me with my research paper?*

    - This is too vague. Without specifics, AI will likely give generic advice instead of a realistic, actionable plan.

- Okay Prompt: *Can you create a three-week schedule for writing an 8-10 page research paper?*

    - This is more structured but still leaves out important details like specific tasks, deadlines, and personal workflow preferences.

- Good Prompt: *I have an 8-10 page research paper due in three weeks. I need a schedule that includes research, outlining, drafting, revising, and finalizing. Break tasks into daily goals, ensuring I stay on track without last-minute stress.*

    - This gives the AI clear expectations such as a timeline, task breakdown, and end goal, so it can create a practical, tailored schedule you can adapt to your own needs.

Expert Tip: Always review the AI-generated schedules to make sure they fit your other deadlines, class commitments, and personal working style! The tools may not take into account your own working ebbs and flows. It’s okay to take a suggested work schedule and flex it to best suit your needs.

# Benefit #4. Revision Help

AI tools like Grammarly or built-in word processor editors can improve grammar, clarity, and style. However, they often prioritize standardized academic English, which can unintentionally erase linguistic diversity or personal voice. While these tools are useful for polishing surface-level errors, writers should critically evaluate each suggestion to ensure they preserve their tone, style, and intended meaning, especially when working in dialects, informal registers, or multilingual contexts.

- Poor Prompt: *Can you check my paper for errors?*

    - Too broad. This leaves the AI tool free to overcorrect, often prioritizing formal academic English without considering your style, meaning, or rhetorical goals.

- Okay Prompt: *Can you review my paper for grammar, clarity, and conciseness?*
    - While more structured, it still doesn’t ask the AI tool to consider potential bias in its suggestions or to preserve your personal voice.

- Good Prompt: *Review my paper for grammar, clarity, and conciseness while respecting diverse language styles. Do not flag dialectical choices, informal phrasing, or nonstandard English unless they interfere with readability. Highlight any suggested changes that may erase my unique writing voice.*

    - This prompt balances technical improvement with voice preservation. It gives the AI clear parameters, reducing the risk of reinforcing linguistic bias or erasing the individuality of your writing.

# Benefit #5. Accessibility

AI-driven accessibility tools, such as voice-to-text, text-to-speech, screen readers, and AI-powered summarizers, can support writers with disabilities as well as those who simply prefer speaking over typing or listening over reading. While many of these tools (like Dragon NaturallySpeaking or Kurzweil 3000) have existed for decades, generative AI has expanded their capabilities, making them more accurate, adaptive, and integrated into everyday writing workflows.

In composition studies, scholars like Cynthia Selfe and Kathleen Blake Yancey have long emphasized the importance of multimodal composing and universal design for learning (UDL), which advocates for offering multiple pathways to access and produce texts. AI tools fit within this framework by reducing physical, cognitive, and linguistic barriers to participation in writing. For example, voice-to-text dictation can help writers with mobility impairments or chronic pain contribute more easily to drafting. AI-enhanced text-to-speech can assist students with dyslexia, ADHD, or visual impairments in reviewing and revising their work by hearing it read aloud. Even real-time captioning and transcription tools make collaborative writing spaces more inclusive for deaf and hard-of-hearing students.

Generative AI also opens new possibilities for access to rhetorical participation. It can help create alternative modes of scholarly work, such as AI-assisted visual rhetoric projects, presentation scripts, or accessible multimodal content, particularly valuable in fields like communication, design, and the arts, where meaning is made through multiple channels. However, as composition researchers like M. Remi Yergeau and Jay Dolmage have argued, accessibility is not just about providing tools. It’s about reshaping rhetorical environments to value diverse ways of composing. While AI can be a powerful assistive resource, it must be used critically to avoid reinforcing ableist assumptions about what “good writing” looks or sounds like. Prompt engineering can also help in getting the AI tool to help you as effectively as possible.

- Poor Prompt: *Read my paper out loud to me.*
    - Too vague. The AI tool may simply read the paper without allowing you to control pace, emphasis, or pronunciation, making it less useful for revision or comprehension.

- Okay Prompt: *Read my paper out loud slowly so I can check for errors.*
    - While this is a bit more useful, it’s still limited since it focuses only on catching surface-level errors rather than broader accessibility needs.

- Good Prompt: *Read my paper out loud slowly, emphasizing each sentence ending. Highlight words you mispronounce so I can check them and pause between paragraphs so I have time to take notes.*
    - This gives the AI clear, specific instructions that make the reading experience more interactive, accessible, and tailored to your workflow.

As with all prompts, know what you need and be as specific as possible. Give AI clear context, like your goals, timeline, or accessibility needs, so it can give suggestions that work for you. Always check the results to make sure they fit your style, needs, and the assignment.

# Cautions for AI Use

While AI can support writing, misusing it can hinder growth and academic integrity. Scholars highlight risks such as disengagement from writing, over-reliance on AI, mistrust due to unreliable detection tools, linguistic bias favoring standardized English, and inequitable access due to cost barriers. In writing studies, there is a movement called “Refusing GenAI,” started officially via WordPress by Jennifer Sano-Franchini at West Virginia University, Megan McIntyre, and Maggie Fernandes, both at the University of Arkansas. Here, they offer refusal as a clear “disciplinary stance” and give ten reasons for why they refuse the use of AI in writing studies (“Refusing Generative AI in Writing Studies”). In her 2025 keynote talk at the Conference on College Composition and Communication (CCCC), Sano-Franchini shared an example of using ChatGPT. She gave it a line she had written, and ChatGPT changed her wording to make her sound less convicted. Her original statement was that “linguistic homogenization… is accelerated and advanced by GenAI,” but ChatGPT altered it to say “linguistic homogenization, which *can* be accelerated by GenAI” (emphasis added). This was only a small example of how AI can change not only one’s voice but also one’s true meaning. However, this also underscores the importance of carefully reading and considering the output an AI tool generates.

As composition scholars have long argued, writing is not simply a product but a recursive process of invention, reflection, and revision (Yancey), and outsourcing too much of this process to AI risks sidelining the development of rhetorical awareness. Some other common issues with AI include loss of one’s writerly voice, fake information, and biased outputs. Using AI early in your writing journey can weaken your ability to develop a unique style. AI often generates neutral, formulaic text that lacks nuance, emotion, and individuality. Revising AI-generated drafts with your own voice, perspective, and passion is key. If an AI-suggested revision doesn’t sound like you, rewrite it to restore authenticity. AI should be a brainstorming partner, not a replacement for your writing.

AI can also produce incorrect, outdated, or entirely fabricated information (called “hallucinations”). This happens because generative AI, like ChatGPT, predicts text based on patterns in its training data without fully understanding the context. Elena Cotos’ research on automated writing evaluation (AWE) tools shows that although these tools provide seemingly confident feedback, they can offer misleading or surface-level suggestions that don’t attend to deeper rhetorical concerns. Recognizing hallucinations is crucial for ensuring academic integrity, critical thinking, and ethical AI use, as it helps prevent the spread of misinformation. Make sure to fact-check all information and use multiple sources to identify any misinformation.

Finally, AI systems are trained on tons of datasets that have been collected from the internet and other sources, often containing subjective and biased information. This can result in the statistical model perpetuating harmful stereotypes, misinformation, and one-sided perspectives. Digital rhetoric scholars, such as Safiya Umoja Noble, have shown how algorithms often encode and amplify social inequalities, meaning that “neutral” outputs can still reinforce bias. To avoid bias in AI use, do the work to understand signs of biased language, stereotypes, and one-sided arguments. Also, be sure to watch out for oversimplification of complex ideas, as they could lead to misinformation and a lack of nuance. It’s best practice to always cross-check with other sources, aiming for diverse perspectives to keep your own writing from becoming too one-dimensional. Finally, of course, use critical AI literacy, which values critical thinking to assess AI-generated content.

# Being Ethical about AI Use in Academic Writing

You can use AI responsibly in your research and writing by approaching it critically and following strategies to avoid plagiarism. First, always know your institution’s AI policies. Colleges vary widely, and some require acknowledgment of AI use, while others prohibit generative tools altogether. If you are unsure, it is best to check with your professor.

Second, remember that AI is a tool for writing, not a substitute. It can help brainstorm ideas, outline essays, or rephrase sentences, but it should never be used as a shortcut for actual writing, especially because it may produce biased or even false information. It is not an intelligent being---it is a statistical model---so always double-check AI outputs and think of it as a simple tool you control. AI may also be biased or even untrue. So, always double-check AI outputs, and again, think of it as a simple tool you wield.

Third, cite AI when you use its text. If you directly use AI-generated ideas, summaries, or phrasing, you must cite it like any other source. Remember, that the format for citing may differ across different classes and institutions. Citation styles such as MLA and APA now offer guidelines for AI attribution. For example, in MLA, if you were to use specific wording from an AI output, your citation would look like this (with “Text” replaced with your actual word-for-word prompt):

“Text” prompt. ChatGPT, [Date] version, OpenAI, [Date Used], chat. openai.com/chat

Finally, avoid over-reliance on AI and focus on the skill you are learning. If you use AI in a project, it should strengthen---not replace---your learning of a skill. Dependence on AI can weaken your critical thinking and writing abilities. Instead of copying AI-generated text, use it as inspiration to develop your own style and express ideas in your own voice.

# Critical AI Thinking in Action

AI tools can serve as powerful aids in the writing process, helping you refine your arguments, improve organization, and enhance clarity. However, they often need to be reviewed, revised, and edited by the user with a clear understanding of what you want the output to look like.

Below are examples of how AI-assisted revisions have strengthened a Critical AI thinker’s work versus how they have wounded a passive AI user’s work.

# Example 1: Refining a Thesis Statement

Passive AI User, Ryan is working on an argumentative essay but is unsure how to phrase his thesis. He inputs a vague prompt “Write me a thesis about social media” into an AI tool and copies the first response without reviewing it: “Social media has both positive and negative effects on mental health, making it a complex issue that affects different people in different ways.” Ryan barely reads it as he throws it into his paper. However, because the AI-generated thesis is too broad and noncommittal, the teacher grades Ryan harshly as the thesis fails to take a clear stance and lacks specificity, making it difficult to develop a strong argument.

Meanwhile, Aisha, a Critical AI Writer, is drafting a literature review essay on the effects of social media. She starts with her broad thesis: “Social media is bad for mental health because it makes people feel anxious and depressed. People are always comparing themselves, bullying each other, and don’t interact as much because they’re constantly on their phones.” She asks an AI tool to help refine her statement for clarity and professionalism. The AI revises the thesis to: “Excessive social media use negatively impacts mental health by increasing anxiety and depression through comparison, cyberbullying, and constant exposure to curated content.”

While it sounds good, it doesn’t feel right, and Aisha remembers that her teacher told her that a literature review paper thesis needs clear signposting too. She revises her thesis to: “In this paper, I review the dangers of excessive social media use on one’s mental health, focusing on the unrealistic social comparisons, the perpetuation of cyberbullying, and the diminishing of in-person social interactions through constant exposure to curated content.” Aisha’s revised thesis is more precise and nuanced, avoiding an oversimplified claim and incorporating specific contributing factors, and, most importantly, feels like her.

# Example 2: Improving Transitions

Passive AI User, Soph, working on the same topic, realizes their transitions are weak. They copy an AI-generated sentence without modification: “The Industrial Revolution was a significant event. Consequently, people started moving to cities, and this had an impact on their lives.” The AI-generated transition is vague and repetitive, adding little value to the sentence, but because Soph didn’t critically evaluate the output, their writing remains unclear and lacks depth.

At the same time, Critical AI Writer, Daniel, is writing a history paper on the Industrial Revolution. His initial draft includes choppy sentences such as, “The Industrial Revolution changed how goods were produced. Many workers moved to cities to find jobs.” Recognizing the abrupt transition, he uses AI to generate transition suggestions. The first option is “Due to these advancements, many workers moved to cities in search of jobs.” Daniel likes that it connects the two ideas, but realizes that the phrasing “these advancements” is vague; it doesn’t specify what changed. The second suggestion, “With factories growing in number, cities saw an influx of workers looking for employment,” adds context but feels indirect and overly wordy. After reviewing multiple options, he revises the paragraph: “The Industrial Revolution not only transformed production methods but also reshaped urban demographics, as many workers relocated to cities in search of employment.”

# Scenario Reflection Activity

Now it’s your turn to be a Critical AI Writer! Read the following scenarios and reflect on how you would respond.

# Scenario 1: AI and Unintended Bias in Research

You’re working on a research paper that involves analyzing large datasets on social media trends. To expedite the process, after reading the articles, you use an AI tool to help you comb through the social media research and generate insights based on these trends. However, when reviewing the AI-generated summary, you notice that certain voices and perspectives (e.g., from minority communities) are consistently underrepresented or misrepresented, while mainstream views are overrepresented.

Reflection Prompt: *How might the AI tool have introduced bias in this case?* *What ethical considerations should you keep in mind when using AI to analyze* *and summarize research? How would you adjust your research process to ensure* *more balanced and inclusive representation in your work?*

# Scenario 2: AI-Generated Content and Plagiarism Concerns

For your upcoming essay, you use ChatGPT to help you generate a rough draft of your introduction and thesis statement. While ChatGPT gives you some great ideas, you decide to submit the content without much modification. Later, your professor informs you that your submission has been flagged for possible plagiarism, as the text you submitted closely resembles another AI-generated draft available online.

Reflection Prompt: *What role does AI play in the concept of authorship* *and originality in academic work? How can you ensure that the content you* *generate with AI tools is ethically used and properly credited? What steps could* *you take to avoid plagiarism and produce truly original work?*

# Scenario 3: AI-Assisted Writing and Accessibility

A classmate with a visual impairment uses voice-to-text software to transcribe their lecture notes. While reviewing their transcript, you notice that the tool has made some errors, such as transcribing specialized terms incorrectly. Because of these errors, the notes may not be entirely accurate, which could affect the classmate’s ability to study effectively for exams.

Reflection Prompt: *How might AI tools improve accessibility for students with* *disabilities, and what are the limitations of relying on these tools? In what ways* *can you assist your classmate in improving the accuracy of their notes, and what* *ethical responsibility do you have as a peer when working with AI-generated* *content that may impact another student’s learning?*

# Conclusion

This chapter is meant to serve merely as a starting point for your discussions on AI’s evolving role in writing and research. As AI continues to develop, both you and your instructor must critically engage with its strengths and limitations, developing and honing your own critical AI literacy every time you interact with these tools. Rather than viewing AI as a shortcut or a substitute for human thought, we need to approach it as a collaborative tool, one that requires thoughtful guidance, careful revision, and ethical responsibility.

By sharing adaptations of these strategies and reflecting on real-world challenges, we can foster a collaborative learning community where AI is used responsibly and creatively. This means not only learning how to use AI effectively but also questioning its biases, limitations, and ethical implications in academic and professional contexts. As writers, researchers, students, and educators, we have to remain active participants in shaping how AI fits into our evolving understanding of voice, originality, and critical thinking.

Remember: AI literacy is not just about mastering a tool; it’s about developing the judgment and agency to determine when, how, and why to use it. By embracing this mindset, we empower ourselves and others to navigate the complexities of AI-assisted writing with confidence, integrity, and intellectual curiosity.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

Critical thinking skills are the cornerstone of informational literacy, and in today’s digital age, it is increasingly challenging to teach, particularly with the rise of misinformation.[^tr-1] AI further complicates that, as it is often used to create and perpetuate fake news. The MLA-CCCC Joint Task Force on Writing and AI notes that “Educators are now operating in an environment where the very term artificial intelligence can create unrealistic expectations by implying the existence of an entity capable of independent logic and reasoning. However, it can quickly become apparent to users of [generative AI] that its processes and output require the supervision and intervention of critically thinking humans” (5). Picasso et al. also highlight that it’s up to instructors to design authentic, real-world assessments that integrate critical data and AI literacy to prepare students for a data-driven society.

So, how can we approach this challenge? This chapter serves as a starting point for helping students navigate AI’s role in their academic work. It aims to alleviate fear of AI as a tool, while also addressing the misconception that AI is a “magic genie” offering easy shortcuts. Writing instructors can use this chapter as an introduction to AI tools, providing a balanced perspective that encourages students to use AI responsibly.

Additionally, when introducing AI tools into the classroom, I encourage you to look beyond the most popular ones like ChatGPT. Do your own search into research programs, transcription programs, AI podcast creators, screen readers, AI navigation software, etc. While programs change quickly, as of this writing (October 2025), some helpful programs include Perplexity.ai for research searches, Otter.ai for meeting transcripts and syn-

opses (especially helpful for accessibility and online/hybrid courses, and Claude (Anthropic) which I’ve found creates more nuanced prose and asks good questions to create better outputs. If you are hoping to incorporate more multimodal projects in your class, I recommend Canva (especially since you can unlock the premium features through an educator account), and Canva Magic Studio has great AI text-to-image creation, presentation building, and copywriting help. By incorporating a range of AI tools into the discussion, instructors can demonstrate how these technologies apply to students’ specific fields of study, fostering a broader and more inclusive understanding of AI’s role in academic and professional work.

# Activities and Assignments

## Group Assessments

Ask students to use an AI tool (e.g., ChatGPT or an academic database chatbot) to generate a list of sources on a specific research topic. Students will select two or three sources from the list and evaluate their credibility, relevance, and accuracy using a checklist (e.g., author credentials, publication date, source reliability). In small groups, students can discuss whether these sources are appropriate for academic use, identifying any gaps, biases, or limitations in the results. This will also help students practice how to engage with AI-generated suggestions critically.

## Using AI for Research and Topic Development

Students can engage with AI tools to help brainstorm research questions or narrow down a research topic. Encourage students to evaluate how AI can be useful in refining their ideas and how it might shape the direction of their research.

## AI as Peer Reviewer

Consider introducing the concept of using AI as a peer reviewer. Ask students to submit their drafts to an AI tool for feedback and then reflect on the comments provided. Discuss how AI feedback compares to that from human peers and consider its potential value and limitations in the writing process.

WAC Clearinghouse hosts more activity and assignment ideas to draw from called TextGenEd at https://wac.colostate.edu/repository/collections/textgened/. They have a new collection each year and include a section on AI literacy.

**Ethical Consideration:** *Before using AI, students should be aware that inputting full drafts into AI tools may raise concerns about data privacy and ownership. Instructors should discuss AI privacy policies and encourage students to input only sections of their work rather than entire drafts. This helps prevent potential ethical issues regarding data storage and the unauthorized use of student work by AI systems.*
Incorporating AI literacy into existing assignments not only helps students engage critically with technology but also prepares them to understand the evolving relationship between human writers and artificial intelligence in academic and professional contexts. If you want to expand on some of your existing assignment frameworks, the following are some ideas on how to incorporate AI literacy into common first year writing assignment genres:

## Rhetorical Analysis of AI

After analyzing a text or video (e.g., a public advertisement or an editorial), students can compare the rhetoric of human-created content with AI-generated content (e.g., generated by ChatGPT). Additionally, you can expand this by having students use AI to create a similar piece of content (e.g., an AI-generated ad or persuasive article) and analyze how the AI makes rhetorical choices, such as tone, audience, and persuasive techniques. Students can reflect on how these choices differ from human-created content and what that means for understanding AI’s role in persuasive communication.

## Literature Review Help

While conducting a literature review, encourage students to use AI tools like research databases with AI capabilities or even academic chatbots like Perplexity.ai to generate initial sources for their topics. Be sure that they have a firm understanding of critical AI literacy before doing this. To expand on this idea with a more critical lens, after the AI program generates a list of sources, students can evaluate the relevance, credibility, and scholarly value of the sources. They can also reflect on how AI tools might influence their research process, and compare the AI-generated list with a list they create manually. This can encourage students to think critically about AI’s role in the selection of academic sources.

## Argumentative or Persuasive Essay

Students can use AI to help generate arguments or counterarguments on a particular issue, and then reflect on how they incorporated or modified those AI-generated ideas. To further expand, ask students to evaluate how AI tools shape the persuasiveness of their arguments, the ethical considerations of using AI to support claims, and whether AI influences their personal stance on a topic. This helps students develop awareness of how AI can both aid and challenge their reasoning.

# Discussion Questions

1. What responsibilities do writers have when using AI tools like ChatGPT for generating ideas, drafting, or editing? How can students ensure their work remains authentic and theirs?

2. How might AI tools challenge traditional notions of plagiarism? What steps can you take to integrate AI-generated content ethically and transparently into your academic work?

3. In what ways might AI tools mislead or hinder your writing or research process? How can you critically evaluate the information or suggestions these tools provide?

4. How can using AI tools complement rather than replace your development as a writer and researcher? What balance should you strike between relying on AI and honing your own skills?

5. As AI tools become more advanced, what do you think their role will be in academic and professional writing? How can we prepare for and shape this future responsibly?
