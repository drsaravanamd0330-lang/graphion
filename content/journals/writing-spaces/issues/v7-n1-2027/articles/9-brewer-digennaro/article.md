abstract: The word *jargon* tends to be used negatively, as something to avoid.[^1] Teachers advise students against using jargon, with the reason that people only use jargon to appear smarter or to make others feel left out for not understanding them. But as academics who often use jargon in our own writing, we argue in favor of jargon in certain contexts. In this chapter, we first present common critiques of jargon, and then offer some definitions and reasons for using jargon. We suggest that, when writers learn the jargon (or specialized language) of their disciplines and other communities, they go from being novices to experts and develop a sense of belonging through their use and understanding of jargon. We conclude by encouraging students to become aware of the contexts where you find jargon and, rather than dismiss it as useless, consider why and when speakers and writers use it.

One of the authors of this chapter, Meaghan, was in the office of a friend who worked for a graphic design firm when she overheard the following exchange:

Amy: Hey, can you send me the code for that lockup?

Brynn: Yeah, I still need to knock out some shapes from the logo though.

> Amy: Oh, so it’s FPO?

> Brynn: Yep. Oh, and maybe after lunch we can talk about that double truck.

> Amy: Sounds good.

If you’re familiar with graphic design lingo, you would know that a lockup refers to a combination of images and text that form a brand’s logo. FPO is an acronym meaning “for position only,” referring to the fact that sometimes a designer needs to show a client an idea for an image before they’ve bought and finalized artwork in the design. And a double truck is an advertisement, usually in a newspaper or magazine, that stretches across two pages that face each other. However, since Meaghan wasn’t the audience for this exchange, and isn’t part of the discourse community of graphic designers, she had very little idea of what Brynn and Amy were talking about, with even the reference to “knock[ing] out . . . shapes” going over her head.

When we overhear vocabulary specific to an industry or profession we’re not a part of, we might refer to it as jargon. In some cases, as outsiders we might understand that this way of talking (or writing) isn’t intended to include us and go about our day. But in other situations where we think we’re part of the intended audience, we might find jargon frustrating or annoying. For example, if you took out federal student loans to help finance your education, you might have had to sign a “promissory note” with additional legal and financial jargon like “deferment” (which refers to temporarily postponing payments) or “aggregate loan limits” (which refer to the maximum amount of money you can borrow). Or you could one day serve on a jury and wonder what phrases such as “voir dire” (which refers to when a judge does a preliminary screening of potential jurors) or “nolo contendre” (where a defendant accepts a penalty without admitting guilt) mean. Meaghan was recently teaching a college class where she asked students about their associations with the term “jargon.” The discussion started fairly neutral with students recognizing it as vocabulary shared in professional communities. The conversation turned, however, when a student who worked at a cafe mentioned the term “eighty six,” which in restaurant lingo means that they’re out of something on the menu. Another student responded that servers only used such terms to be “snobby” and “pretentious” prompting a heated debate over whether the term was necessary or merely a way of signaling insider status.

Some of this frustration has led to negative connotations for the term jargon. As students, you might have even gotten the advice to avoid jargon in your writing altogether. In their popular writing style manual, *The Elements of Style*, William Strunk and E.B. White caution writers to avoid “fancy words” (76), “foreign languages” (81), and acronyms (80), giving the ironically vague advice to “be clear” (79). More recently, in a style guide for academics, English professor Helen Sword accuses academic writing of being “impersonal, stodgy, jargon-laden, abstract prose” (3), and calls such writing an example of “jargonitis,” as if using too much jargon were a dreaded disease.

As writers who use jargon in our own writing, we think there is room, and even a need, for jargon. Instead of rejecting all uses of specialized vocabulary as just bad cases of jargonitis, we think it’s important for you as students to understand what jargon is so that you can decide when, where, and how to use it. To put it simply, we think defining and understanding jargon will help you as students:

1. distinguish what we will argue is true jargon (or specialized vocabulary) from other kinds of vocabulary, like slogans or buzzwords, that might have lost meaning

2. think about jargon as a choice a writer can make to appeal to and share important information with their audience

3. begin using jargon yourself as you advance in your major or other expert communities

4. and finally, give yourself a break or ask for help when reading you do for school (or elsewhere) is difficult because of the author’s use of jargon Significantly, what one person calls jargon could be what someone else

sees as everyday language used among peers. For example, here’s a typical conversation between two teachers of composition (also the authors of this chapter) talking about our class activities:

> Kristen: I really like how you scaffolded the activities in your class!

> Meaghan: Thanks! I think it’s helpful for students to get comfortable with those lexical bundles in the reading before they use them in their own writing. Kristen: Yes! And the same for conventions in writing. The idea of including a blueprint in the introduction is probably something they’ve seen before but may not have included in their own writing.

> Meaghan: Especially if they’re more accustomed to writing reflective papers in the expressivist tradition.

> Kristen: Exactly! Your approach really helps prepare them for the other genres they’ll encounter across the disciplines.

Readers who are also composition instructors will find nothing unusual about the underlined words in the dialogue above, yet a college professor from another discipline who overheard this conversation commented about our use of jargon. This example illustrates the outsider vs. insider perspective in how we define *jargon*. While we may be quick to use the label *jargon* to refer to the specialized vocabulary that others use but that we don’t, we fail to identify the jargon-y nature of our own insider language. In short, we could say that jargon is in the eye, or the ear, of the beholder. In the following sections, we’ll examine criticisms of jargon to analyze how they’re defining it and what their specific objections are. We’ll then define jargon from the perspective of linguistics, which is the field that studies language, to decide whether we need jargon at all, and, if so, when.

# Criticisms of Jargon

Judging jargon as bad writing has become so common that writers who do so often don’t bother to define what they mean by jargon. For example, in his book *The Banned List: A Manifesto Against Jargon and Cliché*, John Rentoul compares the use of jargon to spelling or grammatical errors, things he associates with bad writing. In Rentoul’s view, writers use jargon to appear clever, hide their uncertainty, and announce their membership in certain groups. Notably, Rentoul never defines jargon, which is unfortunate since we can’t decide to use or avoid jargon if we’re not clear on what it is. Instead, Rentoul associates jargon with elitist or aspirational attitudes, arguably characteristics of the people he thinks use jargon. While Rentoul’s connotations associated with jargon are worth considering, they’re incomplete without a denotation, or simple definition, of what jargon is.

Similarly, in an online guide written to help academic writers make their writing more readable for general authors, many of the authors admonish their colleagues to avoid jargon in their writing. For example, Steven Pinker, a prolific writer and researcher in cognitive psychology, harshly criticizes his colleagues for using what he calls *academese*, which, he claims, includes jargon. In his attempt to explain what’s wrong with jargon, Pinker provides examples of common assumptions about its use.

Pinker first appears to defend jargon noting, “Every human pastime --- music, cooking, sports, art --- develops an argot to spare its enthusiasts from having to use a long-winded description every time they refer to a familiar concept in one another’s company” (5). But then he rejects this explanation (which is unfortunate since we think it is an accurate way of viewing the importance of jargon) based on his experience struggling to understand sentences by writers within his own discipline. Pinker also repeats the common complaint that academic writers, especially in the humanities and social sciences, use jargon to “hide the fact that they have nothing to say. They dress up the trivial and obvious with the trappings of scientific sophistication, hoping to bamboozle their audiences with highfalutin gobbledygook” (5). But Pinker rejects this explanation too, believing that most academic writers do not intend to confuse their readers or conceal their (lack of) knowledge. Nor does Pinker accept the insider-outsider explanation such as that proposed by Meaghan’s student for restaurant workers using the phrase “eighty-six” mentioned earlier.

So if jargon isn’t about avoiding long explanations where short ones will do, nor about covering for a writer’s lack of knowledge, nor even about insiders writing for members of their own specific group, then what leads writers to use jargon? Well, according to Pinker, writers use jargon because they suffer from “the curse of knowledge,” or “a cognitive blind spot” making it difficult for them to “imagin[e] what it is like for someone else not to know something that you know” (8). A more familiar term for the curse of knowledge, notes Pinker, is simply egocentrism. In other words, jargon is what happens when writers are unaware of, or ignore, the fact that their audience is made up of readers, separate from themselves, with different backgrounds and degrees of knowledge. Writers who use a lot of jargon, then, are (according to Pinker) so much inside their own heads that they fail to make better vocabulary choices.

While we disagree with most of Pinker’s criticisms of jargon, we find his accusation of self-centeredness intriguing, especially since he also observes that many of the most famous cases of bad jargon use are by English professors[^a1] (8). Is this a case of “Do as I say, not as I do,” with teachers of English telling students to avoid jargon as they indulge in using it themselves? We leave it to you, as students, to consider this possibility further. For our current discussion, it’s revealing that, for all his theorizing on *why* bad writers use jargon, Pinker, like Rentoul, never offers a useful, objective definition of jargon.

# Defining---and Defending---Jargon

Instead of simply telling you to avoid jargon in all cases, or maybe to use it as you please, we believe it’s important to understand what jargon is so you can make informed decisions about when and where to use it. In linguistics (the field of study that examines language), jargon is often used synonymously with “technical terms,” or “discipline-specific vocabulary” (Hock and Joseph; Thonney). According to linguists Hans Henrich Hock and Brian D. Joseph, “The major purpose of jargon is to serve in-group communication and social cohesion” (279). Similarly, writing studies scholar Teresa Thonney argues that jargon is an important feature of academic writing and that using it helps members of different academic communities be more concise and precise (356). Linguists Ken Hyland and Polly Tse also argue for the importance of students’ acquiring the specific vocabulary of their majors or other important fields as a way of becoming literate in that area. In other words, using a field’s jargon can help newcomers (like you!), feel a sense of belonging in your discipline and communicate your subject-matter knowledge and insider status to others (Woodward-Kron 246). Note that the focus on in-group communication and community membership in these definitions does not indicate pretentiousness or attitudes of *exclusion*, as some criticisms we described earlier of jargon suggest. Rather, they focus on *inclusion*, as students and novices gain expertise and increasingly identify with their academic communities. We think the difference is important, as it shows how learning the jargon of their major can help students develop a sense of belonging. In fact, educational linguist Robyn Woodward-Kron contends that knowing a field’s language is closely connected with knowing its concepts and other content, meaning it isn’t superficial signaling for insiders or outsiders.

Some linguists also use the phrase “a jargon,” similar to how we speak of “a language,” to refer to an entire academic or professional way of speaking or writing, like a lawyer’s or legal jargon (Hock and Joseph 291). In other words, “a jargon” in this sense refers to the entirety of how these specialized communities communicate and not just specific words. Additionally, some discussions of jargon mistake it for what linguists call *style*, which indicates the level of formality or informality of a discourse. While academic writing might be associated with a more formal style, the two don’t always align, as jargon can be used in informal discussions, like the one between composition instructors we cited earlier.

While jargon can be exclusive, the goal of jargon is not secrecy. Linguists thus differentiate jargons from “argots” (a term used in an earlier quote by Pinker), which consist of language used by groups such as criminal organizations whose express purpose is secrecy (Hock and Joseph 279). Instead, the purpose of jargon is to communicate as unambiguously and succinctly as possible. For instance, while outsiders to educational fields might think that the word “pedagogy” is just a highfalutin way of saying “teaching,” we would argue that pedagogy means something more specific because it refers not simply to the act of teaching but rather to the philosophy and theory that informs someone’s way of teaching. After looking at this table, you might be thinking, well, why not just use the lay or commonplace term? The reason for using jargon is that, as with our pedagogy example, if you talk to experts in these fields, these terms are doing something beyond what just the lay term can do on its own. Namely, in server or restaurant discourse the word “top” refers not just to any table but rather to the specific tables where customers are seated, and top is usually combined with the number of people who can be seated at the table, conveying extra information. (So, a four-top is a table that seats four.)

Jargon also includes terminology that might mean something different to the general public. For instance, outsiders to psychology might define the word “affect” in the table above as a verb meaning something like “to influence” and pronounce it with emphasis on the second syllable (aff-ECT). But to psychologists, affect (pronounced AFF-ect) is a noun meaning not just emotion but how someone is presenting that emotion in their body language, tone of voice, and facial expression. This pronunciation difference is similar to how in sports like basketball or football, players and professionals will pronounce defense as DEE-fense with the emphasis on the first syllable, in contrast to how legal or other settings in American English place emphasis on the second syllable, as in duh-FENSE. In both examples, different pronunciations signal different, specialized meanings.

As we noted earlier, jargon can also serve as a marker of solidarity, meaning it signals belonging within a community, similarly to how teens and young adults use slang to signal membership in their friend or generational groups. As an aside, while we’ll never be fully in on the slang used by the young adult college students we teach (since we’re not part of that generational group!), our students have taught us that “bussin” refers to something (usually food) that is extremely good or that saying “no cap” means they’re not lying. Using jargon can also signal that the discussion is professional or technical rather than casual. For instance, whereas a family member might say that they have high cholesterol, a physician will likely use the term dyslipidemia, which not only signals the professional context but also means something more specific, referring not just to high low-density cholesterol, but also low high-density cholesterol (the good kind), and high triglycerides.

Related to its solidarity-building function, jargon can even be used to elicit humor. For example, surfers use technical vocabulary to refer to different kinds of waves and moves in the water, but Meaghan was most intrigued when she overheard surfers talking about “groms” or “grommets,” terms that refer to younger, less experienced surfers, who sometimes don’t know surfing etiquette. So an experienced surfer, waiting on her board for a wave, might say to another experienced surfer something like, “Hey, check out the grom!” to reference a third surfer who appears to be a newbie. This is a way for the two experts to both share a joke and also information in case the less experienced surfer “drops in” (more jargon!) on their wave or gets himself into trouble by taking on a wave he doesn’t have the experience to handle. In this example, the use of just one jargon term does a lot of heavy lifting in terms of creating both community and meaning between surfers.

Critics of jargon blame academics for using language that is difficult for the public to understand. But, as history scholar Johann N. Neem argues, “jargon and complexity have their place” (19). When physicists are writing for other physicists, who are we, non-physicists, to say that their writing is bloated or unclear? That would be like an eavesdropper telling the unsuspecting person they’re listening to, to speak up.

# Beyond Jargon: Slogans and Buzzwords?

So far, we’ve been arguing that jargon serves a specific, and we would say, crucial purpose within specialist communities. However, it can be hard for outsiders to tell when jargon is necessary (that is, it carries special meaning within that community) versus something more performative or hollow. In fact, when we searched for the term jargon online, much of what we encountered should more accurately be described as buzz words or slogans, which are terms that might have started as jargon but then were used so frequently that they began to lose their original meaning. For example, we interpreted corporate terms like “deliverables” and “synergy” or even phrases like “Ping me,” or “I don’t have the bandwidth” not as jargon but rather as words and phrases that have been used so often that they no longer refer to anything specific.

Parodies involving what people think of as jargon show how its uses (or abuses) can go too far. In one famous case, a professor of physics, Alan Sokal, managed to get an article full of nonsense concepts published in an academic journal, having fooled the journal’s editors and peer reviewers into thinking the paper included real data and arguments. Some see this case as evidence that academics are so charmed by jargon and complex sentences that they can’t recognize nonsense when they see it. Or more likely, that they avoid calling it out for fear that their intelligence will be questioned. While the Sokal case certainly uncovers some negative truths about academics, using it as evidence against jargon equates jargon with gobbledygook. Yet, as we saw in the previous section, for linguists and writing specialists, such gobbledygook has a real purpose. Our advice, then, is to approach terms like this in a descriptive, critical way, rather than jumping to judgment.

# Conclusion: Approaching Jargon Critically as Readers and Writers

Developing as a writer includes becoming aware of academic language, including jargon, within the disciplines (or subject areas) you’re studying. In fact, one purpose of college writing courses is to familiarize students with concepts like genre and discourse community, so you can analyze and become aware of how language is being used in the different communities you belong to. Such language use and awareness can also help you develop a sense of belonging in those communities, something that we often do naturally and subconsciously.

As writers, we try to adopt a style and words that match the audience and situation we’re writing for. As we join new communities, we can extend our vocabularies by observing and recording the jargon and other language practices we hear or read. To this end, we sometimes ask our students to keep a word diary, where each week they write down one or two terms from their assigned readings or class discussions that they either didn’t know before or that were being used in a new way. Students write the word, the sentence or spoken context where it’s being used, and possible definitions in their diary. As we approach paper deadlines, we ask students to use at least one term from their diary in their paper.

Our word diary assignment is one way of acknowledging and helping to counter students’ feelings of being overwhelmed when encountering unfamiliar academic language. We became even more determined to help our students with academic jargon after we conducted a study on college students’ reading practices, and some students we interviewed reported feeling so frustrated with jargon in their assigned reading that they either didn’t complete it or struggled through with the feeling that they just weren’t getting it. One student, Edward, described reading an anthropology textbook where he was so preoccupied with remembering terms like Australopithecus that he kept “reading and rereading the same paragraph over and over again” without feeling he was comprehending it (interview, 30 Sept.). However, when *we* read the passages from Edward’s textbook, instead of worrying about the unfamiliar vocabulary, we focused on the overall narrative about how archaeologists and anthropologists pinpointed when the ancestors of modern humans began to walk upright.

This is all to say that you might have to change your reading strategy when you encounter texts that contain a lot of jargon: sometimes it might make sense to define it while other times you might have to be content with partially understanding. As Edward discussed his reading strategy, he admitted that he “should just sort of keep pushing . . . [because] eventually the author will explain [a term he was puzzling over] a bit better” (interview, 30 Sept.). In other words, it might make sense to skim or even skip difficult sections of a reading and come to class ready to ask questions about terms you were unfamiliar with.

As academics, we write to many different audiences, including other experts in our field, the public, and students. Our most common audience outside of the classroom is other experts, so that, even while writing this chapter, we had to remind ourselves to use jargon and lengthy sentences sparingly. When experts interact with other experts, we arguably need jargon as a shortcut, for clarity, and to be more precise---that is, jargon is a useful tool that helps to build and define our communities.

But when experts are writing for non-experts, jargon becomes a barrier to understanding. And often, these barriers can keep students, including ones from historically marginalized populations, from entering disciplines and professions where they (and that community) would benefit and thrive based on their contributions. Recently two of our colleagues wrote about the importance of the “Plain Language Movement,” which advocates making language more accessible especially in medical and legal fields, where not understanding jargon can have dire consequences (see Shapiro and Aull). In fact, they argue that plain language is key to making academic spaces more inclusive.

That’s why we end by stating that the onus isn’t just on you, the student, to figure the jargon out. We need textbooks (like the one you’re reading now), encyclopedias, and other resources to translate jargon for students, so that novices have the opportunity to learn from experts before they’re experts themselves. While jargon serves a critical purpose, and it’s not always possible or even preferable to avoid it, we think academics would benefit from being more critical about *when* and *how* we use it, especially in public and student-facing communication.

# Teacher Resources

# Overview and Teaching Strategies

As writing and language instructors who have often addressed jargon in our classes, we’ve found that college students are naturally curious about this topic and have a lot to say about the jargon they’ve used and encountered in academic contexts and beyond.[^tr-1] Lessons on jargon work well with units on genre, discourse communities, and academic discourse. In fact, this chapter could be paired with the articles by Steven Pinker and Teresa Thonney that we cite, as students can compare their perspectives on jargon and academic writing.

The following discussion questions can also serve as activity prompts wherein students identify jargon examples in short passages from academic texts, or even in their own writing.

# Discussion Questions and Activities

1. Think back to a time when someone was using a term that you didn’t understand. What was the word and who was using it? What do you think the purpose of that term was?

2. Reread the criticisms of jargon by writers such as John Rentoul and Steven Pinker. Describe a situation when you used jargon to appear smarter or to exclude certain readers of listeners. Alternatively, describe a situation when you felt that someone else was using jargon for these reasons.

3. What was your understanding of and attitude toward jargon before reading this chapter? What have your teachers and other writing

> experts told you about using jargon? Have you changed your position after reading this chapter?

4. Look at Table 1 again. Add two or three words to the list, and define them in both the technical sense and the lay (or commonplace) sense. How do your additional words communicate something that the lay terms don’t? Write a short dialogue or paragraph using your words to show how they are used in context.

5. Find a paragraph from an academic reading that you feel is full of jargon. Share the paragraph with another student without identifying the words you think are jargon. See if your classmate identifies the same words as potential jargon. Together, revise the paragraph to avoid any jargon. Describe the differences between the original version and your revised version, besides the vocabulary words.

6. In small groups, do an image search for jargon and see what kinds of memes or other images come up. Do you think the jargon in the images is jargon or is it more like a slogan or buzzword?

7. John Rentoul’s book, *The Banned List,* was inspired by the novelist George Orwell’s 6 Rules for Writing. Search the internet for a list of these rules. Where did you find these rules posted---who posted them and for what purpose? Read and comment on these rules from your own perspective. Which rules do you agree with and why?
